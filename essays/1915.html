Intro: The Trenches of Mexico

On the morning of December 4th, 1914, two of the most famous men in Mexico met for the first time, in a suburb called Xochimilco, to discuss what to do with the country they had just conquered between them.

Pancho Villa arrived heavyset, gregarious, and largely illiterate — a former cattle rustler from Durango who had, in a few short years, built the División del Norte into the most feared fighting force in Mexico. Emiliano Zapata arrived slight, somber, and precise — a horse trainer and smallholder from Morelos whose Ejército Libertador del Sur fought for one thing above all: the return of village land seized by sugar estates under the old regime. The two men had almost nothing in common except an enemy, and even that was a recent development. Two months earlier, at a convention in Aguascalientes meant to unify the revolution's factions, Villa's delegates had astonished everyone by voting to endorse Zapata's land-reform program in full — a gesture of solidarity that the Constitutionalist delegates, loyal to Venustiano Carranza, refused to accept. The convention split. Carranza, a landowning lawyer from Coahuila who had inherited leadership of the anti-Huerta coalition and had no intention of redistributing anyone's land, retreated to Veracruz rather than recognize its outcome. Villa and Zapata, calling themselves Conventionists, marched on the capital he'd abandoned.

On December 6th they entered it together — more than fifty thousand men between them, parading down the Paseo de la Reforma to the Zócalo while the city watched. Zapata's Indian fighters, reputed in the capital's press to be barbarous, startled everyone by begging politely for food at the doors of the rich; Villa's men fired their rifles into the air at all hours and held drunken orgies in the streets. At the National Palace, Villa insisted the two of them take turns sitting in the presidential chair for photographs. Zapata refused to enjoy it. "I didn't fight for that," he is said to have told Villa. "We should burn that chair to end all ambitions." Neither man wanted the office for himself, which was, in its way, the whole problem — the revolution's two strongest armies now controlled its capital, and neither had the slightest interest in governing it.

They didn't stay to find out what that would mean. Within weeks both had left the city to continue their separate campaigns, Zapata south toward Puebla, Villa north to consolidate Chihuahua, and the vacuum they left behind was exactly the opening Carranza's best general had been waiting for. Álvaro Obregón, a Sonoran chickpea farmer turned soldier with no formal military training and an instinct for terrain that made up for it, marched back into Mexico City on January 28th, 1915 — barely seven weeks after Villa had sat in its presidential chair. It was retaken almost without a fight. The Conventionists, it turned out, could occupy a capital far more easily than they could hold one, and by the time Villa understood what the loss actually meant, Obregón was already dug into a stretch of flat agricultural land outside a colonial town in Guanajuato called Celaya, waiting for him.

He had arrived in early March, five weeks ahead of Villa, with engineers before soldiers — men who understood what four years of watching Europe had taught anyone paying attention: that a line held with trenches, barbed wire, and machine guns could absorb almost anything thrown at it in the open. By April he had roughly six thousand cavalry, five thousand infantry, eighty-six machine guns, and thirteen field pieces dug into the ground, with a further six thousand cavalry concealed in a nearby wood, waiting for a signal. Villa did not send scouts to check.

What followed was the same tactic that had never failed him — the massed cavalry charge that had broken Huerta's federal army and every force put in front of it for four years running — thrown twice at a position built specifically to survive it. The first assault came at dawn on April 6th and broke against the wire; it reformed and came again on the 13th, larger and no more successful. Obregón, dressed for the occasion not in his usual khaki but in the silver-studded trousers and goatskin jacket of a charro — Villa had once mocked him as "el perfumado," the perfumed one, and Obregón had decided to smell like a goat in reply — rode among his Sonoran and Yaqui troops as the lines held. Estimates of the Villista dead vary widely, since Villa did what defeated commanders have always done with casualty figures, but even the more conservative counts run into the thousands, against Constitutionalist losses that stayed in the low hundreds. Historians of the period have called Celaya Villa's Waterloo — not because he was captured or killed, but because the aura was gone. An army that had never lost had now lost twice in ten days, and armies built on invincibility rarely survive its disproof.

Obregón didn't let him recover. Through May he pushed north, and in early June, near León, Villa attempted one final surprise — a night assault on Obregón's base at Trinidad. It failed, but not before a shell fragment took Obregón's right arm off at the elbow; he was carried from the field expected to die, and a German military adviser attached to the Constitutionalist staff took command of the position until he could be moved. He lived. The arm did not — it was preserved, first in a jar and later beneath a monument, and would remain on public display in Mexico City for most of the twentieth century, a literal fragment of the revolution that outlasted both the men who might have argued over what it meant.

By autumn the División del Norte existed mostly as a name. Obregón broke it again at León in May and at Aguascalientes in June, and by July the once-undefeated Villistas had been reduced to guerrilla bands retreating toward the Sierra Madre. Zapata held on longer in the south, where the war had always been smaller and more local and about land rather than the capital — but the larger question, the one that had seemed answered in December when two peasant armies sat, briefly, in a president's chair, was answered differently in a wheat field five months later. Mexico City could be taken. Keeping it, or even the power to threaten retaking it, required something neither the cattle rustler nor the smallholder from Morelos had ever built: an army that could survive contact with a man who had done his reading.

Part I: Birth of a Nation

On February 18th, 1915, projectionists in evening dress set up a screen in the East Room of the White House and showed the President of the United States a film in which the Ku Klux Klan rides to the rescue of a besieged white South.

Woodrow Wilson was an easy audience. Thomas Dixon, whose novel The Clansman the film was based on, had been Wilson's classmate at Johns Hopkins, and used the friendship deliberately — get the President to watch it, and no city censor board would dare touch it afterward. The film that D.W. Griffith had built from Dixon's book was, by any technical measure, extraordinary: twelve reels, an orchestral score, battle scenes shot from angles no one had used before, close-ups and fades that would still be standard technique a century later. It was also, in its second half, a work of grotesque historical inversion — Reconstruction restaged as a nightmare of freedmen "running amok" and a corrupt North, with hooded riders as the only force capable of putting the world back in order. Wilson is reported to have called it "like writing history with lightning," though no contemporary source records him saying it; the line surfaces only decades later, in a 1937 magazine piece, and historians have never been able to verify it. What isn't in dispute is that Wilson approved of the film, and that he had reason to.

He'd written some of its footnotes himself. Griffith spliced passages from Wilson's own scholarly history of the United States directly onto the screen as authoritative captions — the President's academic writing, published years before he held office, now doing duty as intertitles justifying the Klan's return. It wasn't a coincidence of sympathies so much as a closed loop.

The film opened in Los Angeles February 8th under Dixon's original title, moved to New York in March as The Birth of a Nation, and set off protest wherever it played. The NAACP, seven years old and still finding its footing, organized boycotts and demonstrations in city after city; in Boston, the activist William Monroe Trotter led a campaign so persistent that Wilson himself had him thrown out of the White House after a confrontation. A dozen mayors banned the film outright. None of it stopped it. It was the first genuine blockbuster in American film history, and by some later analyses, its arrival in a city correlated with a measurable spike in local lynchings within the following month.

Wilson's part in this went well beyond one evening's screening. He was the first Southern-born president since the Civil War, a man whose father had defended slavery from a Presbyterian pulpit and served as a Confederate chaplain, and Wilson brought that inheritance into the machinery of the federal government itself. Within his first year in office, cabinet secretaries — Postmaster General Albert Burleson foremost among them — had begun segregating the departments that employed the largest numbers of Black civil servants: the Post Office, the Treasury, the Bureau of Engraving and Printing. Black and white clerks who had shared offices, washrooms, and lunchrooms since Reconstruction were separated, often by screened-off partitions built into shared workspaces; Black supervisors were demoted or dismissed outright and replaced with white appointees, and applicants were newly required to submit photographs with their federal job applications, a quiet mechanism for filtering out Black candidates before an interview ever occurred. When Black leaders protested, Wilson's response was that segregation served their own interest, sparing them what he called the friction of working alongside people who resented them. It was the most significant reversal of Black federal employment since emancipation, dismantling in a few years what Reconstruction-era administrations had spent decades building.

That was the country The Birth of a Nation arrived in, and the one it left more combustible than it found. In August, in Atlanta, a Jewish factory superintendent named Leo Frank — his death sentence for murder already commuted amid serious doubts about his conviction — was dragged from a state prison farm by a mob calling itself the Knights of Mary Phagan, driven across the state, and hanged from an oak tree. The lynching had its own furies, chiefly antisemitic, largely separate from the film. But the two things did not stay separate for long.

On Thanksgiving eve, three months later, a failed preacher and fraternal-lodge organizer named William Joseph Simmons led fifteen men up Stone Mountain outside Atlanta. Several had been part of the mob that killed Frank; two were elderly survivors of the actual Reconstruction-era Klan, dead in all but memory for forty years. Simmons built an altar of stones, laid a Bible and an unsheathed sword across it, and set fire to a wooden cross visible from the city below. He declared himself Imperial Wizard of a new Invisible Empire. He would later describe the night as bitterly cold, the men shivering as they took their oaths; weather records for Stone Mountain that evening show a low of 45 degrees.

The cross itself was the tell. The Reconstruction Klan that Simmons claimed to be reviving had never burned one — it isn't in any of their own recorded rituals. The image belongs to Griffith, who took it from Dixon's novel, who had taken it in turn from Walter Scott's poetry and half-remembered accounts of Scottish clan signal fires, and grafted the whole invented tradition onto the Klan for dramatic effect. Simmons had seen it on a movie screen nine months earlier and carried it up a real mountain as though it were history. In a sense, by the time he lit it, it already was — a fiction so effective it built its own memory, and then built an organization to defend it.

Part II: Humiliation

Ambassador Hioki Eki asked for a private audience, which was itself the first warning. On January 18th, 1915, he sat across from Yuan Shikai, president of a five-year-old republic that had inherited an empire's debts and none of its authority, and handed him a sealed list of twenty-one demands, along with the suggestion that Japan expected an answer, and that the consequences of a wrong one would be severe. He asked that the contents stay confidential. Yuan had it leaked to the foreign press within weeks.

The demands arrived in five groups, each more corrosive than the last. The first simply asked China to bless what Japan had already taken — the German concessions in Shandong province, seized the previous autumn the moment Japan declared war, ostensibly against Germany, actually against the province's ports, mines, and railways. The second extended Japan's reach across southern Manchuria and eastern Inner Mongolia: a ninety-nine-year lease, rights of settlement, the appointment of Japanese financial and administrative officials inside Chinese institutions. The third handed Japan joint control of China's largest iron and steel combine. The fourth was almost an afterthought by comparison — China could cede no further coastal territory to anyone except Japan, a clause aimed less at China than at every other power with interests on the coast. And then there was the fifth.

Group Five didn't ask for territory. It asked for the country. Japanese advisers embedded in China's political, financial, and police administration; joint policing in key cities; a decisive Japanese voice in purchases of arms and in any future railway construction. Nothing in the first four groups had proposed anything the other imperial powers hadn't already normalized in China over the previous half-century — spheres of influence, extraterritoriality, railway concessions were simply how the great powers had been treating China since the century turned. Group Five went further than any of them ever had. It would have made China a Japanese protectorate in substance, whatever it was called on paper.

Yuan was not the pliant figure the demands assumed. He instructed his negotiators that Group Five was non-negotiable under any circumstance, and for three months, from February to April, they held twenty-five rounds of talks whose entire purpose was delay — delay long enough for someone, anyone, among the treaty powers to intervene. No one did. Britain, allied to Japan since 1902 and consumed by a war in Europe it hadn't expected to still be fighting, could not risk the friction. The United States restated its commitment to the Open Door Policy, a phrase that by 1915 had come to mean American disapproval registered in a form requiring no American action. Russia and France had their own understandings with Tokyo to protect and their own fronts to bleed on. Japan had read the moment correctly: with the entire Western half of the alliance system otherwise occupied trying not to die in Belgium and Champagne, there was no one left to actually enforce a Chinese sovereignty that existed, at this point, mostly as a diplomatic courtesy.

On April 26th, China formally rejected the revised terms. Japan's own elder statesmen, more cautious than the ambassador on the ground, pulled Group Five from the table entirely — it had proven, even by the standards of 1915 imperial appetite, too openly what it was. What remained was delivered on May 7th as an ultimatum: thirteen demands, a forty-eight-hour clock, and an implicit threat of war that Yuan, with no army capable of surviving one, could not call. He accepted on May 9th. The formal agreements were signed on the 25th.

China's press had a name for the 9th within days: National Humiliation Day, observed for years afterward in schools and newspapers as a date to remember rather than forget. There was a particular twist to Yuan's humiliation, one the popular telling — Yuan as simple collaborator — tends to flatten. He had, by most of the actual diplomatic record, fought Group Five hard, leaked what he could, stalled as long as stalling served him, and gotten the single worst provision struck from the final document. It didn't matter. The public did not experience the demands as a negotiation Yuan had partially won; it experienced them as a president who had let a foreign power dictate terms to a Chinese government at all, and the distinction between capitulation and damage control did not survive contact with a nationwide boycott of Japanese goods that emptied Japanese shelves in Chinese cities and sent Chinese students studying in Japan home in protest, unwilling to finish their educations in the country that had just written the terms of their nation's subjugation.

Yuan would not have long to enjoy even the compromise he'd salvaged. Within the year, advisers who had watched Europe's monarchies hold together far better than its republics convinced him the moment had come to restore what 1911 had abolished. A hand-picked national assembly duly petitioned him to accept the throne; Yuan performed the expected reluctance and, on December 12th, proclaimed himself Emperor of a restored Chinese Empire, taking the era name Hongxian — Constitutional Abundance. Japan, whose tacit cooperation had been quietly assumed as part of the price of the Twenty-One Demands, offered no such thing; within weeks it was threatening intervention against the very restoration it might once have blessed. It did not take that long for the rest of China to answer, either. On December 25th, not even a full two weeks into the new reign, the military governor of Yunnan declared his province in open revolt, the first province of many. Yuan's throne, and the National Protection War it provoked, would not survive the year that followed it — but the empire he'd just proclaimed was already cracking before 1915 itself ran out.

Part III: The Trenches

By the start of 1915, the war that had been supposed to be over by Christmas had instead become a fixed geography — two parallel scars running from the Swiss border to the North Sea, deepening every week into something less like a battlefield and more like a buried city. What had begun in the autumn of 1914 as hastily scraped ditches had, within months, developed into layered systems: front-line trenches, support trenches a short distance behind them, reserve trenches behind those, all connected by communication trenches running back toward the rear, the whole network patched with duckboards, revetted with sandbags, and populated, at any given time, by hundreds of thousands of men who ate, slept, defecated, and died within a few feet of where they stood. Historians of the war have called this landscape by many names. None of them fully capture what the men living in it called it themselves, in letters home that censors sometimes let through and sometimes didn't: a charnel house, dressed up as a strategy.

The day had a shape, and the shape rarely changed. At dawn and again at dusk came "stand-to" — every man up on the fire step, rifle ready, watching the mist over no man's land for the shapes of an attack that usually didn't come, because both sides had learned that dawn and dusk were exactly when the other side would be watching for it. Between those hours, life narrowed into a monotony broken only by the arbitrary arithmetic of artillery and snipers. Sentry duty. Filling sandbags. Repairing a section of parapet that had collapsed in the rain. Letters written and re-read. Cards played by men who had stopped expecting to need the money they were winning. One British soldier who served on the Western Front in 1915 remembered it plainly: trench life was extremely dull, apart from the sentry, and you simply slept and wrote letters. A French soldier in the same year described his own days almost identically — eating what bread and chocolate could be found, smoking, firing occasionally at the Germans, absorbing whatever bombs came over. The war, from inside a trench, was mostly waiting, with the waiting occasionally and without warning turning fatal.

Real work happened at night. Under cover of darkness, ration parties made the run back to supply points and returned with food, water, and mail; work parties strung wire, dug new saps, and shored up positions that the day's shelling had degraded; fresh units filed in past the men they were relieving, who filed out past them going the other way, a nightly changing of a guard that never really rested. It was also, for the same reason, when men died in numbers that never made it into the official communiqués — a stray shell, a sniper's shot at a silhouette against the sky, an entire work party caught by a machine gun that had been waiting patiently for exactly this. The British planned, coldly, for a ten percent monthly loss of total strength to death, wounds, and illness, in a war that had not yet had a major battle in the sector that month. That figure was simply the price of holding a line, independent of any offensive at all.

The trenches themselves fought back against the men occupying them almost as persistently as the enemy did. Standing water was constant — trenches flooded in rain and stayed flooded, since drainage in clay soil across Flanders and northern France ranged from poor to nonexistent, and men stood for days in water that never fully drained before the next rain arrived. The result, especially in that first winter, was trench foot: feet gone numb and swollen from days of cold immersion, turning gangrenous if untreated, sometimes requiring amputation. Over twenty thousand British soldiers needed treatment for it in the winter of 1914 to 1915 alone. Officers instituted whale-oil rubdowns and mandatory dry-sock inspections, and the numbers did eventually fall — not because the trenches got drier, but because the army got better at making men change their socks.

Lice were simply permanent. They bred in every seam of every uniform, impossible to eliminate and only briefly interrupted by the delousing stations behind the lines, which steamed clothing and scrubbed skin for a few days of relief before the infestation reasserted itself on the walk back to the front. Soldiers called the ritual of picking them out by hand "chatting," and did it constantly, sitting in pairs running fingernails along seams, though no one yet knew that the itching parasites in their clothes were the actual vector for the debilitating trench fever spreading through the ranks — that link wouldn't be confirmed by army doctors until later in the war. Rats were worse, and more openly hated: fed on the dead in no man's land and the refuse of the trenches alike, they grew, by soldiers' own accounts, to the size of cats, and were bold enough to bite sleeping men. Soldiers organized rat hunts as a form of both pest control and simple entertainment, one of the only entertainments available. Then there was the smell that sat over all of it — mud, rot, gas residue, unburied bodies, open latrines, unwashed wool, a single continuous stench that survivors described decades later with more immediacy than they described the shelling.

What the shelling did leave behind, less visibly, was a new category of casualty that medicine had no name for yet. In February 1915, a British Army psychologist named Charles Myers examined a soldier who had been near several exploding shells and reported symptoms with no visible wound behind them — reduced vision, lost sense of smell, patchy memory, a reaction the soldier himself described as like a punch on the head, without any pain after it. Myers published his findings in The Lancet under a term that stuck immediately and permanently: shell shock. He did not coin the phrase — soldiers in the ranks had already been using something like it before he ever wrote it down — but he gave the army's doctors, and eventually the public, a word for something they had been watching happen without being able to describe it. His own superiors mostly refused to believe him. The prevailing military view held that a man who could not walk, speak, or stop shaking despite no physical wound was a coward or a malingerer, not a casualty, and men diagnosed by their own symptoms as shell-shocked would spend years being court-martialed and in some cases executed for exactly the condition Myers was trying to get the army to recognize as real.

Then there was the gas. The first attempt came at the end of January, when German artillery fired eighteen thousand shells filled with a crude tear gas called xylyl bromide into Russian lines near a minor Polish town called Bolimów. A German staff officer climbed a church tower that morning specifically to watch his side's new weapon work. It didn't. The cold froze the chemical before it could vaporize properly, and what little gas did form drifted back toward the German lines instead of the Russian ones. The follow-up Russian counterattack cost forty thousand casualties for no ground gained on either side, and the failure was so total, so quietly absorbed into the general catalogue of the Eastern Front's slaughter, that Russian command never bothered reporting it to their allies in the west. Britain and France went into spring with no warning at all of what had just been tested against someone else.

They found out on the afternoon of April 22nd, outside Ypres. German engineers had spent days waiting on the wind, since chlorine had to be released from cylinders and carried by whatever breeze happened to be blowing, and on that Thursday the wind finally cooperated. Nearly two hundred tons of it drifted across a four-mile stretch of the line held by French territorial troops and Algerian colonial divisions as a low, greenish-yellow cloud that at first looked to the watching soldiers like nothing more than smoke concealing an infantry advance. It took only minutes to become clear it was not smoke. Men who inhaled it began coughing, retching, clawing at their throats as their lungs filled with fluid faster than they could expel it — a British officer who witnessed gas casualties later that year described the process as equivalent to drowning, only on dry land, ending in a tightening panic, a greenish froth coughed up from the lungs and stomach, and a body that turned from white to a mottled greenish-black before death. Thousands of French and Algerian troops died or fled within the first quarter hour, tearing open a gap more than six kilometers wide in the Allied line — a hole large enough, in theory, to end the entire stalemate in a single afternoon.

It didn't, because the German command hadn't actually planned to exploit it. Chlorine gas had been authorized as an experiment, not as the opening move of a breakthrough, and no reserves had been positioned to pour through the gap once it opened. German infantry advanced cautiously behind the cloud, wearing crude cotton pads soaked in chemical neutralizer, and took thousands of prisoners and dozens of guns — but stopped well short of Ypres itself, astonished at what their own weapon had done and unprepared to capitalize on it. Canadian troops on the flank, who identified the gas by its smell before they understood what it was, held the line in the following days using improvised protection that would have been laughable if it hadn't worked: rags and handkerchiefs soaked in water, or failing that, in their own urine, since the ammonia in it was enough to partially neutralize the chlorine. It was crude, and it killed fewer of them than the gas alone would have, and it bought enough time for actual gas masks to reach the front within weeks. By July, effective respirators were standard issue on both sides, and the weapon that had briefly threatened to unmake trench warfare in an afternoon instead became one more fixture of it — another item added to a soldier's kit alongside the rifle and the entrenching tool, another way the war had found to kill a man who thought he understood, by 1915, every way there already was.

Part V: Gallipoli

Winston Churchill had a good idea, on paper. The First Lord of the Admiralty proposed forcing the Dardanelles Strait with naval power alone, opening a supply route to Russia through the Black Sea, threatening Constantinople directly, and possibly knocking the Ottoman Empire out of the war entirely — all without committing the kind of infantry that was already being consumed by the hundred thousand on the Western Front. It was the sort of plan that looked elegant in a briefing room and depended on almost everything going right to survive contact with reality.

The naval assault came first, in February and March, and it went wrong in the most literal way possible: on March 18th, Allied battleships pushing through the strait struck an undetected minefield. Three were sunk or crippled outright, several more damaged, and the naval commander pulled back rather than risk what remained of his fleet. The straits stayed shut. What had been conceived as a way to avoid a land campaign now required one, and the month-long gap between the naval failure and the eventual invasion gave Ottoman forces, reinforced under a German commander named Otto Liman von Sanders, all the time they needed to fortify a peninsula the Allies had just told them, by attacking it once already, they intended to take.

The landings came on April 25th. British troops went ashore at Cape Helles on the peninsula's southern tip; further up the Aegean coast, Australian and New Zealand troops — soon abbreviated into the acronym that would outlive the battle itself, ANZAC — landed at a beach that wasn't the one they'd planned for. Currents or navigational error had pushed the boats north of their intended point, into a narrow cove backed not by the gentle slope the maps had promised but by a maze of ravines and steep scrub-covered ridges that made any advance inland close to impossible. The beach they scrambled onto would be known, from that morning forward, simply as Anzac Cove.

Waiting above them was a young Ottoman lieutenant colonel named Mustafa Kemal, who had studied the terrain from an earlier posting and correctly guessed where the Allies would try to land. He rushed his best regiment, the 57th, into position on the high ground above the cove, and reportedly gave them an order that has been quoted in nearly every account of the battle since: I do not order you to attack, I order you to die — in the time it takes us to die, other troops and commanders can come and take our place. The line comes down to us through a biography written years later rather than a document from the day itself, so its exact wording carries some of the same uncertainty as any battlefield quote passed through memory before it reached the page. What isn't in doubt is what the 57th Regiment actually did: it held the ridge, at a cost close to annihilation, and stopped an Anzac advance that had a real chance, in its first confused hours, of taking the high ground before the Ottoman defense could properly form.

It never got another chance. Within days, both the Helles and Anzac beachheads had calcified into exactly the kind of trench warfare the whole campaign had been conceived to avoid — just transplanted from Flanders mud to Turkish scrub, and worse in its own particular ways. The heat by summer was extreme, water was scarce and often contaminated, and dysentery spread through both trench lines so thoroughly that by some counts more men were evacuated for illness than for wounds. Flies, drawn to unburied corpses in a landscape too dangerous for either side to safely retrieve its dead, coated food before it reached a man's mouth. The distances involved were absurdly small — the entire Anzac perimeter measured barely a mile across at its widest — which meant that men lived, for months, within easy artillery range of an enemy they could sometimes see without binoculars, in a stalemate too cramped to maneuver and too well dug-in to break.

The one real attempt to change that came in August, with a fresh landing at Suvla Bay meant to link up with the Anzac position and finally seize the high ground that had eluded everyone since April. It might have worked — the initial landing met almost no resistance, and for some hours the ground above Suvla sat essentially undefended. Allied commanders hesitated instead of advancing, giving Ottoman reinforcements time to arrive and dig in on the heights before the opportunity could be used. It was the campaign's last real chance at a breakthrough, and it dissolved into the same trench deadlock as everywhere else within days.

The political cost fell hardest on the man who had championed the whole operation. Churchill, whose confidence and impatience had pushed the naval assault forward over the objections of more cautious officers, became the obvious target once it failed; when a government crisis forced the ruling Liberals into a coalition with the Conservatives that May, one of the Conservative conditions was Churchill's removal from the Admiralty. He was demoted to a minor cabinet post, resigned it within months, and by November had left government altogether to take a battalion command on the Western Front — the paper strategist who had helped design one disaster now going, by choice, to serve in the one he hadn't. It would be a quarter century before Gallipoli stopped following him, if it ever entirely did.

The evacuation, when it finally came at the end of the year, would prove to be the campaign's single unambiguous success: a night withdrawal from the Anzac and Suvla positions in December, executed with such care that the Ottoman defenders barely noticed it was happening, followed by the final pullback from Helles in early January with only light casualties — a retreat better planned than almost anything that had preceded it. What the campaign left behind, on both sides, was something closer to founding myth than military lesson. For Australia and New Zealand, the landing at Anzac Cove would become the reference point for a national identity distinct from Britain's, commemorated every April 25th as the day their countries' sense of themselves as more than colonial extensions first took visible shape, forged, as these things often are, not by the victory that was promised but by the eight months of dying that came instead. For Mustafa Kemal, the officer who guessed correctly where the enemy would land and held the ridge above them, Gallipoli was the opening chapter of a rise that would end, within less than a decade, with him remaking an empire's wreckage into a republic and taking the name history would remember him by: Atatürk, father of the Turks. Two new national identities, assembled from the same eight months of failure, on a peninsula neither side could actually take.

Part VI: The Birth of Genocide

On the night of April 24th, 1915 — a date Armenians would come to observe every year afterward as the beginning — Ottoman police moved through Constantinople and arrested somewhere between 235 and 270 Armenian community leaders: writers, editors, clergymen, physicians, lawyers, members of parliament. Most were sent east to holding centers in the Anatolian interior. The overwhelming majority of them were never seen again. It is remembered, in Armenian, as Karmir Kiraki — Red Sunday — and it is conventionally treated by historians as the opening act of what followed: not a wartime security measure that spiraled out of control, but the deliberate removal of the one class of people capable of organizing resistance or documenting what was coming, cleared out of the way before the rest of the machinery started.

The justification, when the Committee of Union and Progress bothered to offer one, rested on a defeat. In the winter just past, Ottoman War Minister Enver Pasha had led an invasion of Russian territory in the Caucasus that ended in catastrophe at Sarikamış — sixty thousand men lost, most of them to the cold rather than to Russian guns. Enver needed somewhere to put the blame that wasn't his own strategic judgment, and he settled on the empire's Armenian population, a Christian minority he accused of collaborating with the invading Russian army. The accusation found a receptive audience among CUP leaders already primed by loss: the empire had spent the previous three years hemorrhaging its European territory in the Balkan Wars, and a government that had lost almost everything else was looking for an internal enemy it could still do something about. When an Armenian uprising broke out in the city of Van that same April — Armenians there, facing a governor's demand that thousands of their men report for what looked unmistakably like a death sentence disguised as forced labor, seized the city rather than comply — it confirmed, for CUP leaders who had already decided what they believed, that the conspiracy they'd been describing was real.

What followed the April arrests was not improvised. On May 27th, the Ottoman government passed the Tehcir Law, formally authorizing the mass deportation of Armenians from the empire's eastern provinces under the language of wartime resettlement. In practice, it authorized what the interior minister, Talat Pasha, had already set in motion and would later describe to a German diplomat with no particular effort at concealment — that Turkey intended to use the cover of the war to permanently liquidate its internal Christian populations without foreign interference. Able-bodied Armenian men, many of them already stripped of their weapons and reassigned from active army units to unarmed labor battalions earlier that year, were marched out of their towns and killed, often before the wider deportations even began. What remained — women, children, the elderly — were driven from their homes toward the Syrian desert on foot, in convoys that received no food, water, or shelter from the government that had ordered their movement, escorted by paramilitary units that subjected them, as the record is now extensively documented from multiple independent sources, to systematic robbery, rape, and periodic massacre along the route. Those who survived the march arrived at destinations like Deir ez-Zor to find no camps prepared to receive them — only open desert, where disease and starvation finished what the roads hadn't.

The scale is contested only in its precise figure, not in its basic shape. Estimates for total Armenian deaths in 1915 and 1916 range from roughly eight hundred thousand to considerably more than a million, out of an Ottoman Armenian population of perhaps a million and a half to two million before the war began. Talat Pasha kept his own internal statistics on the deportations — a bureaucrat's habit that would later become part of the documentary record against him — and the discrepancy between the numbers he recorded as deported in 1915 and the numbers known to have survived by 1917 is itself one of the most damning pieces of evidence historians point to.

None of this happened invisibly. The American ambassador in Constantinople, Henry Morgenthau, cabled Washington repeatedly through the summer of 1915 describing what he was being told directly by Ottoman officials — men who, in his own account, made no particular attempt to conceal what they understood themselves to be doing. On May 24th, before the deportations had even fully unfolded, the Triple Entente issued a joint declaration condemning the Ottoman government for what it called crimes against humanity and civilization — among the first uses of that exact phrase in the way it would come to be used through the rest of the century — and warned that Ottoman officials would be held personally responsible. The New York Times ran well over a hundred articles on the killings before the year was out. The atrocity, in other words, was reported in close to real time, by an American ambassador, by the Allied powers, by the newspaper of record in the country that would eventually enter the war against the empire committing it. Recognition of what to call it, and accountability for who had ordered it, would prove to be an entirely separate and much longer fight — one that Turkey's government still refuses to fully concede, even as the historical consensus among non-Turkish scholars, and a growing number of Turkish ones, has become essentially uniform: this was a deliberate campaign of ethnic destruction, not a wartime tragedy that simply happened to Armenians.

Talat Pasha did not live to see that consensus form. He fled the empire in 1918 as it collapsed, and in 1921, on a street in Berlin, he was shot dead by Soghomon Tehlirian, an Armenian who had survived the deportations and lost most of his family to them. At Tehlirian's trial, so much of the historical record of the genocide was entered into evidence in an attempt to explain his motive that the German court acquitted him outright. The Armenian genocide is contested today, as all great crimes against humanity inevitably are from the inheritors of their legacies. The record shows what it shows.

Coda: Torpedo

On May 7th, 1915, off the Old Head of Kinsale, a German submarine put a single torpedo into the side of the Lusitania. The ship sank in eighteen minutes. Of the nearly two thousand people aboard, 1,198 died, including 128 Americans — families, businessmen, the popular writer Elbert Hubbard and his wife, who by one survivor's account linked arms on the tilting deck and simply waited, unable to think of anything else to do.

The Lusitania was not an accident of war. It was Cunard's answer to German dominance of the transatlantic passenger trade — the fastest ship afloat, holder of the Blue Riband, a a floating advertisement for what human engineering could accomplish by 1907: turbine engines, electric light, fifty percent more passenger space than any rival, built in close cooperation with the British Admiralty on the explicit understanding that in wartime she could become a cruiser. She never did. She spent the war doing exactly what she'd always done, carrying passengers and cargo across the Atlantic at speed, under the assumption — widely shared, publicly stated by a Cunard official days before she sailed — that nothing built by man could actually catch her. Germany had announced months earlier that it considered the waters around Britain a war zone, and had placed a formal warning in fifty American newspapers the same week the Lusitania left New York, cautioning that ships flying the British flag sailed at their own risk. Almost no one believed the threat was real. A German submarine catching the fastest liner in the world seemed, to most of the people who boarded her that week, like a physical impossibility.

It took Kapitänleutnant Walther Schwieger a single torpedo, fired from a submerged position, to prove otherwise.

The parallel that occurred to people almost immediately, and has occurred to nearly everyone who has written about the sinking since, is the one that hardly needs pointing out: three years earlier, another unsinkable marvel of the age had gone down in the North Atlantic, taken not by any human hand but by a piece of ice that didn't know or care what it was tearing open. The Titanic had been a tragedy of hubris — a testament to what happens when human confidence outpaces the ocean's actual indifference to it. The Lusitania was something else entirely. Nothing indifferent sank her. A man in a steel tube beneath the water had looked at a ship carrying nearly two thousand civilians, made a decision, and fired. The age that had built unsinkable ships to conquer nature had, within three years, built the weapon that made nature irrelevant to the question of whether they'd survive.

That, more than the diplomatic fallout — and there was plenty: American outrage, three strongly worded notes from President Wilson to Berlin, a German government that tried first to justify the sinking by pointing to the rifle cartridges genuinely stored in the Lusitania's hold, and then simply refused to apologize at all — is the fact this year keeps arriving at from every direction. It is there in a Bajío wheat field, where an army that had never lost discovers that machine guns end invincibility as surely as they end men. It is there in a mountaintop cross burning in Georgia, an old hatred rediscovering itself through the newest medium available to broadcast it. It is there in a sealed document handed to a Chinese president, informing him that his country's sovereignty was worth exactly as much attention as the great powers could spare from killing each other in Belgium. It is there in the frozen failure at Bolimów and the successful horror at Ypres, in a Russian empire burning its own provinces as it retreated from a war it hadn't wanted and couldn't win, in a peninsula where two new nations were assembled from the wreckage of a single failed invasion, and in a desert where a government emptied out its own ancient minority under cover of a war that gave it permission no one thought to check.

Nineteen-fifteen did not invent any of this. It simply removed, one confirmation at a time, every remaining assumption about what the century's opening promises had actually been worth. The submarine that sank the Lusitania did not need to sink the largest ship in the world to prove its point. It only needed to prove that the world which had built that ship — fast, luxurious, certain of its own permanence — was already gone, and had been for longer than anyone sailing on her that week had let themselves believe.
