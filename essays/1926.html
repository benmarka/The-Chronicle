FLUCTUATIONS

Covers Celebrity, Politics, Succession Themes Economics, Instability, Replacement

Intro: Death of the Old Order

Claude Monet died at Giverny on December 5, at eighty-six, of lung cancer. He had outlived almost everyone who mattered to the movement he helped name — Renoir, Degas, Cézanne, all gone — and he had lived long enough to see Impressionism, once dismissed by the Paris Salon as unfinished and undisciplined, become the safest, most beloved painting style in the Western world. He had also lived long enough to watch something stranger take its place. Six years earlier André Breton had published his first automatic-writing experiments; two years before that, the Surrealist Manifesto. A movement built on the dream, the accident, the unconscious was already crowding the space Monet's generation had cleared. He died working on the water lilies, a body of paintings so dissolved into color and light that later critics would call them proto-abstract — an old radical's final canvases anticipating the next radicalism without quite belonging to it.

Rudolph Valentino died in August, in a New York hospital, of peritonitis following a ruptured ulcer. He was thirty-one. What happened afterward had little to do with the illness. Tens of thousands filed past his casket at Frank Campbell's funeral home; mounted police were called in to control the crowds; windows broke. A woman in black — never conclusively identified, possibly hired publicity, possibly genuine — was reported returning to lay a rose at the casket each year after. Newspapers ran breathless accounts of grief-stricken fans taking their own lives, though how many of those deaths were real and how many were invented to match the story is not something the record settles cleanly. What is certain is that a man whose entire public self was constructed — the accent softened, the name shortened, an image built frame by frame for an audience that had never seen him speak — died and the illusion did not break. It deepened. The mourning was, in its way, the most successful performance of his career, staged without him.

Harry Houdini died on Halloween, in Detroit, also of peritonitis, also from a ruptured appendix — the timing close enough to Valentino's death, the diagnosis close enough to the letter, that newspapers noted the coincidence at the time. The immediate cause was murkier than Valentino's. Days earlier, a McGill University student named Jocelyn Gordon Whitehead had punched him in the stomach, testing a boast Houdini had made about withstanding blows to the abdomen; whether the punch ruptured an already-inflamed appendix or simply masked symptoms that were building regardless is a question his doctors could not answer with confidence, and neither can anyone since. It was a fittingly uncertain end for a man whose career had been built on convincing people that the impossible was happening in front of them — the locked trunk, the milk can, the straitjacket suspended over a city street — while never once, in fact, breaking a law of physics. Houdini had spent his last years turning that same skepticism outward, touring the country exposing the mediums and spiritualists who claimed to speak with the dead, most famously breaking with his friend Arthur Conan Doyle over it. He died refusing to believe in the impossible even as he built a career on performing it. Appropriately, no séance held in his name in the years afterward ever produced the message he had promised, in advance, to send back if he could.

Three deaths, one autumn, and in each case the same reversal: the illusion outlasting the man who built it. That was, in its way, the condition of the whole decade. It ran on spectacle, on performance, on things that looked solid holding almost no structural weight — and 1926 was the year that condition stopped being confined to the movie palace and the magic act and started showing up everywhere else.

Part I: The Lost Generation

Ernest Hemingway published The Sun Also Rises in October, and the Lost Generation had its testament. The novel's flatness was the point — expatriates drinking through Paris and Pamplona, a war wound that makes physical love impossible, characters who talk around everything that actually hurts them. Gertrude Stein had given Hemingway the phrase that became his epigraph, "You are all a lost generation," meant half as diagnosis and half as insult, and Hemingway printed it without apparent irony. The prose itself was the argument: short declarative sentences, almost no adjectives, feeling forced underground into what he was leaving out rather than what he said. It was a style built for people who had stopped trusting grand language to mean anything, which was, not coincidentally, most of Europe and a fair share of America in 1926.

Uptown from where American expatriates were failing to feel things in Paris, Harlem was in the middle of its richest literary stretch. Langston Hughes published The Weary Blues this year, his first collection, built on the rhythms of the music itself — the twelve-bar structure, the repetition, the vernacular voice — rather than imposing conventional poetic form on Black subject matter from the outside. Countee Cullen and Nella Larsen were publishing in the same narrow window. The Harlem Renaissance was never a single aesthetic program, and its writers argued openly about what the work should be doing — art for its own sake against art as racial uplift, respectability against raw vernacular truth — but 1926 was a year when that argument was producing extraordinary work on every side of it rather than just posturing about it.

The music underneath the literature was moving just as fast. Louis Armstrong's Hot Five recordings from this period essentially invent the jazz solo as a form unto itself — a voice stepping out of the ensemble to improvise, then stepping back in, a structure that would define popular music for the rest of the century. Duke Ellington was building his band at Harlem's Kentucky Club, several years from the Cotton Club residency that would make him famous, but the sound was already there: sophisticated, orchestral, built for dancing but too complex to be only that. Josephine Baker, who had arrived in Paris the year before with La Revue Nègre, was the sensation of the city by 1926 — a Black American performer more celebrated in France than she had ever been allowed to be at home.

Fashion moved in the same direction as the music: toward motion, toward the body freed from the corset and the floor-length hem. This is generally the year credited with Chanel's little black dress — Vogue ran the sketch, comparing it to the Model T, a dress simple and standardized enough to be worn by anyone. The garçonne silhouette was everywhere by now: short hair, dropped waists, straight lines where the Edwardian era had insisted on curves. It was a fashion built for a woman who danced, smoked, and moved through public space on her own terms, and it had been building since the war took so many men out of civilian life that women had simply kept doing the jobs and occupying the spaces they'd been given by necessity.

That same appetite for the body doing something verifiably difficult showed up outside the arts entirely. In August, Gertrude Ederle swam the English Channel — the first woman to do it, and faster than any of the five men who had managed it before her. It made front pages on both sides of the Atlantic. Next to a decade so saturated with performance and illusion, there was something almost bracing about a feat with no ambiguity in it at all: a stopwatch, a coastline, a result nobody could stage.

None of this — the clipped prose, the Harlem verse, the improvised solo, the dropped waistline — was really about art for its own sake. It was a culture working out, across every medium at once, how much could be left unsaid, undecorated, unresolved, and still land. The old order had built its authority on control: the studio-manufactured stardom, the tightly engineered illusion, the corset. What was replacing it ran on improvisation instead — on trusting the gap, the syncopation, the sentence that stopped short of the feeling. It was, in its own way, a culture starting to get comfortable with uncertainty before physics gave it a vocabulary for that comfort.

Part II: Economic Shocks

Britain spent nine days in May finding out what a general strike actually costs. The trouble had been building for months. Coal owners, squeezed by a return to the gold standard the year before that had made British exports more expensive and by German mines in the Ruhr coming back online with modernized equipment, wanted miners to accept longer hours and wage cuts of ten to twenty-five percent depending on the district. The Miners' Federation refused under the slogan "not a penny off the pay, not a second on the day," and the Trades Union Congress, after a year of subsidies and a Royal Commission report that satisfied nobody, called a general strike in solidarity beginning May 3. For nine days, roughly one and a half million transport workers, printers, dockers, iron and steel workers, and builders walked out alongside the miners — not everyone, the TUC held some unions in reserve, but enough to stop the country's ordinary functioning cold.

The government had been preparing far longer and far better than the unions had. Winston Churchill, as Chancellor, ran an emergency newspaper, the British Gazette, out of commandeered printing presses to keep official information flowing; the Navy and volunteer strikebreakers kept docks working; middle-class and upper-class volunteers, many of them undergraduates treating it as an adventure, drove buses and trains under police escort. The TUC, by contrast, had spent the run-up to the strike negotiating rather than organizing, and had no real plan for what happened if the government simply refused to talk and the country didn't grind to the halt they'd expected. It didn't grind to a halt. Enough of the volunteer effort held that daily life in London and the southeast continued in a diminished but functional way, and after nine days the TUC's general council, frightened by its own audacity and worried about the legal exposure of an indefinite strike, called it off on May 12 without securing a single concession for the miners it had walked out to support.

The miners, feeling betrayed and having no intention of accepting the pay cuts that had started the whole dispute, kept striking alone. They held out through the summer and into the autumn, increasingly hungry, increasingly broke, increasingly abandoned by the labor movement that had called out and then stood down around them, until district by district they drifted back to work through November and December on terms considerably worse than what they'd been offered before the strike began — longer hours, lower pay, and in many cases the loss of the national wage agreements they'd been defending in the first place. The general strike as a political weapon never really recovered its credibility in Britain after 1926; the government moved quickly to pass legislation making sympathetic strikes illegal, and the labor movement drew the lesson that direct confrontation with the state was a fight it couldn't win.

Florida's collapse was quieter but larger in raw financial terms, and it had been building for longer. Since roughly 1923, land in and around Miami had been changing hands in a speculative frenzy that had almost nothing to do with the land itself. Buyers put down modest deposits — binders, they were called — on lots they'd frequently never seen, then resold the binder itself before the balance ever came due, sometimes flipping the same parcel three or four times in a matter of weeks, each sale at a markup. Developers were subdividing palmetto scrub and mangrove swamp into paper cities with names like Boca Raton and Coral Gables, selling the promise of a tropical paradise to buyers as far away as the Midwest and New England who understood the transaction as pure speculation and had no intention of ever setting foot on the property. At the boom's peak, some lots had appreciated tenfold in under two years without a single structure being built on them.

By early 1926 the strain was already showing. A shortage of cement and building materials, made worse when a schooner carrying lumber sank in the mouth of Miami's harbor and blocked shipping for weeks, slowed the construction that was supposed to eventually justify the paper prices. Credit was tightening as northern banks grew wary of Florida exposure. Buyers who'd been counting on reselling before their balance came due were starting to find no one left to sell to. Then in September a major hurricane came ashore directly over Miami — sustained winds over a hundred miles an hour, a storm surge that drowned much of the bay front, several hundred people killed and tens of thousands left homeless. It didn't cause the collapse so much as make it impossible to keep pretending the collapse wasn't happening. Property that had existed mostly as a number on a contract was suddenly visibly, physically wrecked, and the fantasy that had sustained the boom couldn't survive contact with a fact that concrete. Florida's economy stayed depressed for years afterward, sliding into a regional depression a full three years before the rest of the country followed it into the real one — the same mechanism in miniature, price detached entirely from underlying value and sustained only by the expectation that someone else would keep believing in it, and almost no one outside Florida read it as a warning at the time.

Germany's economy, meanwhile, was being kept upright by a more deliberate and more fragile arrangement. The Dawes Plan, now in its second full year, had restructured Germany's reparations payments into installments calibrated to what Weimar's economy could plausibly bear, and had arranged for an initial loan, heavily subscribed by American banks, to get the payment schedule started. What followed was a financing loop that would have looked reckless if anyone examining it closely: American capital flowed into Germany as loans and direct investment, German industry used that capital to rebuild and modernize, Germany made its reparations payments to France and Britain out of a mix of that borrowed capital and genuine recovering tax revenue, and France and Britain used a portion of those reparations to service their own war debts back to the United States. Money that originated on Wall Street was, in effect, circling back to Wall Street by a different route, with German industrial recovery and French and British budget stability both financed along the way. In the narrow, immediate sense it was working. Real wages in Germany were recovering, industrial output was climbing back toward prewar levels, the hyperinflation of 1923 — when workers had been paid twice daily because prices changed between the morning and the afternoon — was fast becoming a memory rather than a lived condition. Stresemann's diplomacy and this financial architecture were reinforcing each other: a stable, solvent Germany was easier to admit into Locarno and the League, and a Germany being welcomed back into the European order was a safer bet for American lenders.

But the arrangement depended entirely on that capital continuing to flow inward at roughly the same rate indefinitely, financing reparations, industrial expansion, and municipal borrowing all at once, on the assumption that American investors would keep finding better returns in German bonds than at home. Nothing about that assumption was load-bearing in any structural sense. It was confidence, dressed up as policy, exactly like the money still moving through Florida's binder contracts and exactly like the fragile truce the TUC had extended to the miners before losing its nerve — three different countries, three different mechanisms, and underneath all of them the same quiet dependence on everyone continuing to believe the thing was solid, right up until, in one case or another, it stopped being true.

Part III: International Fluctuations

Chiang Kai-shek launched the Northern Expedition from Guangzhou in July, and by the end of 1926 it was clear the warlord era in China was not going to survive it intact. The country had been fragmented since the collapse of the Qing and the death of Yuan Shikai a decade earlier, carved into shifting territories controlled by regional militarists who taxed, conscripted, and fought each other with a fluidity that made any national government mostly theoretical. Sun Yat-sen's Kuomintang had spent years building a base in the south, and after Sun's death in 1925 leadership of both the party and its National Revolutionary Army had passed to Chiang, a Whampoa Military Academy commandant with Soviet training and Soviet advisors behind him. The Expedition's initial run north through Hunan and into the Yangtze valley went faster than almost anyone expected — cities fell in weeks rather than months, in part because Chiang's army had genuine popular organizing behind it, peasant associations and labor unions doing political work ahead of the troops, softening resistance before a shot was fired. By the end of the year, Wuhan and Nanchang had both fallen, and the Kuomintang controlled a swath of central China it hadn't held a year before.

All of this was still happening under the First United Front, the uneasy alliance between the Kuomintang and the young Chinese Communist Party that Soviet advisors had brokered in 1923. Communist organizers were doing much of the peasant mobilization that made the Expedition's advance so fast, and Chiang was, for the moment, willing to use them for it. The alliance would not survive 1927 — Chiang's purge of Communist cadres in Shanghai the following spring is one of the twentieth century's sharper historical hinges, and everyone involved in 1926 was operating in the last stretch of road before it — but that rupture hadn't happened yet. What 1926 shows is the alliance at its most productive, a coalition of convenience achieving in months what a decade of divided warlordism hadn't allowed, with the contradiction that would break it already fully present and simply not yet triggered.

Germany's path back into the international system was slower and less dramatic, but no less consequential. Locarno had been signed the year before — a set of treaties in which Germany, France, and Belgium mutually guaranteed their existing western borders, with Britain and Italy standing as guarantors — and 1926 was the year that framework produced its concrete result. In September, Germany was formally admitted to the League of Nations, with a permanent seat on the Council, a status the Treaty of Versailles's architects would have found close to unthinkable seven years earlier. Gustav Stresemann, Germany's foreign minister, had spent the mid-1920s pursuing what amounted to a rehabilitation campaign: honoring reparations obligations under the Dawes Plan, renouncing any revision of the western frontier, and trading that cooperation for readmission to the community of powers that had excluded Germany since the war. It worked, at least on its own terms. Stresemann and his French counterpart Aristide Briand would share the Nobel Peace Prize that same year for the Locarno work, and for a brief window the postwar settlement in western Europe looked less like an armistice and more like an actual peace, built on mutual guarantee rather than imposed restriction.

The catch, largely unspoken in 1926, was that Locarno only guaranteed Germany's western borders. Its eastern borders — with Poland and Czechoslovakia, drawn at Versailles through territory Germans across the political spectrum considered unjustly ceded — were left conspicuously unaddressed, guaranteed by nothing beyond the League's general authority. Stresemann had wanted it that way. Rehabilitation in the west bought him room to maneuver in the east, and nobody negotiating the treaties in 1925 had wanted to force the question of Germany's eastern frontier if it meant the whole framework might collapse. It was a peace built with a deliberate gap in it, and the gap would still be there in 1938.

In Morocco, a different kind of order was being restored by force rather than treaty. Abd el-Krim's Republic of the Rif had, for several years, done something almost no colonial resistance movement in Africa had managed: it had inflicted a genuine military catastrophe on a European power, wiping out a Spanish army of some thirteen thousand men at Annual in 1921 and holding functional independence across a swath of northern Morocco's mountains ever since. By 1925 the threat had grown serious enough that Spain and France, colonial rivals who rarely cooperated on anything, put together a joint military campaign against him — over a quarter of a million troops between them, backed by aircraft and, in incidents that would later draw scrutiny, chemical weapons dropped on Rifian villages. Abd el-Krim surrendered to French forces in May 1926, choosing French custody over the harsher treatment he expected from Spain, and was exiled to Réunion in the Indian Ocean. The Rif War's ending restored colonial control in name, but restoring it had required the two largest powers in western Europe to combine their militaries against a movement of tribal irregulars, and had taken five years to do it. Abd el-Krim's resistance became, almost immediately, a reference point for anti-colonial movements elsewhere — Ho Chi Minh cited him admiringly, and the playbook of protracted guerrilla resistance against a technologically superior occupying force would be run again, with growing success, for the rest of the century.

Three different theaters, three different mechanisms — revolutionary alliance, diplomatic rehabilitation, colonial reconquest — and in each case a power structure reasserting itself over the year, whether that meant a fractured nation being pulled back toward unification, a defeated nation being pulled back into the concert of Europe, or a rebellious protectorate being pulled back under the flag. None of the three reassertions would hold in the shape they took in 1926. The United Front would break within a year. The eastern gap in Locarno would eventually swallow the whole architecture around it. And the peace bought in the Rif would be remembered, by the people who'd fought it, less as a defeat than as a proof of concept.

Part IV - Cracks in the Peace

Poland's parliamentary democracy, such as it was, ended in three days in May. The Second Polish Republic had struggled since independence in 1918 with a fractured parliament, a dozen or more parties splitting the vote, weak coalition governments that rarely lasted a year, and an economy battered by hyperinflation earlier in the decade. Józef Piłsudski, the military commander who'd led Poland's forces against the Soviets in 1920 and had been the closest thing the country had to a founding father, had stepped back from formal power in 1923, disgusted with the parliamentary chaos. By May 1926 he'd concluded the chaos wasn't going to fix itself. He marched loyal army units into Warsaw on May 12 — the same week, as it happened, that the TUC was calling off the British general strike — and after three days of street fighting that killed several hundred people, forced the resignation of the government and installed a regime under his effective personal control, though he kept the parliamentary shell technically intact and never formally declared himself dictator. The system that emerged, called sanacja — "sanitation," or "healing" — was Piłsudski's own term for it, a claim that he was curing the republic's dysfunction rather than ending its democracy. Whatever the label, functional parliamentary politics in Poland was over. It wouldn't return before the war ended the state that held it.

Portugal's First Republic ended the same month, on May 28, in a coup with even less resistance than Poland's. The Republic, sixteen years old by 1926, had been chronically unstable almost from its founding — dozens of governments in sixteen years, a revolving cast of short-lived cabinets, political violence that had included the assassination of a sitting president in 1918. A group of army officers seized power with minimal fighting and installed General Óscar Carmona, and while the coup's immediate goals were vague — restoring order, ending the instability, little more specific than that — it opened the door to the Ditadura Nacional that would, within a few years, produce António de Oliveira Salazar and the corporatist authoritarian state that governed Portugal for the next four decades. As with Poland, nobody staging the coup in May 1926 was necessarily aiming at what it eventually became. What both cases share is a pattern that would repeat across interwar Europe: a parliamentary system too fractured and too discredited to defend itself, and a military willing to promise order in exchange for the public simply not objecting very hard when democracy was quietly retired.

Italy's crisis in 1926 wasn't the collapse of democracy — that had already happened, incrementally, since Mussolini's march on Rome in 1922 and had been essentially finished off in 1925 after the murder of the socialist deputy Giacomo Matteotti. What 1926 offered instead was proof of how far the consolidation had already gone, in the form of two assassination attempts Mussolini survived within five months of each other. In April, an Irish aristocrat named Violet Gibson, acting alone and by her own account on instructions from God, shot him at close range in Rome; the bullet grazed his nose, and he continued the day's public appearances with a bandage across his face rather than cancel them. Gibson was found insane and deported to an asylum in England, where she remained for the rest of her life. In September, an anarchist stonemason named Gino Lucetti threw a bomb at Mussolini's car in Rome; it struck the vehicle and bounced off before detonating, and Mussolini was unhurt. Both attempts, rather than exposing the regime's fragility, became instruments of its consolidation. Mussolini used them as pretext for a fourth round of "exceptional laws" passed that November — banning all opposition parties outright, reinstating the death penalty for crimes against the king or the head of government, establishing a special tribunal for political offenses, and formally completing the transition from an authoritarian-leaning parliamentary government to an outright one-party fascist state. By the end of 1926, Italy had no legal opposition left to attempt anything through, violent or otherwise.

Three different mechanisms, again, and three different outcomes on a spectrum from ambiguous to total: Poland's sanacja, presented as a cure rather than a coup and leaving parliament's furniture in place even as its function emptied out; Portugal's coup, vague enough in its founding purpose that its authoritarian destination took years to fully declare itself; and Italy, where the pretense had already been dropped and 1926 simply closed the last gaps. What connects them isn't ideology — Piłsudski was no fascist, and his regime never resembled Mussolini's in structure or aim — but a shared diagnosis, made independently in three capitals, that parliamentary democracy in its 1920s form couldn't hold, and a shared willingness among the people watching it fail to accept something else in its place without much of a fight. The peace of 1919 had assumed a Europe of stable parliamentary states. By the end of 1926, a third of the continent's newer democracies had already concluded otherwise.

Part V: Succession Crisis

Lenin had been dead for two years by 1926, and the fight over what came after him had never really stopped, only changed shape. In the immediate aftermath of his death in January 1924, Stalin had allied with Zinoviev and Kamenev — the ruling troika, functionally — against Trotsky, using his position as General Secretary, a job everyone else had considered administrative and beneath serious attention, to place loyalists throughout the party apparatus while his rivals fought Trotsky over doctrine and legacy. Trotsky, the most visible revolutionary hero after Lenin himself, the organizer of the October seizure of power and the architect of the Red Army's victory in the Civil War, was also the man the rest of the leadership trusted least — too brilliant, too contemptuous of the party's duller functionaries, too recently arrived to Bolshevism from Menshevism to be considered fully one of them by the old guard. By late 1924 and through 1925, the troika had used exactly that distrust to marginalize him, stripping him of his post as Commissar of War in early 1925 and reducing him to a member of the Central Committee with diminishing real authority.

What nobody quite anticipated was that the troika itself wouldn't survive its own victory. Zinoviev and Kamenev, having helped break Trotsky's power, discovered across 1925 that Stalin had used the same period to build an apparatus loyal to himself rather than to any of them, and that his patient control of appointments and party organization was translating into something closer to personal dominance than any of them had planned for. Zinoviev, based in Leningrad and chairing the Comintern, and Kamenev, based in Moscow, both found their own regional power bases increasingly outmaneuvered by Stalin allies moving into position beneath them. By late 1925, at the Fourteenth Party Congress, Zinoviev and Kamenev found themselves booed down and defeated on the floor by a Stalin-aligned majority they'd helped create the conditions for. The lesson landed slowly, but by 1926 it had landed: the man they'd used to destroy Trotsky was going to destroy them too, by the same methods, unless they moved first.

What followed in 1926 was one of the stranger alliances of the decade. In the spring, Zinoviev and Kamenev approached Trotsky — the man they had spent two years marginalizing, whose defeat they had personally engineered — and proposed joining forces against Stalin. Trotsky, either out of genuine conviction that Stalin represented a bureaucratic betrayal of the revolution worse than anything his former enemies had done, or simply out of the calculation that isolated opposition had already failed and a coalition was the only path left, agreed. The result, formed through the spring and summer of 1926, was what became known as the United Opposition — a platform combining Trotsky's critique of "socialism in one country" as a retreat from international revolution with Zinoviev and Kamenev's own grievances about the suppression of internal party democracy, the growing power of the bureaucracy, and the marginalization of the old Bolshevik cadre in favor of newer, more pliable appointees.

It did not work, and arguably could not have worked, given how it started. The Opposition's members had spent the previous several years publicly attacking each other in terms the party's rank and file remembered clearly — Zinoviev and Kamenev had called Trotsky a threat to Leninism itself as recently as 1925, and reversing that position within months looked, to exactly the party members whose support they needed, like naked expediency rather than principle. Stalin, meanwhile, controlled the apparatus that decided who spoke at party meetings, who got promoted, whose resolutions reached a vote, and increasingly whose voice reached print at all. Through the second half of 1926 the Opposition attempted open appeals — factory floor speeches, underground leaflets, a failed attempt at a demonstration in Moscow in November that was broken up before it could gather momentum — and each attempt gave Stalin's majority fresh grounds to accuse them of factionalism, a charge that under the party's own rules, adopted at Lenin's urging back in 1921 to prevent exactly this kind of internal faction-building, was itself a disciplinary offense. In October, Trotsky was removed from the Politburo. Zinoviev lost his post as head of the Comintern that same month, the international body he had led since its founding. Kamenev was already reduced to a peripheral role.

None of them were expelled from the party outright in 1926 — that escalation, along with Trotsky's exile first internally and then from the Soviet Union entirely, was still a year or more away, and Stalin was still working within forms that resembled collective party governance rather than the personal dictatorship the 1930s would bring. But the shape of what was coming was fully visible by the end of the year to anyone watching closely. The old Bolshevik leadership, the men who had made the revolution together and fought the Civil War together, had spent 1926 discovering that the habits of intrigue and elimination they had first turned on their enemies outside the party worked exactly as well turned on each other, and that the one among them who understood the party's internal machinery best, rather than the ones with the most prestige or the sharpest doctrine, was the one accumulating the power to decide who got to keep speaking at all.

Coda: Physical Disruptions

In January 1926, working from a sanatorium in the Swiss Alps where he'd gone partly for his tuberculosis and partly, by his own later account, to think without interruption, Erwin Schrödinger published the first of four papers laying out what became known as wave mechanics. The atom, in his formulation, wasn't a miniature solar system with electrons orbiting a nucleus the way earlier models had pictured it. It was a wave — a mathematical function spread through space, describing not where an electron was but the probability of finding it at any given point if you went looking. It was, by the standards of physics up to that point, a genuinely strange claim, and it arrived only months after Werner Heisenberg had published an entirely different mathematical formulation, matrix mechanics, that described the same phenomena using no spatial picture at all — just tables of numbers and operations on them, with no attempt to say what an electron was actually doing between measurements. Two theories, radically different in method and in what they claimed reality looked like underneath, and within the year Schrödinger himself proved they were mathematically equivalent: different languages describing the identical thing.

What neither formulation could do, and what became the year's most consequential complication, was say what was actually happening to a particle when nobody was measuring it. Max Born, working through the implications of Schrödinger's wave function later in 1926, proposed that the wave shouldn't be read as a literal physical wave at all, but as a probability distribution — the square of the wave function at any point gave you the likelihood of finding the particle there, and nothing in the mathematics allowed for more certainty than that, even in principle. This was the interpretation that would calcify, over the following years, into what's usually called the Copenhagen interpretation, and it offended people who might have been expected to embrace it. Einstein, whose own 1905 work on light quanta had helped start quantum theory in the first place, spent the rest of his life uneasy with the idea that the universe's fundamental description was probabilistic rather than definite — that a particle didn't have a position before you measured it, only a spread of odds. God, he would write to Born the following year, does not play dice. Schrödinger felt something similar, disliking the probabilistic reading of his own equation enough that he'd later construct the cat-in-a-box thought experiment specifically to make the interpretation look absurd. Neither of them dislodged it. Uncertainty, by the end of 1926, wasn't a temporary gap in physicists' knowledge waiting to be closed by a better instrument or a cleverer equation. It was, on the evidence so far, a description of how the universe actually worked at its smallest scale — not our ignorance of the particle's position, but the particle's position genuinely not existing as a single fact until something forced it to.

Almost nobody outside a small circle of physicists paid this any attention in 1926, and even fewer paid attention to what happened on a snow-covered farm in Auburn, Massachusetts, on March 16. Robert Goddard, a physics professor who had been pursuing liquid-fueled rocketry largely alone and largely ridiculed for it — the New York Times had run an editorial two years earlier mocking his claim that a rocket could function in the vacuum of space, on the mistaken grounds that a rocket needed air to push against — launched a rocket powered by liquid oxygen and gasoline from his aunt's farm. It rose about forty-one feet, flew for roughly two and a half seconds, and landed a hundred and eighty-four feet away in a cabbage patch. Goddard's own journal entry for the day was almost comically understated next to what the flight actually represented: the first liquid-fueled rocket launch in history, the direct ancestral line running forward through every rocket that would eventually leave the atmosphere, carry a satellite, or put a human being on the moon. It attracted no press coverage worth mentioning at the time. Goddard, stung by years of public mockery, had in fact become fairly secretive about his work by 1926, publishing less and trusting fewer people with the details, which meant that the flight that quietly opened the Space Age took place in front of almost no witnesses and made almost no impression on the world it was about to eventually leave.

Two events, then, at the furthest edges of what 1926 understood about itself — one describing the universe's smallest scale as fundamentally, permanently uncertain, the other taking the first physical step toward leaving the planet that scale exists on. Neither looked especially momentous at the time, next to a general strike, a fallen land boom, three governments extinguished in a season, and a fight for Lenin's succession that was rewriting who controlled the century's largest revolutionary state. But that was, in the end, the truest thing the year had to say about itself. 1926 had spent its politics, its economics, and its culture discovering the same fact from a dozen directions at once — that the structures people had trusted to be solid, a currency, a coalition, a peace treaty, a matinee idol's public image, a party's collective leadership, were never as fixed as they appeared, only probable, only a wave function that hadn't yet been asked to resolve. Physics simply arrived, that year, at the same conclusion by more rigorous means, and gave the condition a name precise enough to survive being written down: not a flaw in the measurement, but the actual shape of the thing being measured.
