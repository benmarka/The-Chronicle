THE MACHINE

Covers Assassination, Diplomacy, War Themes Violence, Shattering, Failure

Intro: The Massacre

Greek Easter fell on April 19 that year, and the miners at Ludlow, Colorado kept it the next morning as best they could — a tent colony of some 1,200 people, mostly immigrants, spread across a wind-scoured stretch of the southern Colorado coal country. They had been on strike since September, evicted from company housing by the Colorado Fuel and Iron Company for daring to organize, and had spent the winter building a canvas town in the snow rather than go back to the mines on the old terms: three dollars a day, twelve-hour shifts, company stores, company doctors, company law.

By late morning on April 20, the National Guard was firing into the tents from the hills above the colony. Nobody agrees, even now, on who fired first. What followed is less contested. Guardsmen brought up an improvised armored car mounted with a machine gun — the miners called it the Death Special — and raked the colony through the afternoon. Louis Tikas, a Greek-American organizer who had emigrated barely a decade earlier and become the camp's most trusted voice, walked out under a flag of truce to negotiate a ceasefire. He was shot in the back and left in the road.

At dusk the Guard moved down from the hills with torches and burned the tents. Most of the colony had already fled into the surrounding hills or the dry creek bed, but not everyone made it out. A telephone lineman picking through the ruins the next morning lifted an iron cot covering a pit dug beneath one of the tents — a cellar, the kind families had been digging all winter as shelter from stray gunfire — and found eleven children and two women dead of suffocation, the fire having burned the air out of the hole before it ever reached the bodies. Estimates of the day's total dead run from nineteen to twenty-five, depending on the source and what is counted as part of the massacre proper. In the ten days of retaliatory violence that followed — miners dynamiting mine works, executing guards, fighting pitched battles across two hundred miles of coalfield — the toll climbs higher still, past sixty by some counts, past a hundred by others. No one was ever indicted for what happened at Ludlow. Not a militiaman, not a mine guard, not an officer.

John D. Rockefeller Jr., who held a controlling stake in Colorado Fuel and Iron and had testified before Congress about the strike only months earlier, spent the spring at the family estate insisting there had been no massacre at all — that the fighting had been a fair contest between armed men, that no women or children had died at anyone's hands but their own bad luck. Upton Sinclair wrote him an open letter promising to see him indicted for murder in the court of public opinion, if nowhere else. The strike itself dragged on into December and then simply stopped, unresolved. The union got no recognition. The eight-hour day the miners had struck for would not come to the coalfields for another two decades.

It was, on its own terms, a very American atrocity — a private company arming a state militia to break a strike, an immigrant labor force treated as expendable, a massacre absorbed into the country's memory as a labor dispute rather than a killing. It would not stay contained to its own terms for long. Within months the machine gun would stop being a strikebreaker's novelty and become the year's defining instrument, and the particular horror of Ludlow — ordinary people dug into the earth, waiting for a violence too large and too impersonal to negotiate with — would repeat itself, at a scale no one at Ludlow could have imagined, in trenches on the other side of the world.

Part I: America’s Adventure in Mexico

Nine sailors went ashore at Tampico on April 9 to buy gasoline for the USS Dolphin's whaleboat. They wandered, by accident, into a part of the port that was off-limits to foreigners, and were arrested by soldiers loyal to General Victoriano Huerta, Mexico's dictator by way of a coup and an assassination the year before. The Mexican officer in command released them within the hour and apologized. It should have ended there — a footnote, a translation error, nine men who spoke no Spanish confused by nine soldiers who spoke no English.

It did not end there because Rear Admiral Henry T. Mayo decided an apology was not enough. He demanded a formal salute to the American flag — twenty-one guns, fired on Mexican soil, as a gesture of contrition for an incident that both sides agreed was a misunderstanding. Mexico offered a simultaneous exchange of salutes instead, flag for flag, honor for honor. Wilson rejected the compromise and turned the demand into an ultimatum. Huerta refused it. By April 18 the United States and Mexico were, in effect, at war over a salute that neither side had actually needed to ask for or refuse.

Wilson had wanted Huerta gone since the day he took office. He had refused to recognize the government in Mexico City as anything but "a government of butchers," installed by an ambassador he despised and maintained by a leader he considered illegitimate on principle. The Tampico incident gave him a pretext with genuine urgency behind it: American intelligence had learned that a German-flagged steamer, the Ypiranga, was approaching Veracruz loaded with rifles and ammunition for Huerta's army, arms that had been quietly routed through Odessa and Hamburg to dodge Wilson's own embargo. On April 20, before Congress had finished debating whether to authorize force at all, Wilson gave the order anyway. Take Veracruz, he told the Navy. At eleven the next morning, eight hundred Marines and sailors went ashore.

The fighting was not the formality Washington expected. Huerta's field commander had orders to abandon the port rather than defend it, but the cadets of the Mexican naval academy refused to leave. They fought from the windows of their own school, alongside armed dockworkers and civilians who had never held a rifle, against a landing force with naval gunfire in support. One of the cadets, José Azueta, kept firing a machine gun from a rooftop after he was already wounded, and died of the wounds days later; his name is still read aloud in the ceremonial roll call of the Mexican armed forces. The city fell within two days. Nineteen Americans died taking it. Mexican dead, soldiers and civilians together, are usually put somewhere between one and five hundred — the range itself a kind of evidence for how little care was taken to count.

And then the reason for the whole operation sailed away. The Ypiranga's captain refused to hand his cargo to the American officers now occupying the port and simply took the ship back out to sea, eventually unloading the arms at another Mexican port entirely, arms and all, to be carried overland to the army Wilson had gone to war to disarm. The United States had bombed a city, killed hundreds of people, and turned Latin American opinion against it for a generation, without stopping a single rifle from reaching Huerta.

Argentina, Brazil and Chile offered to mediate at Niagara Falls in May, and the talks accomplished almost nothing — Wilson would not withdraw until Huerta resigned, Huerta would not resign while foreign troops occupied his country's chief port, and the diplomats recessed in June with the actual question untouched. It was the battlefield that finally decided it, not the conference table. Venustiano Carranza's Constitutionalist armies, which the American embargo had done nothing to stop and American arms had occasionally reached by way of Villa, ground down what remained of Huerta's government through the spring, and on July 15 Huerta resigned and left the country for good. Wilson got the outcome he wanted. He had simply spent four months and several hundred lives achieving it by means that had nothing to do with the war he'd started to bring it about.

American troops stayed in Veracruz until November, an occupation with no remaining purpose once its ostensible purpose had already resolved itself elsewhere. Mexico's revolution did not pause to notice; Carranza and Villa were already turning on each other, and the deeper civil war — the one that would eventually draw in Zapata's peasant armies and pull the United States back across the border in earnest — was only getting started. But that is a story for the following year. What 1914 in Mexico offers, on its own, is a smaller and stranger preview of the one to come: a war triggered by an insult nobody quite gave, justified by a threat that dissolved on its own the moment troops landed, fought by boys who held a schoolhouse because men with more authority had already given up on it, and concluded, months later, for reasons that had nothing to do with any of the fighting in between.

Part II: The Opening Shots

Franz Ferdinand had married for love, which was itself an act of quiet defiance. Sophie Chotek came from minor Bohemian nobility, respectable but not remotely exalted enough for an heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, and Emperor Franz Josef made his displeasure a matter of permanent record. He allowed the marriage only on the condition that it be morganatic — Sophie would never be empress, their children would never inherit — and enforced the distinction with a pettiness that followed the couple everywhere. At court functions Sophie entered rooms alone, behind her husband, and was seated apart from him at state dinners as though the marriage were merely a rumor. There was one place protocol made an exception: when Franz Ferdinand appeared in a strictly military capacity, his wife could ride at his side as his equal. In June of 1914 he traveled to Bosnia to inspect imperial army maneuvers as inspector general, and Sophie went with him. For once, they could sit together in an open car. That is the reason she was in Sarajevo at all.

They had been warned. Bosnia had been annexed from Ottoman control only six years earlier, over the furious objections of Serbia, which considered the province rightfully its own and had spent those six years cultivating exactly the kind of underground nationalism that now waited for the archduke in the streets. Franz Ferdinand was told the trip carried risk and went anyway, reportedly joking, when the axle on his car overheated that morning, that first the car burned and now they'd throw bombs at them. He was not wrong about the bombs. He was wrong about how many chances the plot would need.

Seven young men were positioned along the Appel Quay that Sunday morning, June 28, recruited and armed by a Serbian secret society called Union or Death — the Black Hand — with pistols, grenades, and cyanide capsules in case of capture. Most of them were barely out of adolescence; several were still teenagers. As the motorcade passed, the first conspirator lost his nerve and did nothing. The second couldn't bring himself to draw his weapon. The third, Nedeljko Čabrinović, threw his bomb — it bounced off the folded back of the archduke's car, which Franz Ferdinand apparently deflected himself, and detonated under the vehicle behind it, wounding an officer and several bystanders. Čabrinović swallowed his cyanide and threw himself into the river to avoid capture. The cyanide, old and weak, only made him vomit. The river was ankle-deep. He was fished out and beaten by the crowd before police could reach him. The remaining conspirators, watching the motorcade speed away toward the town hall, did nothing at all. By late morning, the plot had already failed.

Franz Ferdinand insisted on continuing the day's schedule anyway, delivering his planned remarks at the town hall with the fresh bloodstains of the bombing still being cleaned from the street outside. Afterward he changed his plans: he wanted to visit the wounded officer in hospital before leaving the city. Nobody thought to tell the lead driver about the new route. The first car turned, out of habit, onto Franz Joseph Street rather than continuing straight along the quay; the archduke's car followed it; someone realized the mistake and ordered the driver to stop and reverse. The car halted, engine idling, directly in front of a delicatessen on the very corner where Gavrilo Princip, the plot's most committed member, had ended up after giving up on his own chance to act. He had not gone there for lunch, whatever the more colorful retellings would later claim — the shop happened to sit on the motorcade's original route, and Princip, unlike his more hesitant collaborators, had not stopped watching the street. He stepped forward, drew his pistol, and fired twice from a few feet away. One bullet caught Sophie in the abdomen. The other found Franz Ferdinand's neck. He is said to have begged her, as the car reversed away in confusion, not to die, to stay alive for their children. Both were dead within the half hour.

Princip tried to kill himself too — the same cyanide, the same failure to work, then a pistol wrestled out of his hands by the crowd before he could turn it on himself. He was nineteen, three weeks too young by Austrian law to be executed. He was sentenced instead to twenty years and died of tuberculosis in an Austrian prison in the spring of 1918, five months before the war he had started would end, having lived to see almost the whole of it.

Vienna's own investigators, sent to establish what the Serbian government had actually known, would report back within weeks that they had found no evidence tying Belgrade's ministers to the plot. The finding changed nothing about what came next. Franz Ferdinand's death was, for the men who governed Austria-Hungary, not really a grief to be processed so much as an opportunity finally arrived — a pretext, long wanted, now sitting in their hands with a body count already attached and a grieving empire behind it. What Serbia's government had or hadn't done was, by the time that report was filed, no longer the question anyone in Vienna was interested in answering.

Part III: The Blank Check

Franz Ferdinand and Sophie were buried on July 4, quietly, at his own estate at Artstetten rather than in the imperial crypt in Vienna — his marriage had disqualified her even in death. Emperor Franz Josef, who had never liked his nephew and had chosen him as heir only after his own son's suicide left no better option, did not attend the funeral, though he was in the city at the time. Grief, in Vienna, was thin on the ground. Opportunity was not.

On July 5, an Austrian envoy named Alexander von Hoyos arrived in Berlin carrying a letter from Franz Josef to Kaiser Wilhelm. It said, without much ornament, that Austria-Hungary could not survive unless Serbia was eliminated as a state. It was the fourth time in two years that Vienna had asked Berlin for backing to invade Serbia; the first three times Germany had said no, judging its own military not yet ready for the general war such a move might provoke. This time Wilhelm, still raw over the killing of a man he had personally liked and hosted at his own estate weeks earlier, said yes — full support, no conditions, whatever Austria chose to do, even if Russia intervened. He did not wait to consult his chancellor. The promise became known afterward as the blank check, and its defining quality was exactly that: nobody in Berlin asked to see what Austria intended to write before agreeing to back it.

Having gotten what it wanted, Vienna then took more than two weeks to use it — not from hesitation, but from scheduling. Reservists were on harvest leave and recalling them early would have tipped its hand and starved the empire besides. More tellingly, Austria's ministers deliberately timed the ultimatum to land only after French President Raymond Poincaré finished a state visit to Russia on July 23, so that France and Russia would not have the chance to coordinate their response to it face to face. The Austrian ambassador in Berlin kept Wilhelm's foreign office informed of the drafting as it happened. One dispatch reported that the note was being worded so that Serbian acceptance would be, in the ambassador's phrase, "practically excluded." Wilhelm underlined the sentence twice in the margin. When he learned the delivery would be delayed until after the French president's departure, he scrawled beside it: "What a pity!" He was not troubled by what the ultimatum would say. He was only impatient that it hadn't been sent already.

It arrived in Belgrade on the afternoon of July 23: ten demands, a forty-eight hour deadline, and a requirement — buried in point six — that Austrian officials be allowed to operate inside Serbian territory to investigate the assassination themselves, a demand that no sovereign government could accept and remain sovereign. Winston Churchill, watching from London, called it "the most insolent document of its kind ever devised." It had been written, by its own authors' private admission, to be refused.

Serbia refused almost nothing. Its reply, delivered just before the deadline on July 25, accepted nine of the ten demands outright, expressed regret for any Serbian involvement in the crime, and offered to submit the single sticking point — the demand for Austrian officials on Serbian soil — to arbitration at the Hague. It was, by the standards of ultimatums accepted under threat of war, a remarkable capitulation. The Austrian ambassador in Belgrade read it once, judged it insufficient, and left the country within the hour, breaking diplomatic relations before his own government had even finished discussing what to do next.

Wilhelm read the Serbian reply on the morning of July 28 and had, for a few hours, a moment of clarity that history has not been kind to. Serbia's submission, he wrote, was a great moral victory for Vienna, and with it "every cause for war" had disappeared. It should have ended there, by his own logic. Instead, in the same breath, he proposed that Austria occupy Belgrade anyway, just to be sure the promises were kept, dismissing the Serbs in his marginal notes as "Orientals, and therefore liars, fakers and masters of evasion." Having declared the crisis resolved, he immediately suggested a way to continue it. Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia that same day. The following morning, an Austrian river gunboat opened fire on Belgrade, after Serbian engineers blew the rail bridge connecting the two countries rather than let the war arrive over it intact. Two nations had gone to war very much on purpose. What came next would not be a matter of anyone's purpose at all.

Part IV: The Timetables

Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia on July 28. Within hours, an Austrian river gunboat was shelling Belgrade. From that moment, the decisions being made across Europe stopped resembling choices in any meaningful sense and started resembling switches — thrown one after another, each one closing off whatever remained of the alternative.

Russia moved first. It had already begun a partial mobilization against Austria-Hungary before the declaration of war, meant to pressure Vienna without directly provoking Germany. On July 30, under pressure from his generals, who warned that a partial mobilization was itself logistically unworkable — the rail networks and supply plans existed only for the all-in version — Tsar Nicholas II authorized general mobilization of the entire Russian army. Germany answered with two ultimatums delivered the following day: Russia had twelve hours to halt all mobilization, and France, Russia's ally, had eighteen hours to declare whether it would remain neutral in a Russo-German war. Neither ultimatum was remotely answerable in the time given, and neither government answered it. On August 1, at five in the afternoon, Kaiser Wilhelm II signed the order for German general mobilization. Within the hour, Germany declared war on Russia.

It was that same evening that the war's real machinery revealed itself, in a scene that would have been comic if it weren't catastrophic. A telegram arrived from the German ambassador in London suggesting — on a misunderstanding, as it turned out — that Britain might guarantee French neutrality if Germany fought only Russia and left France alone. Wilhelm, delighted at the prospect of a one-front war, summoned his chief of the general staff and told him to redirect the entire army eastward: "Now we can go to war against Russia only. We simply march the whole of our Army to the East!" General Helmuth von Moltke told him it could not be done. Not would not — could not. Germany's mobilization plan, the product of a decade of railway scheduling refined down to the individual troop train, existed in exactly one version, aimed at France through Belgium. There was no eastward equivalent ready to execute at that scale, no matter what Moltke's own subordinates might quietly have been able to improvise if asked. He did not ask them. He told his Kaiser flatly that redirecting millions of already-moving soldiers was not the kind of thing that could be reversed by decree, and that trying would produce not an army but "a disorganized mob of armed men with no arrangements for supply." Wilhelm, stung, snapped that his uncle would have found a way. Moltke did not relent. The trains kept running west. The misunderstanding about British neutrality was cleared up within hours anyway — Britain had offered no such thing — but by then it hardly mattered. The machine that could not be paused for a real diplomatic opening could also not be paused for an imaginary one.

The invasion of France required passing through Belgium, whose neutrality Britain, France, and Germany itself had jointly guaranteed by treaty in 1839. On the evening of August 2, Germany presented Belgium with an ultimatum: allow German troops free passage or be treated as an enemy. King Albert refused. At eight in the morning on August 4, German forces crossed the border anyway, and the war that had begun as an Austro-Serbian quarrel over an assassination now had a fourth and fifth combatant neither the assassin nor his handlers had ever had in mind.

Britain's involvement had been genuinely uncertain until that morning — its cabinet divided, its formal treaty obligations to France ambiguous by design, decades of diplomacy built around not quite promising to fight. Belgium settled it. The British government sent its own ultimatum to Berlin demanding a German withdrawal by midnight. Foreign Secretary Edward Grey, watching the lamplighters go about their evening work outside the windows of the Foreign Office, remarked to a friend that the lamps were going out all over Europe, and that they would not see them lit again in their lifetime. No answer came from Berlin. At eleven that night, London time, Britain declared war on Germany.

One week had passed between Austria's declaration of war on a country of five million people and the involvement of Germany, Russia, France, and Britain — four empires and a kingdom, controlling between them a quarter of the earth's population, now formally at war over a chain of ultimatums, deadlines, and mobilization tables that had left almost no one, at almost any point, an actual decision to make. The month of choices was over. What came next would be decided in the field.

Part V: The Marne

By the end of August, the Schlieffen Plan appeared to be working almost exactly as drawn. German armies had smashed through Belgium, thrown back the French offensive into Alsace-Lorraine, and were now driving southwest in pursuit of a French and British force in full retreat — the so-called Great Retreat, nearly two hundred miles on foot in summer heat, wool uniforms, sixty-pound packs. By September 2, German cavalry had been sighted at Meaux, twenty-five miles from Paris. The French government left the city for Bordeaux. Kaiser Wilhelm's officers were already speaking, without much modesty, about which Parisian buildings could be requisitioned.

What broke the plan was not French genius so much as German overreach, and one general's decision to chase glory instead of following orders. General Alexander von Kluck, commanding the German First Army on the exposed western edge of the advance, had been told to wheel west of Paris and screen the capital while the armies to his east did the encircling. Confident that the French and British were a beaten, disorganized rabble, and eager instead to run down the retreating French Fifth Army before it could regroup, Kluck instead angled his army southeast, crossing the Marne on September 3 well east of Paris — closer to Bülow's Second Army but not screening the capital at all. He was unaware that a new French army, the Sixth, had been quietly assembled at Paris under General Joseph Gallieni, the city's sixty-five-year-old military governor, specifically to guard against this exact opening. Kluck had marched his own exposed flank directly across its front.

Gallieni saw it immediately. He convinced a reluctant Joffre — who had spent the retreat sacking generals he judged insufficiently aggressive, thirty-eight of eighty division commanders among them — to halt the retreat and turn the whole left wing back into a counteroffensive. On September 6, some 150,000 men of the French Sixth Army struck Kluck's flank along the Ourcq. Kluck, forced to swing his own exhausted troops back around to meet the threat, tore open a thirty-mile gap between his own army and Bülow's beside it. French and British forces poured into the gap.

For two days the outcome hung in real doubt. Kluck's troops nearly broke Maunoury's exhausted Sixth Army on the Ourcq, and on the night of September 7, with the situation critical, Gallieni commandeered every vehicle he could find in Paris to rush reinforcements to the front — trains, requisitioned trucks, and, memorably, some six hundred Parisian taxicabs, ferrying perhaps three thousand soldiers north through the night in a chaotic, half-improvised operation that French propaganda would later inflate into the whole battle's turning point. Military historians since have mostly agreed the taxis mattered more to French morale than to the actual arithmetic of the battle. What mattered more was the widening gap in the German line, into which the reconstituted French Fifth Army, under a newly appointed and considerably more aggressive commander, and the long-hesitant British Expeditionary Force, finally persuaded to commit by a direct visit from their own war minister, both advanced.

By September 9, with his flank collapsing and his communications badly strained, Moltke — who had directed the entire battle from a headquarters in Luxembourg, never once visiting the front, relying on subordinates and a staff officer sent out to gauge the true state of things — ordered a general German retreat to the Aisne. It was his last order as chief of the German general staff. He is said to have told the Kaiser, in the aftermath, that they had lost the war; whether he used those exact words is disputed, but he wrote to his wife that same week that things had gone badly and that Germany would have to pay for the damage it had done, which amounts to much the same admission with less theater. He suffered what contemporaries described as a nervous collapse and was quietly replaced within days.

The battle cost something on the order of half a million casualties across both sides in barely a week, a figure that would have seemed unthinkable in July and would come to seem almost modest within another year. Paris was saved. The war Germany had planned to win in six weeks would not be won in six weeks, or in six months, or in four years. Both armies, having failed to outflank each other in a series of increasingly desperate maneuvers that autumn — the so-called Race to the Sea — settled instead into fixed lines, dug into the earth, running unbroken from the Swiss border to the North Sea by winter. The war of movement that every general in Europe had trained for his entire career ended within the year's first six weeks of real fighting. What replaced it — trenches, wire, artillery, the machine gun turned from a strikebreaker's curiosity into the war's defining tool — would not move by more than a few miles in either direction for most of the next three years.

Coda: Christmas Truce

By December the two armies that had marched to war in August expecting a decision within weeks were instead facing each other across a few hundred yards of frozen mud, dug into trenches that ran, unbroken, from the Swiss border to the sea. Roughly a million men, by conservative estimate, were already dead. The war that was going to be over by Christmas had, instead, arrived at Christmas exactly where it started, with neither side having moved the line more than a few miles in either direction in two months of digging.

Pope Benedict XV had appealed for a formal Christmas ceasefire that December; the combatant governments ignored him. What happened instead was smaller, unauthorized, and came from the trenches themselves rather than from anyone in a position to order it. On Christmas Eve, German soldiers in some sectors began setting up small fir trees along their parapets — sent from home, some of them personally by the Kaiser — and lighting candles on them, singing carols into the dark. British troops, listening across no-man's-land, answered back in kind: a German "Stille Nacht" met with an English "Silent Night," the same melody sung on both sides of a line neither army had crossed in weeks except to kill each other. Voices called out season's greetings in accented English. By morning, in stretches of the line held loosely enough and by units willing to risk it, soldiers had begun climbing out of their trenches to meet in the open ground between them.

What followed was uneven, local, and entirely unofficial. Men who had spent the previous months trying to kill each other shook hands, traded cigarettes, chocolate, buttons, and badges, and in a few well-documented and many more embellished instances, kicked a football around in the mud. Joint burial parties retrieved and buried the dead who had been lying in no-man's-land since earlier fighting, British and German chaplains sometimes reading services together over graves neither side could claim alone. A French officer conducted midnight Mass fifty meters from German lines with a makeshift altar built from village debris; the Bavarian Catholics across from him, he noted afterward, did not fire a single shot through the whole ceremony. It was, by most accounts, most successful precisely where British troops faced Saxon regiments, regarded by their opposite numbers as more amiable than the Prussians; it barely took hold at all in French sectors, where five months of German occupation had left far less appetite for camaraderie with the men who had done the occupying.

None of it was ordered, and much of it was actively forbidden by commanders on both sides once word reached headquarters. It also did not last. The truce held, in most places, for a day or two at most, and by the new year the guns were firing again along the same line, at the same men who had traded cigarettes days earlier. When Christmas came around again in 1915, army command on both sides made certain nothing like it happened twice — strict orders against fraternization, artillery barrages deliberately scheduled for Christmas Eve in some sectors specifically to prevent any repeat of the quiet. There was no truce on the Eastern Front at all; Russia was still on the Julian calendar and would not mark Christmas for another two weeks.

What the truce proved, in the end, was not that the war could have been avoided — the men who shook hands in no-man's-land on December 25 were, within days, shelling each other's positions again without apparent contradiction — but that the war was not, in fact, inevitable in the way the summer's machinery had made it feel. Nothing about mobilization tables or alliance obligations had compelled anyone to sing across a trench line, or to walk into an open field where snipers could still, technically, have fired. For one day, in a year that had run almost entirely on timetables, treaties, and the schedules of troop trains, a few hundred thousand men on both sides did something no general staff had planned for and no ultimatum had required: they simply decided, briefly and without permission, to stop.
