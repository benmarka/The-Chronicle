WHAT GOES UP...

Covers Stocks, Elections, Mold Themes Optimism, Victory, Apex

Intro: A Pleasing Prospect

The doors opened at two o'clock. Ushers in eighteenth-century livery, with silver epaulettes and red satin breeches, led the delegates into the Salle de l'Horloge at the Quai d'Orsay. Crowds stood along the Seine and on the roofs of taxis to watch them arrive. Over the city, for the first time in history, flew the flag of the German Reich.

The room had hosted this kind of occasion before. In January 1919, the Paris Peace Conference had opened beneath the same gilded clock. The victors had gathered there to settle a war. Nine years later, they returned with the defeated to abolish war itself.

It was August 27, 1928. Eight powerful lamps had been rigged in the hall so that the ceremony could be filmed with sound. The BBC carried it by wireless. Aristide Briand, the French foreign minister, rose and told the room that silence best suited so solemn an occasion. Then he gave a speech. When he finished, the delegates came forward one at a time and signed with a single gold pen. Gustav Stresemann signed for Germany at a quarter to four, and the room applauded. Frank Kellogg signed for the United States. The British Empire signed in pieces: London first, then Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, the Irish Free State, and India, each with its own name on the page. Fifteen signatures in all.

The treaty itself was very short. Its first article condemned recourse to war and renounced it as an instrument of national policy. Its second promised that all disputes, of whatever nature, would be settled only by peaceful means. There was no third article that mattered. The pact had no enforcement mechanism. It imposed no penalties and defined no aggressor. It did not say what war was.

The fine print lived elsewhere, in the diplomatic notes exchanged before the signing. Every party kept the right of self-defense, and every party would decide for itself what self-defense meant. Britain went further. It reminded the other signatories that there were "certain regions of the world" whose protection it considered a matter of self-defense, and it did not say which. In Berlin, a newspaper ran a cartoon of Mars on the telephone, asking Kellogg whether he was serious.

Most people did not laugh. The pact was the most popular diplomatic act of the decade. Church groups had campaigned for it. Women's organizations had petitioned for it. Thanksgiving services were held in Britain the day it was signed. Within a year, dozens more nations would join. The American Senate would ratify it eighty-five to one.

The optimism was not confined to Paris. In America it had a price, and the price kept rising.

In January, the Federal Reserve reported that brokers' loans had reached a record $4.4 billion, up from $3.3 billion a year before. These were loans for buying stock. An investor could put down a fraction of the price and borrow the rest, using the shares themselves as collateral. As long as the shares rose, the arrangement paid for itself. President Coolidge was asked about the figure. He said the increase was natural and no cause for alarm. The next day the market surged.

The Federal Reserve was less sanguine. In February the New York bank raised its discount rate. It raised it again in May, and again in July, to five percent. Money grew more expensive through the year. The market barely noticed. A borrower expecting a large gain in a few months did not much care what the loan cost. By late November, brokers' loans had passed $5.1 billion. In a single week they rose by $176 million.

The man most responsible for the Fed's policy did not see the year out. Benjamin Strong had led the Federal Reserve Bank of New York since its founding in 1914. The board in Washington had loosely defined powers, and Strong had filled the space. He was, in practice, the American central bank. He had been ill for years. On October 16 he died of an abscess, at fifty-five. Wall Street mourned him politely. Some of the market's bulls were still cursing the rate increases his bank had imposed.

On December 4, Coolidge sent his last annual message to Congress. It opened with a sentence he had clearly enjoyed writing: "No Congress of the United States ever assembled, on surveying the state of the Union, has met with a more pleasing prospect than that which appears at the present time." A third of the national debt had been paid. Taxes had been cut, and cut again, and revenue had still grown. Abroad, he wrote, a rule of force was giving way to a rule of reason. He cited the pact.

On the last trading day of the year, the Dow Jones Industrial Average closed at 300. It had begun the year near 200. The number was round, and it was high, and it was still going up.

Part I: The End of Silence

On a set beside the Sacramento River, Buster Keaton stood on a chalk mark and waited for a house to fall on him. The facade was real. It weighed about two tons. In its upper wall was an open attic window, positioned so that it would pass around his body with a few inches to spare. There was no trick photography and no double. If Keaton stood an inch off his mark, the gag would kill him. The wall came down. The window framed him. He did not flinch. He later called it one of the greatest thrills of his career.

The stunt was the centerpiece of Steamboat Bill, Jr., and it was not supposed to be. The film was meant to end in a flood. Then the Mississippi broke its levees in the spring of 1927, and Keaton's producer, Joseph Schenck, would not allow a comedy about rising water. Keaton built a cyclone instead. He spent an extra $25,000 on breakaway streets and wind machines, and he tore an entire town apart on camera. Storefronts peeled away. A hospital wall lifted off and left a patient's bed standing in the open. The town came down around a man who kept trying to stand up.

The film opened in May 1928. Audiences stayed away, and reviewers were puzzled. The New York Times headed its notice "A Gloomy Comedy." When shooting wrapped, Schenck had dissolved Buster Keaton Productions. He steered Keaton toward Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer, where his brother ran the parent company. Keaton signed. He had been an independent filmmaker, with his own writers, his own crew, and final say over his own gags. Now he was an actor under contract. Steamboat Bill, Jr. was the last film he would make on his own terms.

That same spring, in Copenhagen, Carl Theodor Dreyer premiered The Passion of Joan of Arc. Dreyer was Danish, but he had made the film in France, from the transcripts of Joan's trial. He shot it almost entirely in close-up. There were no establishing shots to speak of and almost no sense of the room. There were only faces: the judges, pitted and sweating, and Joan. She was played by Renée Falconetti, a stage actress who never made another film. Antonin Artaud played the one priest who pities her. Dreyer kept his actors without makeup and filmed them under hard light, so every pore registered. The film does not show a trial so much as a pressure.

It reached Paris in October, but not intact. The Archbishop of Paris demanded cuts, and the government censors made more. Dreyer had no say. Six weeks later, on December 6, a fire broke out at the UFA laboratories in Berlin, where the film had been developed. The original negative was destroyed. Dreyer rebuilt the film from outtakes and alternate shots, matching it as closely as he could. The next year that negative burned too. For half a century, his version was believed lost. In 1981, a worker cleaning a closet at a psychiatric hospital outside Oslo found three canisters labeled with the film's title. Inside was Dreyer's original cut, uncensored and complete. No one has been able to explain with certainty how it got there.

Keaton's cyclone and Dreyer's close-ups were two of the most sophisticated things silent film ever did. They arrived as the form was being retired. In July, Warner Bros. released Lights of New York, the first feature in which every line was spoken. It was crude and static, and it made a fortune. By the end of the year, the studios were wiring their soundstages and their theaters as fast as the equipment could be built. A medium that had spent thirty years learning to speak without words was told, all at once, that it no longer had to.

Walt Disney had learned what the studios could do to him months before. Early in 1928 he went to New York to ask for a raise for his cartoon series, Oswald the Lucky Rabbit. His distributor, Charles Mintz, offered him less money instead. Disney then learned that Universal, not Disney, owned Oswald. He also learned that Mintz had quietly hired away nearly all of his animators. Disney went home to Los Angeles without his character and without most of his staff. Only Ub Iwerks stayed.

Together they designed a mouse. The first two Mickey Mouse cartoons were silent, and no distributor wanted them. Disney had seen The Jazz Singer. He decided the third would have sound, recorded after the animation and locked to it frame by frame. It cost just under $5,000. Disney did the voices himself, though there was very little to say. Mostly there were whistles, squeaks, and a cow's teeth played like a xylophone.

He called it Steamboat Willie. The title came from the same place as Keaton's: a popular song from 1910 called "Steamboat Bill," about a riverboat captain racing the Robert E. Lee down the Mississippi. In the song, the captain pushes his boilers too hard, and they explode. Mickey whistles the tune at the wheel in the cartoon's first shot. The cartoon opened on November 18 at the Colony Theatre in New York. The Colony belonged to Universal. It played before a crime picture called Gang War, and it was the part of the program people talked about. Six months after Keaton's steamboat sank at the box office, a mouse took the same river and the same song and made a fortune. One was silent, and one could whistle.

Virginia Woolf was working on a different problem. In October 1927, she had written in her diary about a new idea: a biography beginning in the year 1500 and continuing to the present day. Its subject would be Vita Sackville-West, her lover, with a change from one sex to the other. Woolf wrote it in a rush, as a holiday from the harder work of her serious novels. She called it a joke. Vita was not allowed to read a word until it was published.

Orlando follows a young nobleman from the court of Elizabeth I through three and a half centuries, in which he ages perhaps twenty years. Midway through, in Constantinople, he wakes as a woman and carries on. The book was dressed as a real biography, with a preface, an index, and photographs of Vita in costume as its heroine. It mocked the conventions of biography while using all of them. It was also, as Vita's son later put it, the longest and most charming love letter in literature.

The final chapter arrives in the present. Orlando drives a motorcar through London and shops in a department store. Time, which has moved in centuries, begins to move in minutes. An aeroplane comes down out of the night sky. Her husband leaps from it. A wild goose flies overhead. Then the clock strikes, and the book stops: "the twelfth stroke of midnight, Thursday, the eleventh of October, Nineteen hundred and Twenty Eight." That was the day the book was published.

It had carried its heroine across four hundred years. It could not carry her one minute further.

Part II The Vote

On February 20, 1928, about ten million Japanese men went to the polls. Four years earlier, the electorate had been 3.3 million. The difference was a law passed in 1925 that abolished the tax requirement for voting. Any man of twenty-five or older could now cast a ballot. Women could not. Turnout was eighty percent.

The result was nearly a tie. Prime Minister Tanaka Giichi's Seiyūkai won 217 seats. The opposition Minseitō won 216. The new proletarian parties, campaigning for workers and tenant farmers for the first time before a mass electorate, won a handful of seats. It was not much. It was enough to alarm the government.

The universal suffrage law had not arrived alone. In the same spring of 1925, the Diet had passed the Peace Preservation Law. It made it a crime, punishable by up to ten years in prison, to organize against the kokutai, the national polity centered on the emperor, or against private property. The two laws were designed as a pair. The government would widen the vote and fence in what the vote could be used for.

Three weeks after the election, the fence closed. Before dawn on March 15, police across Japan raided the homes and offices of suspected Communists and their sympathizers. They arrested 1,652 people. The Communist Party had been illegal since its founding, but its members had campaigned openly inside the legal labor parties. Now those parties were dissolved or broken. The novelist Kobayashi Takiji wrote a story about the raids and what happened in the police stations afterward. He called it March 15, 1928.

The Diet would not go further, so the government did. In June, Tanaka's cabinet amended the Peace Preservation Law by emergency imperial decree. The maximum penalty for organizing against the national polity became death. That summer, the Special Higher Police, the political police the public called the Tokkō, were extended to every prefecture in the country. Japan's first mass electorate had voted for four months before the state decided what it was allowed to have meant.

Germany voted on May 20. The center-right coalition of Wilhelm Marx had collapsed in a dispute over religious schools. The campaign turned instead on a warship. In March, the outgoing Reichstag had approved the first installment for Panzerkreuzer A, a new armored cruiser. It had also cut a subsidy for school meals. The Social Democrats ran on a slogan that needed no explanation: Kinderspeisung statt Panzerkreuzer, food for children instead of battleships.

It worked. The SPD won almost thirty percent of the vote and 153 seats, its best result in years. The Communists rose to fifty-four seats. The nationalist right lost a third of its strength. Far down the list, the National Socialists took 2.6 percent and twelve seats. Their leader, an Austrian, was not a German citizen and could not stand. Among the twelve was the party's Berlin organizer, Joseph Goebbels. A few weeks before the vote, he had explained in his newspaper why an anti-parliamentary party wanted seats in parliament. The Nazis would use democracy's own weapons against it, and the state would pay their salaries while they did. He wrote that they were coming not as friends, and not as neutrals, but as enemies, like the wolf into the sheepfold. Twelve wolves in a chamber of 491 did not look like much.

The Social Democrat Hermann Müller became chancellor at the head of a grand coalition stretching from the SPD to the business-friendly People's Party. It was a fragile arrangement, and the cruiser tested it almost at once. In August, the defense minister asked the cabinet to release the money for construction. The SPD ministers, Müller among them, voted yes to keep the coalition alive. The party rank and file were furious. The Communists launched a petition for a referendum to ban the ship, which fell far short. In November, the SPD's own Reichstag deputies introduced a motion to halt construction. When it came to a vote, the chancellor and his Social Democratic ministers voted with their party and against the decision of their own cabinet. The motion failed. The cruiser was built. The party that had won on food for children had paid for the battleship, and then voted against it.

In Britain, the fight had been over who could vote at all. Since 1918, women over thirty who met a property qualification had been allowed to vote. Men could vote at twenty-one. The gap was deliberate. With so many men dead in the war, Parliament had not wanted women to become a majority of the electorate. In March 1928, Stanley Baldwin's Conservative government introduced a bill to end the distinction. Women would vote at twenty-one, on the same terms as men.

Emmeline Pankhurst did not live to see it. She had founded the Women's Social and Political Union in 1903 and led it through window-smashing, arson, prison, and hunger strikes. She had been force-fed. After the war, her politics moved sharply to the right. She spent years abroad warning against Bolshevism, and in 1927 she was adopted as the Conservative candidate for Whitechapel and St George's in the East End. She died on June 14, 1928, at sixty-nine. The bill received royal assent eighteen days later, on the evening of July 2, in a brief ceremony in the House of Lords. It added about five million women to the rolls. For the first time, women would make up a majority of the British electorate.

Millicent Fawcett was there. She was eighty-one. She had led the movement's constitutional wing for decades, patient where Pankhurst had been militant. As a young woman in 1867, she had sat in the gallery of the House of Commons when John Stuart Mill moved the first amendment to give women the vote. It had taken sixty-one years. That evening she spoke about it on the radio.

In the United States, the franchise was not the question in 1928. For most of its citizens, the vote had been won. For Black citizens across the South, it had been taken away decades earlier, by poll taxes, literacy tests, and violence, and no one in power proposed to give it back. The question was who the voters would accept.

The Democrats nominated Al Smith, the governor of New York. Smith had grown up on the Lower East Side and left school at fourteen to work in the Fulton Fish Market. He was a product of Tammany Hall, an opponent of Prohibition, and a Roman Catholic. No major party had ever nominated a Catholic for president. The response was immediate. Ministers preached against him from the pulpit. In Alabama, the state Republican chairman distributed more than two hundred thousand anti-Catholic pamphlets. Rumors spread that the Pope would move to Washington if Smith won. The Ku Klux Klan burned crosses across the country. When Smith traveled to Oklahoma City in September to speak against religious bigotry, crosses burned along his route.

The Republican candidate barely acknowledged him. Herbert Hoover had never held elective office. He was an engineer and a millionaire, a famine-relief administrator in the war, and the Secretary of Commerce who had organized the response to the Mississippi flood. He gave only a handful of formal speeches and never mentioned Smith by name. He did not need to. On November 6, Hoover won forty states and 444 electoral votes to Smith's 87. He carried New York. He broke the Solid South, taking five states of the old Confederacy that had not voted Republican since Reconstruction. Smith held the Deep South and two northern states, Massachusetts and Rhode Island, where Catholic immigrants and their children now voted in large numbers. In the big cities, the Democratic vote surged. Almost no one counted that as a victory.

Hoover's campaign had begun in August, before more than seventy thousand people in the stadium at Stanford, his alma mater. The speech was carried live on radio across the country. He spoke about wages and hours and the spread of electricity and the automobile. Then he made a promise:

"We in America today are nearer to the final triumph over poverty than ever before in the history of any land. The poorhouse is vanishing from among us."

Part III: Completion

On the morning of July 6, the generals of the Nationalist army climbed into the Western Hills outside Beijing to the Temple of Azure Clouds. Sun Yat-sen's body had lain there since 1925, in a coffin awaiting a mausoleum that did not yet exist. Chiang Kai-shek led the ceremony. Yan Xishan, Feng Yuxiang, and Bai Chongxi stood with him, warlords and commanders who had spent much of the decade fighting one another and now stood in a single row. Chiang read an address informing the dead man that the Northern Expedition was complete. Then he broke down. He threw himself before the casket, sobbing. Feng Yuxiang took him by the arm, raised him up, and led him aside.

The expedition had resumed in April. The Nationalists had spent the previous year purging their Communist allies and fighting among themselves. Now they pushed north again, toward the warlord Zhang Zuolin and the internationally recognized government he controlled in Beijing. Their route ran through Shandong. Japan had economic interests and thousands of residents there, and Prime Minister Tanaka sent troops to protect them.

On May 1, Nationalist soldiers entered Jinan, the provincial capital. Two days later, a clash between Chinese and Japanese troops became a battle. That afternoon, Japanese soldiers surrounded the Shandong foreign affairs office. Inside was Cai Gongshi, a diplomat Chiang had appointed only days before to negotiate a Japanese withdrawal. Cai had studied in Tokyo and spoke Japanese. He and most of his staff were killed. Chinese accounts say they were mutilated first. Japanese forces went on to occupy the city. How many Chinese soldiers and civilians died in the week that followed is still disputed. Chinese figures run to six thousand or more. Japan reported its own dead in the dozens.

Chiang did not fight. He judged that his army could not win a war with Japan and that trying would end the expedition. He pulled his troops back, routed them around Jinan, and kept moving north. According to his biographers, he began writing a phrase at the top of his diary each day from that point on: a reminder to avenge the humiliation.

Zhang Zuolin saw that Beijing was lost. On the night of June 3, he boarded his private train for Mukden, his base in Manchuria. The line was guarded by his own troops along its whole length, except at one point. Outside Mukden, the Japanese-run South Manchuria Railway crossed his track on a bridge. At 5:23 the next morning, as his carriage passed beneath it, a bomb exploded. Zhang was mortally wounded. The plot had been organized by Colonel Kōmoto Daisaku of Japan's Kwantung Army, without orders from Tokyo. The officers had hoped the killing would trigger an intervention and deliver Manchuria to Japan. It did not. The army leadership was caught off guard, and the government in Tokyo was appalled. Tanaka promised the young emperor that the guilty would be punished, and then found he could not punish them. The army closed ranks. In Japan the affair was referred to, officially, as "a certain grave incident in Manchuria."

Zhang's son, Zhang Xueliang, inherited his father's armies. He was twenty-seven. He kept his father's death secret for two weeks while he secured his position. Then he began to talk to the Nationalists.

Nationalist troops entered Beijing on June 8 without a fight. The government renamed the city Beiping, "northern peace," and moved the capital south to Nanjing. Beijing, which had been the seat of emperors for five centuries, became a provincial city. In October, Chiang became chairman of the National Government. On December 29, in Mukden, Zhang Xueliang ordered the Nationalist flag raised over Manchuria. For the first time since the fall of the Qing in 1912, China had a single recognized government.

The unity was largely on paper. The warlords who had stood beside Chiang at the temple kept their armies and their provinces. Within weeks of the ceremony, one of the Nationalist commanders, Sun Dianying, used his troops to dynamite open the tombs of the Qing emperors east of Beijing. His men stripped the bodies of the Qianlong Emperor and the Empress Dowager Cixi and carried off the jewels. No one was seriously punished. In Tianjin, the last emperor, Puyi, received the news. He was twenty-two, living under Japanese protection in the city's foreign concession. He never forgave the Republic.

The Communists had not been destroyed either. That spring, in the Jinggang Mountains on the border of Hunan and Jiangxi, two defeated commanders joined forces. Zhu De brought the remnants of a failed uprising. Mao Zedong had a few hundred men and a base among the bandits of the hills. Together they called themselves the Fourth Red Army. They had a few thousand soldiers and almost no weapons. The Chinese Communist Party's leadership could not even meet safely in China. It held its Sixth Congress that summer in a country house outside Moscow.

Moscow was finishing something too. On January 16, agents of the secret police came to Leon Trotsky's apartment to take him into exile. Trotsky had organized the Red Army and stood beside Lenin in 1917. He had been expelled from the party at the end of 1927. Now he was being sent to Alma-Ata, near the Chinese border, about as far from Moscow as the railways reached. He refused to walk. He locked himself in a room with his family, and the agents broke down the door and carried him down the stairs. His son ran through the building shouting to the neighbors to see how Comrade Trotsky was being carried. Natalia Sedova later wrote that the operation had been directed by Bukharin.

The day before, Stalin had left Moscow for Siberia. The cities were short of grain. Peasants had harvested enough, but state prices were low, and they were holding it back. Stalin spent three weeks touring procurement offices across western Siberia. He told local officials to use the criminal code against peasants who hoarded grain and to seize what they would not sell. The tactic was meant as an emergency measure. It worked, and so it was repeated. The bargain that had governed the countryside since 1921, in which the peasants farmed for themselves and sold to the state at the market price, began to break.

Stalin needed an explanation for why the economy was failing. In May, he supplied one. Fifty-three engineers and managers from the coal mines around the town of Shakhty, in the Donbas, went on trial in the Hall of Columns in Moscow, a ballroom of the old nobility with seats for foreign correspondents. They were accused of sabotaging the mines in league with their former owners and foreign powers. The evidence was mostly confession, and many of the defendants retracted theirs or refused to confess at all. Eleven were sentenced to death. Six of the sentences were commuted. Five men were shot. The word for their crime, wrecking, entered Soviet law. Every failure now had a culprit.

Bukharin had backed Stalin against Trotsky. He began to see what he had backed. He argued in the Politburo that the emergency measures were driving the peasants into revolt. In July, he went secretly to see Lev Kamenev, the opposition leader he had helped defeat, and told him that Stalin was a Genghis Khan who would destroy them all. The conversation was leaked. That same summer, the Communist International held its Sixth Congress in Moscow. It declared that capitalism was entering a new period of crisis and revolution. In the coming struggle, it said, the most dangerous enemy of the working class was not the right but the moderate left. The Social Democrats who had won Germany's election in May were redefined as the enemy.

The First Five-Year Plan officially began on October 1, 1928. It called for an industrial transformation without precedent: steel, coal, tractors, power stations, whole new cities. It had no approved text when it began. The targets were still being argued over and would be raised more than once. The party adopted the plan the following spring and dated it from the autumn before. By the time it was law, it had already been running for six months.

Part IV: Of Bullets and Bananas

Calvin Coolidge left the United States only once as president. In January 1928, he took a special train to Key West and crossed to Havana aboard the battleship Texas. Crowds lined the harbor. He had come to open the Sixth International Conference of American States, and he spoke of goodwill, of the absence of large armies in the New World, and of the peace that the republics of the hemisphere had kept among themselves. Then he went home. He was in Cuba for about three days.

The week before he arrived, a column of U.S. Marines had been ambushed on a mountain trail in northern Nicaragua near the village of Quilalí. The survivors held the town and could not get their wounded out. Over three days, a Marine pilot named Christian Schilt landed a biplane on the village's main street, which the defenders had cleared by knocking down walls. The plane had no brakes. Marines ran alongside and grabbed the wings to stop it. Schilt flew in ten times under fire and carried out eighteen wounded men. Coolidge later gave him the Medal of Honor at the White House.

In Havana, the delegates knew about Nicaragua. Many of them also knew about Haiti and the Dominican Republic and Veracruz. The Argentine delegation pressed for a declaration that no state had the right to intervene in the internal affairs of another. The United States sent Charles Evans Hughes, a former secretary of state and future chief justice, to stop it. Hughes argued that when governments collapsed and Americans were killed, the United States was entitled to act, and that such action was not intervention so much as a temporary interposition. The question was shelved. The Argentine delegate resigned.

The man the Marines were chasing was named Augusto César Sandino. He was a mechanic and the illegitimate son of a landowner. He had spent several years in Mexico working for an American oil company in Tampico, where he absorbed the politics of the Mexican Revolution. In 1927, the United States had brokered an end to Nicaragua's civil war. The Liberal general José María Moncada had accepted the terms: disarm now, and the Marines would supervise a fair election in 1928. Sandino, one of Moncada's commanders, refused. He took a few hundred men into the mountains along the Honduran border and declared war on the occupation.

The Marines brought aircraft against him. They found his mountain headquarters, El Chipote, and stormed it. Inside they found straw dummies wearing hats. Sandino had left weeks earlier. In February, the American journalist Carleton Beals, writing for The Nation, crossed into rebel territory and interviewed him. Sandino said he would fight until the last Marine left Nicaragua. Beals's articles made him famous across Latin America and in parts of the United States. In Washington, he was described as a bandit.

The election was held on November 4. It was supervised by an American general, Frank McCoy. Marines served as the chairmen of local polling boards, at the request of both Nicaraguan parties, who trusted the Marines more than each other. By most accounts it was the cleanest election the country had held. Moncada won. Sandino called it a farce staged by an occupying army and kept fighting. The Marines stayed.

In Colombia, there were no Marines. There did not need to be. Along the Caribbean coast near Santa Marta, the United Fruit Company dominated a banana zone of plantations, railways, and company stores. The workers who cut the fruit were mostly not, in the company's view, its employees. They were hired through subcontractors. That meant the company owed them nothing under Colombian labor law. They were often paid in scrip redeemable only at company commissaries.

In October 1928, the workers presented nine demands. They wanted to be hired directly. They wanted accident compensation, sanitary housing, a six-day week, weekly pay in cash, and hospitals. The company refused to negotiate. On November 12, the workers went on strike. The conservative government in Bogotá treated the strike as a communist uprising. American diplomats cabled Washington in much the same terms. The government sent about seven hundred soldiers under General Carlos Cortés Vargas. They were drawn from outside the region, because their commander did not trust local men to fire on their neighbors.

On December 5, word spread that the governor would come to Ciénaga to settle the dispute. Workers and their families gathered in the square by the railway station. Many stayed through the night. Shortly before midnight, Cortés Vargas received a decree declaring a state of siege in the banana zone. At about one-thirty in the morning, his troops formed up facing the crowd. An officer read the decree. The crowd was told to disperse. Then the soldiers opened fire.

No one knows how many people died. The Colombian press initially reported nine. Cortés Vargas later accepted responsibility for forty-seven. The American embassy, a few weeks afterward, relayed a report from the company itself that the number killed had exceeded a thousand. Oral testimony gathered later put it higher still. In the town of Aracataca, inside the banana zone, there was a boy who had been born that March. His name was Gabriel García Márquez.

Mexico had spent most of the decade proving that it could not be handled this way. The revolution had produced a constitution that claimed the nation's subsoil, and with it the oil that American companies were pumping out of the Gulf coast. For years the two governments had circled each other. Then, in late 1927, Coolidge sent a new ambassador, the banker Dwight Morrow. Morrow ate breakfast with President Plutarco Elías Calles and talked to him as an equal. Within months, the oil dispute had been settled. In his December message to Congress, Coolidge would call relations with Mexico more satisfactory than at any time since the revolution.

Inside Mexico, the revolution was eating itself. Its slogan had been Sufragio efectivo, no reelección: effective suffrage, no re-election. Álvaro Obregón, the general who had won the revolution's decisive battles and lost his right arm doing it, had served as president from 1920 to 1924. He wanted the office back. Congress amended the constitution to allow it. Obregón's two rivals for the 1928 election were both dead by the end of 1927, killed after being accused of plotting rebellions. On July 1, Obregón was elected without opposition.

The country was also at war with its own church. Calles had enforced the constitution's anticlerical articles with unusual severity. The bishops had answered in 1926 by suspending public worship across the country. Rebellion followed. The insurgents called themselves Cristeros, after their battle cry, ¡Viva Cristo Rey! They fought the federal army across the central and western states. Priests were executed and churches burned. Federal troops hanged rebels from telegraph poles along the railway lines.

On July 17, two weeks after the election, Obregón attended a banquet in his honor at La Bombilla, a restaurant in San Ángel, south of Mexico City. A young man with a sketchbook moved among the tables, drawing caricatures of the guests. His name was José de León Toral. He was a devout Catholic and a drawing teacher. He showed Obregón a sketch. Obregón said it was a good likeness and told him to go on. Toral drew a pistol and shot him in the face.

Toral was tortured and tried. He said he had acted alone, for God. The prosecution named a nun, Concepción Acevedo de la Llata, known as Madre Conchita, as the intellectual author. She was sentenced to twenty years in the penal colony of the Islas Marías. Toral was sentenced to death. Many of Obregón's followers suspected someone else entirely. The autopsy reportedly found more wounds, of more than one caliber, than a single pistol could explain. The question has never been settled.

Obregón had been the dominant man in Mexico. Now the country had a lame-duck president, a war in the countryside, and a dead president-elect whose partisans believed the outgoing government had killed him. On September 1, Calles went before Congress to deliver his final annual report. He announced that he would not seek to stay in office, now or ever. He said the death of Obregón offered the country a chance to pass, once and for all, from the historical condition of "a country of one man" to that of "a nation of institutions and laws."

On December 1, Congress installed Emilio Portes Gil as interim president. That same week, a committee began work on a new political party, meant to gather the revolution's quarreling generals and regional bosses into a single organization. Calles was its chairman. The party would be called the National Revolutionary Party, and it would rule for the rest of the century.

Coda: Incidents of Nature

Alexander Fleming spent August with his family in Suffolk. While he was away, he was appointed professor of bacteriology at St Mary's Hospital in Paddington. He came back to his laboratory on September 3. It was a small room, and he was not a tidy man. Before leaving, he had set out a stack of culture plates seeded with Staphylococcus, the bacterium behind boils, abscesses, and blood poisoning. He had meant to study how its colonies varied. Some of the plates had been left out of the incubator.

A former assistant, Merlin Pryce, stopped by to see him. Fleming was sorting through the plates as they talked, discarding the spoiled ones into a tray of disinfectant. One of them had a blot of blue-green mould near its edge. That was not unusual. Moulds got into everything. But around this one, the staphylococcus colonies had stopped growing. Nearer the mould, they had become pale and transparent, as if they were dissolving. Farther away, they were normal. Fleming looked at it for a while. "That's funny," he said.

No one is sure how the mould got there. The story later told, of a spore blowing in through an open window, is probably not true. The window was rarely opened. One floor below, a young mycologist named Charles La Touche was growing fungi to study as a cause of asthma, and his laboratory's spores most likely drifted up the stairwell. The timing also mattered. A spell of cool weather that summer let the mould grow before the bacteria did. A week of warmth would have reversed the order, and the plate would have looked like every other.

Fleming photographed the plate and preserved it. He grew the mould in broth and found that the liquid killed staphylococcus, streptococcus, and the bacteria behind diphtheria and pneumonia. It did not seem to harm white blood cells. For months he called it "mould juice." The following March, he gave it a name: penicillin.

He could not make it into anything. The substance was unstable and hard to concentrate. Fleming was not a chemist and said so. His two young assistants tried to purify it and failed. He published a paper the next year in the British Journal of Experimental Pathology, describing the mould as useful mainly for isolating other bacteria in the laboratory. He mentioned in passing that it might be used as an antiseptic. Few people read it. The work on penicillin at St Mary's ended in 1929. It would be more than a decade before anyone injected it into a patient.

Thirteen days after Fleming came back to his laboratory, a hurricane came ashore near the Jupiter lighthouse in Palm Beach County, Florida. It had already killed hundreds in Guadeloupe and Puerto Rico. On the coast, people had been warned, and most took shelter. The storm moved inland, toward Lake Okeechobee.

The southern shore of the lake was farm country, drained swamp turned to black soil and planted with beans and sugarcane. The towns there were small: Belle Glade, Pahokee, South Bay, Chosen. The people who worked the fields were mostly Black, many of them migrants from the Bahamas and the American South, living in shacks near the water. Between them and the lake stood a dike of packed earth, a few feet high.

On the night of September 16, the wind drove the lake south. The water rose over the dike and then broke through it. It came across the fields in a wall several feet deep and spread for miles. People climbed onto roofs and into trees. Many could not get high enough. The water stood on the land for weeks.

No one knows how many died. The official figure at the time was under two thousand. It is now put at about 2,500, and some estimates run higher. Many of the dead were never identified. Many were never found.

The bodies that were found were sorted by race. In the heat, they decomposed quickly, and the authorities were afraid of disease. Some were stacked by the roadside, soaked in fuel oil, and burned. About sixteen hundred were buried in a single mass grave at Port Mayaca, on the lake's eastern shore. Trucks carried others east to West Palm Beach. The first arrivals went to Woodlawn Cemetery and were buried in wooden coffins. When it became clear how many more were coming, and how many of them were Black, the trucks were redirected. At the city's pauper's field, at Tamarind Avenue and 25th Street, a steam shovel dug a trench. Black men were rounded up to help with the burial. Six hundred and seventy-four bodies went into it.

On September 30, West Palm Beach held two memorial services for the dead, one white and one Black, at the same hour, in different places. At Woodlawn, sixty-nine victims lay beneath a marble headstone. At Tamarind Avenue, there was no marker.
