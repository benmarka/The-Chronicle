Intro: The World Drifts

On the evening of January 6th, a thirty-one-year-old meteorologist named Alfred Wegener stood up in front of the Geologische Vereinigung in Frankfurt and told the room that the continents were not where they had always been.

The Vereinigung was not the establishment. It was a younger, rival body to the official German Geological Society, holding only its second annual meeting, and that may be part of why Wegener chose it as the place to say something this unwelcome out loud. He was not, by training, a geologist at all. He held a doctorate in astronomy, a reputation as a balloonist and polar explorer, and a habit of reading far outside his own field that his colleagues found slightly undisciplined. The idea had come to him almost by accident, paging through a library one afternoon and stopping on a paper that listed fossils found on opposite shores of the Atlantic — the same plants, the same ancient creatures, turning up in Brazil and in West Africa as though the ocean between them had never been there at all. He was not even the first to notice the shape of the thing. A Flemish cartographer named Abraham Ortelius had looked at a map three centuries earlier and seen how neatly the coast of Brazil might tuck into the coast of Africa, if someone were to push them together, and had guessed at earthquakes and floods to explain it. An American geologist named Frank Taylor had floated something close to Wegener's own idea in a lecture four years before. What Wegener had, that they hadn't, was the patience to go looking for confirmation in every direction at once, and the nerve to stand in front of a room and call it a theory rather than a curiosity.

The evidence he laid out that night was not a single clean proof but an accumulation, the kind that convinces by sheer weight. The Appalachian Mountains of eastern North America, he showed, shared rock types and structure with the Scottish Highlands across the ocean, as though the range had been torn down the middle and one half dragged west. Coal seams sat under Antarctic ice, the unmistakable residue of swamp forests that could not have grown anywhere near a pole, suggesting the southern continent had once sat somewhere warmer. Glacial scars turned up in places too hot now for glaciers to have ever reached, unless the land itself had since moved. None of it was new data. Geologists had been cataloguing these oddities for decades, filing each one as a local mystery, a loose thread to be tied off later with some undiscovered land bridge or some unexplained climate swing. Wegener's contribution was to refuse the local explanation and propose instead that all the threads led back to the same fact: the continents had once been fused into a single landmass, and had since drifted apart, and were drifting still, at a pace too slow for any person to feel in a lifetime, or in a hundred lifetimes.

This was not a minor amendment to the accepted picture. The standing theory held that ancient land bridges had once linked the continents and had since sunk beneath the waves — an explanation that asked geologists to imagine entire landmasses vanishing into the deep, which was strange enough, but which at least left the continents themselves alone, fixed in place since the earth had cooled and hardened. The other respectable view was simpler still: that the planet had been shrinking as it cooled, like a piece of fruit drying out, and that mountains were nothing more than the rind wrinkling under the strain. Either way, the continents stayed put. Wegener was asking for something stranger: not that bridges had vanished, but that the continents had never stopped moving, and that the mountain ranges scattered across the world were not ancient and permanent but were, in fact, wrinkles of a different kind — the visible bruising left where one drifting mass had shouldered into another and crumpled at the seam. He could point to the where and the what. He could not yet say why. He had no mechanism, no force he could name that was strong enough to drag a continent sideways through solid rock, and he knew it was the hole in the argument that mattered most. He offered guesses — the pull of the earth's rotation, the slow precession of its axis — and did not pretend they were more than guesses. A claim this large, resting on a force nobody could measure, was exactly the kind of thing a roomful of careful men had been trained to distrust, and they were not wrong to distrust it. It would be decades before anyone found an answer he hadn't had.

Four nights later he gave nearly the same lecture again, to a different society in Marburg.

The Frankfurt minutes recorded only one line about what happened after he stopped talking. There was no discussion, due to the advanced hour.

Part I: Unsinkable

The ship was the largest moving object human beings had ever built. Eight hundred and eighty feet of steel, eleven storeys high, with a swimming pool and a squash court and a Turkish bath, dining rooms got up to look like the interiors of grand hotels, a grand staircase descending under a glass dome. The White Star Line had not, in its advertising, quite called the Titanic unsinkable — that flourish belonged to the public and the press, who took it up and made it a slogan. What the builders had actually said was more careful: that the ship was practically unsinkable. The qualifier mattered, and would be forgotten almost immediately.

The confidence rested on a single feature. The hull was divided into sixteen watertight compartments, sealed off by bulkheads and doors that could be closed from the bridge with the throw of a switch. The reasoning was that even if the sea breached the hull, the flooding would be trapped in one compartment, or two, or three, and the ship would ride lower but stay up. She could lose any four of them and still float. No ordinary accident could fill more than that. The design did not account for an accident that opened the first five at once, and it carried a flaw that no one seems to have thought through: the bulkheads did not run all the way up. They stopped at a deck a little above the waterline, high enough in a level ship, but no higher. Whether this was understood as a danger before the night it became one is unclear. It would not have mattered if the ship had stayed level.

She sailed from Southampton on the tenth of April and spent four days crossing toward New York at a steady twenty-two knots, a brisk speed for the conditions, which the captain saw no reason to reduce. By the evening of the fourteenth she was in waters known to carry ice, and the warnings had been coming in all day over the wireless — seven of them, from ships scattered across the same stretch of ocean, reporting bergs and pack ice and fields of it directly in the lane. The wireless was itself one of the marvels of the age, the thing that made the modern liner feel safe in a way no ship had ever been: a vessel in distress was no longer alone on the water but connected, able to call for help across a hundred miles of dark. On the Titanic the apparatus was run by two young Marconi operators, and on the night in question they were not primarily listening for ice. They were clearing a backlog of passengers' personal telegrams to the relay station at Cape Race — messages about dinner plans and safe arrivals and business in New York, the small vanities of people who could afford to wire from the middle of the Atlantic, piled up because the set had broken the day before and been repaired.

At a quarter to ten the steamer Mesaba sent a warning so specific it was almost a map: heavy pack ice and a great number of large bergs, with the position. The senior operator, Jack Phillips, took it down and acknowledged it and, it appears, went back to his backlog. The message had not been marked with the prefix that would have obliged him to carry it to the bridge, and so, as far as anyone can reconstruct, it never left the wireless room. An hour later the nearby Californian, stopped for the night because she was surrounded by ice, tried to break in with a warning of her own. Phillips, his ear full of Cape Race, cut the man off — told him, more or less, to shut up, that the channel was busy. The Californian's operator, thus dismissed, switched off his set and went to bed. She was close enough to have helped. For the rest of the night she sat dark and silent on the horizon while the larger ship died in plain sight of her.

At twenty minutes to midnight the lookout saw the berg, rang the bell three times, and telephoned the bridge. The officer of the watch ordered the wheel hard over and the engines reversed, and for a moment it seemed the ship might slide clear. She very nearly did. The visible part of the iceberg passed down the starboard side without much more than a scrape. But the bulk of a berg is underwater, and the part no one could see ran along the hull below the waterline and sprung the steel — not a gash so much as a long, intermittent series of buckled plates and popped rivets, opening the sea into the first five compartments at once. One more than the ship could survive.

Now the flaw in the design did its work. The forward compartments filled, and the bow settled, and as the bow settled the water in the flooded compartments rose to the tops of the bulkheads and simply poured over into the next compartment back, and then the next, the way water moves down an ice-cube tray tilted at one end. Each filled section pulled the bow lower, which spilled the sea into the section behind it, which pulled the bow lower still. The very thing that was supposed to save the ship now guaranteed she would go down by the head, methodically, compartment after compartment, with the mathematical certainty of a sum being worked out. The crew did the sum quickly. They had something like two hours.

What they did not have was anywhere near enough lifeboats. The ship carried twenty, with room for about eleven hundred people, against more than two thousand two hundred aboard — and this was not an oversight or a cost-cutting measure so much as a reflection of the regulations, which set lifeboat requirements by the tonnage of a ship rather than by the number of souls on it, and which had been written for a much smaller class of vessel and never updated. By the letter of the law the Titanic carried more boats than she was required to. The law had simply never imagined a ship this size, or had imagined that a ship this size would not need them, the boats being there to ferry passengers to a rescuer rather than to hold everyone at once. And then, having too few boats, the crew launched many of them half-empty. They were untested at this, there had been no proper drill, and in the confusion and the cold and the disbelief — the deck still lit, the band still playing, the danger still abstract to people who had been told the ship could not sink — boats built for sixty-five were lowered with twenty-eight aboard, with thirty, pulling away from the ship with empty seats while hundreds who would shortly be in the water stood watching them go.

The order was women and children first, and on the whole it was followed, but it was followed along the lines that already organized the ship. The first-class staterooms were near the boat deck; the third-class quarters were deep in the hull, behind a maze of corridors and stairwells and, in some places, gates — gates that were locked, or guarded by crew under standing orders to keep steerage below until the better passengers were away. Some of those gates stayed shut long enough to matter. When the counting was done, about six in ten first-class passengers had survived, and about a quarter of those in third class. Of the children in first and second class nearly all lived; of the children in third class, most died. The numbers were not the result of any single cruel decision. They were the result of the ordinary architecture of the ship, the everyday arrangement of who was berthed where and which doors stood between them and the open air, asserting itself under water the same way it had above it.

The wireless, having helped doom the ship, now did the one thing it could. Phillips stayed at his post and sent the distress call — first the old signal, CQD, and then, at his junior's suggestion, the newer code that was just coming into use and might as well be tried, since it could be the last chance anyone had to use it: SOS. The Carpathia, fifty-eight miles off, heard it and turned and ran north through the same ice field at a speed her captain knew was reckless, lookouts doubled, dodging bergs in the dark, and arrived an hour and a half after the Titanic was gone. She found a scatter of lifeboats on a flat calm sea and pulled some seven hundred people out of them. Almost everyone who had gone into the water itself was already dead. The sea that night was a few degrees below freezing, and it killed in minutes, so that the great mass of the fifteen hundred who did not get a seat did not drown so much as freeze, all at once, within sight and sound of the boats, and then fall quiet. The survivors in the boats remembered the silence that followed the noise as the worst part, and the most surprising — the abruptness with which a thing that could not happen finished happening, and the ocean closed over it, and went back to being calm.

Part II: The Edges of the Map

By 1912 the blank spaces were nearly gone. The South Pole had been reached the previous December, the North two years before that, and the news of Scott's death on the way back from the bottom of the world was still arriving over the wires this very year, a final installment of heroic geography. The interior of Africa had been surveyed and partitioned at a conference table in Berlin. A man in the middle of the Atlantic could now send a telegram about his dinner. The map, which for all of human history had trailed off into rumor and sea-monsters at its edges, had been filled in to the corners, and a certain kind of person found this unbearable — not the danger of the unknown, which had always been romanticized at a safe distance, but its disappearance. If there were no more frontiers to be found, they would have to be made. In 1912 three of them were, in three different directions, by three men who had run out of world.

The first was a failure named Edgar Rice Burroughs. He was thirty-six and had been, in roughly this order, a cavalryman chasing Apaches across Arizona, a gold miner, a cowboy, a railroad policeman, and a salesman of pencil sharpeners, succeeding at none of it, until he reached a point where he was pawning his wife's jewelry to buy coal and waiting in a rented office for agents who never sold any sharpeners to come back and tell him so. In the waiting he started writing the kind of pulp adventure he had been reading, on the theory that he could hardly do worse than the authors who were getting paid for it. The story he produced that year, which ran in October in a magazine called The All-Story, concerned an English aristocrat's infant son, orphaned on the West African coast and raised by apes, who grows up to become the lord of the jungle and, on first contact with civilization, reveals himself to be naturally noble, naturally intelligent, naturally superior to every other human being in the book. Burroughs had never been to Africa. He never would go. His jungle was assembled entirely out of other men's adventure stories and the cheap maps of a continent he had never seen, which is part of why it reads less like a place than like a wish — a frontier with all the danger and none of the inconvenient particulars, a wilderness conveniently emptied of any African with a claim to it.

It would be a mistake to treat Tarzan as harmless escapism, because Burroughs did not. He believed, in his nonfiction as much as his novels, in the heritability of human worth, in breeding as destiny, in the natural ranking of races and classes — and Tarzan is the thesis in adventure-story form. The character is not strong and clever because the jungle made him so; he is strong and clever because he was born a Greystoke, an English nobleman, and the blood will tell no matter who raises the boy or where. The apes cannot make him an ape. The Africans, in the book, are scenery to be frightened of. What made the story irresistible to its readers was the fantasy of a man perfectly free, swinging above a world with no rules and no fences — and what made it of its moment was that the freedom was reserved, by the logic of blood, for exactly the sort of people who already believed the world belonged to them. The empty map was a place to put that belief where it could not be contradicted. The book would go on to sell for the rest of the century.

The second frontier was technological, and the men opening it were also outsiders, though of a different kind. The motion picture was barely old enough to walk, and in America it was effectively owned. Thomas Edison's Motion Picture Patents Company — the Trust, everyone called it — held patents on the cameras and the projectors and even, through an arrangement with Kodak, on the film stock itself, and it used them to collect a toll on every foot of film shot or shown in the country, licensing only a favored handful of producers and siccing its lawyers and its agents on anyone who tried to make pictures without paying. The men who refused to pay were mostly immigrants, many of them Eastern European Jews who had come up through the fur trade and the garment business and the nickelodeons — Carl Laemmle, a former clothing-store manager from a small town in Germany; Adolph Zukor, who had arrived from Hungary with nothing; the men who would later be the Foxes and the Mayers and the Warners. The Trust called them pirates. They called themselves the Independents, and they fought back in two ways. They went to court — Laemmle alone defended something like two hundred and eighty lawsuits — and they went west, to a sleepy district outside Los Angeles called Hollywood, partly for the light and the cheap land and the varied scenery, and partly because it was about as far from Edison's process servers as a person could get and still be in the country, with the Mexican border close enough to bolt for if the agents came.

In 1912 the running fight produced the institutions. Laemmle merged his outfit with half a dozen other independents into the Universal Film Manufacturing Company, incorporated that April — a single firm that would make the films, distribute the films, and own the theaters that showed them, the whole apparatus under one roof, which was the shape the entire industry would eventually take. The same year, Zukor founded the Famous Players Film Company, the seed of what would in a few years become Paramount, on a bet the Trust had been too unimaginative to make: that audiences would sit still for a long film with a real story, a "feature," rather than the ten-minute reels of chases and pratfalls the nickelodeons served up. He had imported a forty-minute French picture of Sarah Bernhardt playing Queen Elizabeth, and when it played that summer to an audience that did not grow restless, the length of the American movie, and the ambition of it, changed for good. Laemmle, for his part, had already invented the other thing the Trust had never bothered with. The Trust kept its actors anonymous, the better to keep them cheap; Laemmle started putting their names on the posters, and discovered he had created something new under the sun, which was the movie star. The frontier these men were opening would, within a decade, be the dream-factory of the entire planet — and it had been founded, in large part, by fugitives running from a patent.

The third frontier ran in the opposite direction from the other two. It led nowhere outward at all. On the evening of the twenty-sixth of June, in the Musikverein in Vienna, Bruno Walter raised his baton over the Vienna Philharmonic and conducted the first performance of Gustav Mahler's Ninth Symphony. Mahler was not there. He had been dead a year, carried off at fifty by a heart he had been told was failing, and he had written the symphony knowing it — composed in the summers after the doctors gave him the diagnosis and after his small daughter died, written by a man working in a cabin in the woods with his own ending in clear view. He never heard a note of it played by an orchestra. It came into the world already posthumous, conducted by the protégé who had stood at his deathbed.

There is a long-running argument about whether Mahler meant the Ninth as a conscious farewell — he had, after all, been making plans, looking forward to tours, working on a Tenth when he died, and a dying man does not always know he is dying. But the music has been heard as a leave-taking from the start, and it is not hard to hear why. The thing seems to be letting go of the world even as it clings to it, swelling up into enormous outbursts of life and then subsiding, again and again, into something quieter and more resigned, until the final movement simply thins out and slows and fades, the orchestra dropping away instrument by instrument until almost nothing is left, a few strings holding a chord that gradually stops being there. It does not resolve so much as evaporate. There was an old superstition among composers about the Ninth — that Beethoven had died after his, and Schubert and Bruckner after theirs, so that to reach a ninth symphony was to reach the edge of something — and the Vienna festival that summer leaned into it without quite meaning to, programming three Ninths in the same week, Beethoven's and Bruckner's and now Mahler's, three composers' last words side by side. Mahler had reached the edge the others had, and gone a step past it: the symphony begins in one key and ends, after ninety minutes, having drifted a half-step down into another, so that the ground it finishes on is not the ground it started from. The audience, the critics afterward agreed, was most moved by the long dissolving close, and most baffled by the violent, lurching, sardonic music in the middle — as though they could accept a farewell but not the thrashing that comes before one. They had come to hear the end of something beautiful. They had not quite expected it to fight.

Part III: From Below

Formal politics, in 1912, belonged to a narrow band of humanity. The franchise, where it existed at all, was hedged by property and color and sex; parliaments answered to the men who owned things; and across most of the world the people who did the actual work of mining and farming and carrying had no lawful way to be heard by the powers that disposed of their lives. This did not mean they were quiet. It meant that when they pushed back, they had to do it from outside the system — in the street, in the hills, in rented rooms in foreign cities — and 1912 happened to be a year in which a great many of them pushed at once, in different keys, with results that ranged from immediate slaughter to nothing visible at all.

The slaughter came first, and farthest from anywhere. In the goldfields along the Lena River in eastern Siberia, two thousand miles from the nearest railway, men worked sixteen-hour days knee-deep in freezing water for a company that paid part of their wages in vouchers redeemable only at its own stores, and that recorded something like seven hundred injuries a year for every thousand men. The company was a profitable one; its shareholders included British financiers, a former finance minister, and the Dowager Empress herself, the Tsar's own mother. That winter the workers were sold rotten horse meat dressed up as beef, and it was the meat, on top of everything else, that finally tipped them into a strike — some six thousand of them, with a written list of demands: an eight-hour day, an end to the fines, decent food. The company refused, brought in troops, and in the small hours of an April morning arrested the strike committee. When a crowd marched to petition for the men's release, soldiers opened fire on them. The counts vary — somewhere between two and three hundred killed, by most reckonings, though the figure would later be inflated for the cause — and when the matter reached the Duma, the Interior Minister disposed of it in a sentence that became notorious: so it had been, he said, and so it would be. He was wrong about that. Strikes in Russia had collapsed after the failed revolution of 1905, falling from fourteen thousand a year to barely two hundred; in the months after the Lena shootings, hundreds of thousands of workers walked out in protest, and the annual figure leapt back into the thousands and kept climbing until the war. The killing was meant to settle the matter. It reopened it. One revolutionary, writing afterward, said the Lena shots had broken the ice of silence, and the river was moving again.

In Mexico the river had never stopped moving. The revolution that had thrown out the old dictator Díaz had put in his place Francisco Madero, an earnest, slight, spiritualist gentleman who had wanted free elections and an honest republic and had largely gotten them, winning the presidency in a clean landslide — and who had assumed, fatally, that this was the thing everyone had been fighting for. It was not. The men who had done the fighting had wanted land, and Madero, who believed in political reform and not in tearing up the structure of property, did not give it to them, and so within a year of his taking office his revolution was eating him from both ends. In the south, Emiliano Zapata kept his peasants under arms across the state of Morelos, holding the country they had seized and refusing every offer of peace that did not begin with the land, because an honest election in the capital fed no one in the villages. In the north, a different kind of rising: Pascual Orozco, a hero of the first revolt who felt cheated of his reward, declared against Madero in March — bankrolled, in part, by wealthy landowners who feared the president might actually get around to reform after all, so that the same man was attacked from the left for doing too little and from the right for threatening to do too much. To save himself, Madero reached for a hard old federal general named Victoriano Huerta and sent him north to crush Orozco, which Huerta did, methodically, all through 1912, winning his battles and his prestige and his command of the army. It was the worst decision Madero ever made. Within months Huerta would turn that army on the man who had summoned him, and have him shot. The reformer spent 1912 arming his own assassin.

The other two pushes that year made no noise at all, and changed the most. On the eighth of January, in a Wesleyan church in Bloemfontein, several hundred men — chiefs in their regalia, ministers, lawyers, teachers, a newspaper editor or two — gathered to found the South African Native National Congress, the organization that would, eleven years later, rename itself the African National Congress. The Union of South Africa had been assembled two years before out of the British colonies and the defeated Boer republics into a single white-ruled state, one that left the African majority off the voter rolls and out of the parliament entirely, so that when laws were written about African land and African labor — and a law was coming, the Natives Land Act, that would shortly confine the black majority to owning a thin fraction of their own country — the people those laws fell upon had no lawful voice in the writing of them. The Congress was the answer to that silence. Its founders were not radicals; they were the educated African elite, mission-schooled, several of them trained at universities in America and England, loyal almost to a fault to the British crown, and their chosen weapon was the respectful petition — a delegation, a memorandum, an appeal to the better nature of an empire they still trusted to have one. The empire would ignore them. The petitions would go unanswered for decades, the deputations sent home, the loyalty in the world war that followed repaid with nothing. The architect of the gathering, a lawyer named Pixley ka Isaka Seme who had taken degrees at Columbia and Oxford, had put the case for it the year before in a single line — we are one people, he said, and these divisions are the cause of all our woes. In the church that day they sang hymns and the thing seemed small and decorous and easily dismissed, which it was, and they set in motion something that would take the better part of a century to arrive and would, when it did, govern the country.

And in the same cold first weeks of the year, in a borrowed hall in Prague, a few dozen Russian exiles held a conference. They were the wreckage of a banned and beaten and endlessly quarreling Marxist party, scattered across Europe, arguing over doctrine in cafés while the movement at home lay in pieces, and the man who had called them together, Vladimir Lenin, had spent years insisting that the only useful party was a small, disciplined, ruthless one, purged of everyone who disagreed with him. At Prague he got it. He packed the room with his own faction, expelled the rivals he called liquidators, and walked out with a central committee and the claim that his Bolsheviks were now the party and everyone else the schismatics. The later telling, much tidied by the men who won, made this the clean and deliberate birth of the Bolshevik Party; the truth was messier, and the gathering looked at the time like one more sectarian splinter among émigrés who could not stop splitting. It was also thoroughly betrayed. The conference was meant to be secret — no one, Lenin had ordered, was to know of it — and the Tsar's secret police knew everything, because one of the seven men elected to the new central committee was their paid informer. A dozen quarrelsome exiles in a rented room, watched by a spy in their own leadership, founding a party most of Europe would have laughed at: it was, by every measure available in January of 1912, a non-event. Five years later it would take Russia.

None of it looked, that year, like what it was. The minister shrugged; the petitions were filed; the émigrés went back to their arguments; Madero won his battles. The eruptions were crushed and the foundings were ignored, and a person reading the newspapers in 1912 could have been forgiven for filing all of it under disorder, the ordinary friction of a world that had always had its malcontents and always would. The ground was moving the whole time. It was simply moving too slowly to feel.

Part IV: The Fault Line

The Ottoman Empire had ruled the Balkans for the better part of five hundred years, but by 1912 that rule had been unraveling for a century. Greece had pulled free around 1830; Serbia, Montenegro, Romania, and Bulgaria peeled away over the decades that followed, until the once-enormous Turkey-in-Europe had been worn down to a southern remnant — Macedonia, Albania, Thrace, Epirus, and the old port of Salonika. These last territories had been Ottoman since before Columbus, and they carried the worn, residual air of things that have outlasted their own age. But they were the leftovers of a long retreat, not the solid ground they resembled, and the only real question left about them was who would take them, and when.

The empire's weakness had been advertised that very year. It was bogged down in a war with Italy over Libya, which had revealed how thin its armies were stretched, and the small Christian kingdoms that had pried themselves loose from Ottoman rule across the nineteenth century — Greece earliest, back around 1830, then Serbia and Montenegro, whose independence the powers ratified at Berlin in 1878, and Bulgaria, which had completed its own break only four years before — took note, and saw an opening. They were not fighting for their own freedom; they had that already. They were fighting to seize what the Ottomans still held, and above all Macedonia, the richest and most tangled piece of the remnant — a patchwork of Greeks, Bulgarians, Serbs, Albanians, Turks, and Jews so thoroughly mixed that each of the neighbors could find, in its villages, enough of its own kind to claim the whole, and each of them did. They were not natural friends; their rival claims overlapped like competing surveys of the same plot of ground, and for years they had been sending guerrilla bands into Macedonia to fight the Turks and one another by turns. But Russia... brokered the squabbling kingdoms into a working alliance, the Balkan League, on the understanding that they would drive the Ottomans out first and quarrel over the division after.

Montenegro, smallest and most eager, declared war on the eighth of October; Serbia, Bulgaria, and Greece delivered an ultimatum impossible to accept and followed with their own declarations on the seventeenth. Between them they put something like three-quarters of a million men into the field, against an Ottoman force less than half that size and badly placed to fight.

What no one expected was how fast it would be over. The great powers, who had spent the previous months trying to talk the League out of a war on the assumption that the Turks would win it, watched the Ottoman position in Europe collapse in a matter of weeks. The Bulgarians drove southeast through Thrace and were soon at the Çatalca lines, the last defensive position before Constantinople itself, close enough that the lights of the capital were said to be visible from the front; they laid siege to the great fortress city of Adrianople. The Serbs smashed the main Ottoman army in Macedonia at Kumanovo, took Skopje, pressed on toward the Adriatic. The Greeks raced the Bulgarians for Salonika and won it by a day. By the time an armistice was signed in December, the empire that had held the Balkans for half a millennium had lost something on the order of four-fifths of its European territory, almost all of it gone in the time it takes a season to change. The map of southeastern Europe that had been stable, in its broad strokes, since the Middle Ages was simply not there anymore.

It was not a clean removal, and it should not be remembered as one. The retreat of Ottoman power was accompanied, on the ground, by the kind of violence that follows when long-mixed populations are suddenly sorted by the front line — villages burned, columns of refugees on winter roads, massacres of Muslim civilians who had lived in these places for centuries and now found themselves on the wrong side of a new border, with parallel cruelties visited on Christians where the lines ran the other way. Hundreds of thousands of people were killed or driven from their homes. A commission sent by the Carnegie Endowment to investigate would come back with a report cataloguing atrocities so methodical that it read less like the byproduct of war than its purpose: the deliberate emptying of land of the wrong kind of people, so that the map and the population would finally agree. The splitting open of the East was a human catastrophe before it was anything else, and the people who endured it did not experience it as a realignment of the European balance of power. They experienced it as the burning of their particular house.

But it was also a realignment of the European balance of power, and this is the part that would not stay in the Balkans. The victory left behind a cluster of newly enlarged, newly confident, heavily armed nation-states where a sprawling, sleepy empire had been — and one of them was Serbia, which came out of the war roughly doubled in size and swollen with the conviction that it was the Piedmont of the South Slavs, destined to gather all the Serbs and Croats and Slovenes of the region into a single state. The trouble was that a great many of those South Slavs lived not under the Turks but under Austria-Hungary, in Bosnia, which Vienna had annexed only four years before. A big, triumphant, Russian-backed Serbia was therefore not a regional curiosity to the Habsburg monarchy; it was an existential threat, a magnet that might pull the empire's own restless Slavs out of it and bring the whole multinational structure down. Vienna spent the war and its aftermath looking for ways to cut Serbia back down — insisting, with German backing, on the creation of an independent Albania for the specific purpose of denying Serbia a coastline, and coming, in the process, to the settled conclusion that the Serbian problem would eventually have to be solved by force.

So the alliances rehearsed their parts. Russia stood behind Serbia and the Slavs; Germany stood behind Austria; the powers convened in London to manage the settlement and managed, this time, to step back from the edge. But the reflexes had been exercised, the lines drawn, the resentments laid down like sediment — Serbia denied its sea, Bulgaria about to be cheated of Macedonia by its own allies in a second war the following year, Austria more frightened of Serbia than ever, the German Kaiser reading the whole affair as proof that a reckoning between Slav and German was coming and could not be much longer postponed. The Ottoman buffer that had kept the great powers from facing each other directly across the Balkans was gone, and where it had stood there was now a single raw seam running through the most heavily nationalized, most bitterly contested ground in Europe, with the empires pressing on it from either side and nothing in between. It had taken five centuries for the old arrangement to look permanent and a single autumn for it to disappear. The fault line it left behind would hold for less than two years. In the summer of 1914 a young man would fire a pistol into it, in a Bosnian city named Sarajevo, and the continents would move.

Coda: By Default

For half a century the Republican Party had been the natural party of American government. It was Lincoln's party, the party that had won the Civil War, and it had held the White House for all but eight years since 1860, while the Democrats carried the stigma of the defeated South and won the presidency exactly twice in that whole stretch, with Grover Cleveland. In the spring of 1912 the Republicans held the presidency still, and there was no structural reason to think they would lose it. They lost it anyway, and they did it to themselves.

The trouble was Theodore Roosevelt, who had been the most popular president in living memory and could not stay retired. He had come to the office after McKinley's assassination, won it in his own right, left it at the height of his powers in 1909 having personally handed it to William Howard Taft to keep his work going, and then sailed off to hunt big game in Africa for the better part of a year. He came back unhappy. Taft, in his view, had been too cautious, too friendly to the conservatives and the courts, too willing to prosecute the very trusts Roosevelt had decided were the acceptable kind. In February he announced he was running, declaring his hat in the ring. He entered the new direct primaries and won most of them, the voters plainly with him — but the primaries did not yet decide much, and the party machinery belonged to Taft, and at a furious convention in Chicago the bosses beat back Roosevelt's delegates and renominated the incumbent on the first ballot. Certain the thing had been stolen, Roosevelt led his people out of the hall, founded a third party across town — the Progressives, soon nicknamed the Bull Moose — and accepted its nomination before a crowd that behaved less like a political convention than a tent revival, singing hymns with his name worked into them, while he told them they stood at Armageddon and battled for the Lord.

He nearly didn't finish the campaign. In October, arriving for a speech in Milwaukee, he was shot in the chest at close range by a man who stepped out of the crowd with a revolver. The bullet went through the steel spectacle case in his breast pocket and the folded fifty pages of the speech behind it, which together robbed it of just enough force; Roosevelt, who had hunted enough animals to know the anatomy, observed that he was not coughing blood, concluded the bullet had missed his lung, and refused to go to the hospital. He went to the auditorium instead and gave the speech with the bullet lodged in him, opening with the remark that it took more than that to kill a bull moose, and talking for the better part of ninety minutes before he let anyone look at the wound. He carried the bullet in his chest for the remaining seven years of his life. It changed nothing about the election. Nothing could have; the outcome had been settled the moment the party split, and all the bravery in the world could not unsplit it.

When the votes were counted, Woodrow Wilson — the cool, professorial governor of New Jersey, lately the president of Princeton, a man one reporter said was like a dead fish to shake hands with, and the first Southern-born candidate elected since before the Civil War — had taken four hundred and thirty-five of the five hundred and thirty-one electoral votes, a landslide by any ordinary measure. And yet he had won barely forty-two percent of the popular vote, a smaller share than any victor in twenty years. Roosevelt finished second. Taft, a sitting president of the United States, carried two states and collected eight electoral votes, the most complete humiliation an incumbent had ever been handed. Set Taft's votes beside Roosevelt's and the two Republicans together had outpolled Wilson by more than a million ballots. He was president not because the country had weighed him against the field and chosen him, but because the party that would otherwise have beaten him had broken in half and spent the autumn running against itself.

It would be a mistake, though, to file the result purely under accident, because the same numbers carried a verdict the accident obscured. Three of every four Americans who voted that November had cast their ballots for a party pledged to remake the country — Wilson's New Freedom, Roosevelt's New Nationalism, and the Socialists under Eugene Debs, who polled the highest total his movement would ever reach. The question abroad in the land was no longer whether the federal government should bring the great corporations to heel and stand behind the people who worked for them, but only how far and by what means. The high tide of American reform and the wreck of the party of Lincoln were the same wave. The country had made up its mind to change. It had simply failed to agree on a man, and so it took the one the fracture left standing.

The Republican supremacy that had looked, a few months earlier, as solid as anything in American public life was gone by November — pulled down not by its enemies but by its own hand, the ground giving way beneath the oldest fixture in the nation's politics while everyone stood arguing about the men on top of it. A new era had arrived the way most of that year's arrivals did: unannounced, uninvited, not so much chosen as settled quietly into place while the people it would govern were looking somewhere else. The country had not, in any clean sense, decided to elect Woodrow Wilson. It had decided to move, and he was standing where it moved to.
