Intro: The Guns of Chicago

It rained on Herbert Hoover's inauguration. The rain began shortly before the oath on March 4, 1929, and the crowd on the Capitol grounds was packed so tightly that no one could run for cover. William Howard Taft, the former president and now Chief Justice, administered the oath. He got it slightly wrong. Where the Constitution says "preserve, protect and defend," Taft said "preserve, maintain and defend." A schoolgirl listening on the radio noticed. The newsreels confirmed it. They were the first ever to record an inauguration in sound.

By the time Hoover finished speaking, he was soaked. The speech was confident, and most of it concerned law. Prohibition was nine years old, and it was not working. Hoover did not say that. He said instead that the trouble lay in disrespect for law itself. "Our whole system of self-government will crumble," he warned, if officials chose which laws to enforce and citizens chose which to obey. Then he turned to the country's fortunes, and there he had no doubts at all. "In no nation are the fruits of accomplishment more secure," he said. "I have no fears for the future of our country. It is bright with hope."

Two days earlier, Calvin Coolidge had signed the Jones Act, which raised the maximum penalty for selling liquor to five years in prison and a ten-thousand-dollar fine. Eighteen days earlier, seven men had been shot to death in a garage in Chicago.

The garage stood at 2122 North Clark Street, on the North Side. It belonged, on paper, to the S.M.C. Cartage Company. In practice it was a depot for the bootlegging operation of George "Bugs" Moran. On the morning of February 14, a Thursday, a mechanic named John May was working on one of the trucks. He had brought his German shepherd, Highball, and tied him to an axle. Six other men were waiting with him. Five were Moran's: the brothers Frank and Peter Gusenberg, Adam Heyer, Albert Weinshank, and Albert Kachellek, who went by James Clark. The sixth was Reinhardt Schwimmer, an optometrist who liked to spend time around gangsters. Except for May, they were all dressed well. That was the custom among the North Siders.

At about half past ten, a car pulled up outside that looked like a police car. Four men got out. Two wore police uniforms. They came in through the front as if conducting a raid, and the seven men did what men in their position did when the police came. They put their hands up and faced the wall. Then the men in uniform and the men in plain clothes opened fire with two Thompson submachine guns and a shotgun. When it was over, the two men in street clothes walked out of the garage with their hands raised. The two in uniform walked behind them with guns at their backs. To anyone watching on Clark Street, it looked like an arrest.

Neighbors heard the dog before anyone went in. Highball was howling. Inside, six men were dead. Frank Gusenberg was still alive, with fourteen bullets in him. A police sergeant who had grown up in the same neighborhood knelt and asked him who had done it. "Nobody shot me," Gusenberg said. He died about three hours later without saying anything more.

Moran was not there. He had been running late that morning, and as he approached the garage he saw what looked like a police car outside. He kept walking.

The killing was the culmination of a war that had run for most of the decade. When Prohibition took effect in 1920, it turned beer and whiskey from goods into contraband. That made them enormously valuable. Chicago's trade was split, roughly, between the South Side organization of Johnny Torrio and his lieutenant Al Capone, and the North Side gang led by Dean O'Banion. O'Banion kept his headquarters above a flower shop on State Street, directly across from Holy Name Cathedral, where he had sung in the choir as a boy. He enjoyed arranging flowers himself, and his shop supplied the wreaths for gangland funerals. On November 10, 1924, three men came in to shake his hand and shot him among the chrysanthemums. Two of those reported to have been there were Torrio's gunmen John Scalise and Albert Anselmi.

After that, the war followed its own logic. The North Siders ambushed Torrio outside his home in January 1925. He survived, served a short jail sentence, handed the business to Capone, and left for Italy. O'Banion's successor, Hymie Weiss, was machine-gunned in October 1926 on the steps outside the cathedral, across the street from the shop where O'Banion had died. His successor, Vincent Drucci, was shot by a Chicago detective in April 1927. That left Moran. The bodies through those years ran into the hundreds. Few of the killings were ever solved.

The city's government did not so much fail to stop this as take part in it. William Hale Thompson won back the mayor's office in 1927 promising a "wide open" town, and Capone backed him. In the six months before the Illinois Republican primary of April 1928, more than sixty bombs went off in Chicago, and at least two politicians were killed. The press called it the Pineapple Primary, after the slang for a hand grenade. In January 1929, the head of the Sicilian fraternal society Capone controlled was shot in his own living room. The killing was attributed to Moran. Three weeks later came the garage on Clark Street.

Capone was in Florida that morning, at his estate on Palm Island in Miami. No one was ever charged with the massacre.

When the coroner's jury convened, its first question was whether the killers had actually been police. In Chicago that was a reasonable thing to ask. The jury brought in Calvin Goddard, a former army officer who had made himself an expert in matching bullets to the guns that fired them. Goddard test-fired the Thompsons belonging to the Chicago police and found that none of them matched. Two of the businessmen on the jury were impressed enough to pay for a laboratory, the first independent crime lab in the country. They deliberately kept it at arm's length from the city's police.

The year that followed was not good for Capone. In May, the bodies of Scalise, Anselmi, and a third man, Joseph Giunta, were found beaten and shot on a country road near Hammond, Indiana. Later legend said Capone had killed them himself, with a baseball bat, at a banquet in their honor. Nothing about that has ever been proven. Days later Capone was in Atlantic City, at the first national gathering of the country's crime bosses. On his way home, he was arrested outside a movie theater in Philadelphia for carrying a concealed pistol. He was sentenced to a year. He served it at Eastern State Penitentiary, in a cell that newspapers reported was furnished with rugs, lamps, and a radio. Some suspected he had arranged the arrest himself, to get out of Chicago for a while. That has never been proven either.

In December, a man named Fred Burke shot a police officer during a traffic stop in St. Joseph, Michigan. Police searched his house and found two Thompson submachine guns in a closet. They were sent to Goddard's new laboratory. On December 21, he matched them to the bullets taken from the garage on Clark Street. Burke would spend the rest of his life in prison for the officer's murder. No one was ever tried for the seven.

Part I: The Unraveling

The war had been over for more than ten years before anyone could sell a book about it. Through most of the 1920s, publishers believed readers wanted to forget. Veterans who had tried to write about the trenches found their manuscripts returned. Then, in 1929, the dam broke. In the space of a single year, the generation that had fought the war produced the books by which the war would be remembered. They arrived just as the world those men had come home to was beginning to come apart.

The first and largest was German. Erich Maria Remarque had been conscripted at eighteen and wounded by shrapnel in Flanders in the summer of 1917. He spent the rest of the war in hospitals. Afterward he drifted: schoolteacher, salesman, advertising copywriter, editor of a glossy sports magazine. He had begun the book in 1917 and reworked it over a decade. In 1927, S. Fischer turned it down on the grounds that no one wanted to read about the war anymore. The Ullstein house took it, serialized it in its newspaper, the Vossische Zeitung, through November and December of 1928, and brought it out as a book at the end of January 1929. It became the fastest-selling book anyone in German publishing could remember. Within a year and a half it had sold millions of copies in more than twenty languages. In English it was called All Quiet on the Western Front.

The novel is narrated by Paul Bäumer, a schoolboy who enlists with his classmates after their teacher lectures them on patriotism. One by one the classmates die. Paul is left among older men, a peasant, a locksmith, a scavenger of genius named Kat, who teach him to survive. They do not teach him to believe in anything. The book has almost no politics in it. It has mud, rats, gas, hunger, and the slow discovery that the old men at home have nothing to say that is true. When Paul goes home on leave, he cannot speak to his mother. In the last pages he is killed, in October 1918, on a day so quiet that the army's report records nothing but that there was nothing new to report.

That was the source of the book's power, and of the fury it provoked. It refused heroism without offering any cause in its place. German nationalists called it a slander on the army and attacked Remarque's own war record as too thin to qualify him. The left complained that it blamed no one. Readers bought it anyway, in numbers no one had imagined. The armistice had ended the fighting, but for a decade it had left the meaning of the fighting unresolved. Remarque did not resolve it. He simply said that there was none.

In America that spring, Scribner's Magazine began serializing a new novel by Ernest Hemingway. Hemingway had been an ambulance volunteer on the Italian front in 1918. At nineteen, handing out chocolate and cigarettes in a forward trench on the Piave, he was blown up by an Austrian mortar shell. He spent months recovering in a Milan hospital, where he fell in love with an American nurse who later wrote to tell him she was engaged to someone else. A decade later he made the experience into A Farewell to Arms. Its hero, Frederic Henry, is an American lieutenant in the Italian army. He is wounded, falls in love with a British nurse, survives the catastrophic retreat from Caporetto, and deserts. He calls it making a separate peace. He and the nurse, Catherine Barkley, escape across a lake into Switzerland. There, she dies in childbirth.

The book was written in the shadow of other losses. Hemingway's second wife went through a long, difficult labor ending in a caesarean while he was drafting the childbirth scenes. In December 1928, while he was revising, his father shot himself. Hemingway wrote dozens of versions of the ending before settling on the bleakest one. In June, the Boston police chief banned the issue of Scribner's carrying the second installment, calling it salacious. The publicity did the novel no harm. When it appeared as a book in late September, it became Hemingway's first bestseller and made him financially independent for the first time.

The passage that lasted longest was not about the war's violence but about its language. Frederic Henry says he has always been embarrassed by words like sacred, glorious, and sacrifice. He has seen nothing sacred, and nothing glorious. The only words that still mean anything are the names of villages, the numbers of roads and regiments, and dates. The abstractions had been spent. Gertrude Stein had told Hemingway years earlier that his was a lost generation. He had used the line as an epigraph. In 1929 he showed what had been lost. It was not only young men. It was the vocabulary that had sent them.

William Faulkner had wanted badly to fight in that war, and never did. Rejected by the American air service, he talked his way into the Royal Air Force in Canada, and the armistice came before he finished training. He came home to Oxford, Mississippi, in a British officer's uniform, walking with a limp he had not earned and telling stories about crashes that had not happened. Through the 1920s he was the town eccentric. He was forced out as the university's postmaster for reading on the job. He published two novels that sold poorly, then a third, Sartoris, which his publisher cut heavily.

He wrote the next one, he later said, expecting no one to publish it. The Sound and the Fury took its title from Macbeth's description of life as a tale told by an idiot. Its first section is narrated by one. Benjy Compson is thirty-three, cannot speak, and experiences past and present as a single unbroken surface. The second section belongs to his brother Quentin, on the June day in 1910 when he breaks the crystal of his grandfather's watch, walks through Cambridge, Massachusetts, and drowns himself in the Charles. The third is Jason, the brother who stayed, cruel and petty and counting his money. Only the last section, told from outside and centered on the family's Black servant Dilsey, stands back far enough to see the whole ruin. The Compsons had once produced a governor and a general. They had sold their pasture to pay for Quentin's year at Harvard. By the end there is almost nothing left to sell.

Nothing falls suddenly in the book. The family decays across generations, through drink and pride and silence, and the novel's broken structure is that decay made into form. It was published on October 7, 1929. Reviewers who understood it were impressed. Very few people bought it. Faulkner had taken a night job shoveling coal at the university power plant, and that autumn, he said, he wrote his next novel there in six weeks.

Virginia Woolf belonged to an older generation and had watched the war from London. In October 1928 she had given two lectures to the students of Cambridge's women's colleges on the subject of women and fiction. A year later she published them, much expanded, as A Room of One's Own. Its argument was disarmingly plain. To write fiction, a woman needs money and a room with a lock on the door. Genius alone is not enough. Woolf imagined a sister of Shakespeare's, as gifted as he was, barred from school, from the theater, from any life of her own. She dies young and unrecorded. Intellectual freedom, Woolf wrote, depends on material things.

Her narrator credits her own freedom to an inheritance from an aunt: five hundred pounds a year, for life. It mattered more to her than the vote, she said, because it meant she would never again have to flatter a man for a living. The book was witty, and it was also a hard-headed reckoning with what independence costs and who pays for it. It reached the shops that October. By the end of the month, the money that so many people believed would keep coming had begun to disappear.

Part II: The Last Silence

The first Academy Awards were handed out on the evening of May 16, 1929, at a private dinner in the Blossom Room of the Hollywood Roosevelt Hotel. About 270 people came. Tickets cost five dollars, and the meal was a choice of chicken or sole. The presentation took about fifteen minutes. There was no suspense, because the winners had been announced in February. Douglas Fairbanks, the Academy's president and the most famous swashbuckler in the world, handed out the statuettes. Nobody yet called them Oscars.

The Academy had not been founded to give out prizes. Louis B. Mayer, the head of MGM, proposed it in early 1927, weeks after the studios had been forced to sign their first agreement with the craft unions. Mayer wanted an organization that could settle disputes with actors, writers, and directors before they got the same idea. It was, in effect, a company union with a dinner attached. The awards were an afterthought, a way to give the talent something to want that did not involve a contract.

The films it honored were, almost all of them, silent. The award for outstanding picture went to Wings, a war epic about American fighter pilots, the most expensive production of its day and a spectacle of aerial combat that no one had seen before on a screen. A second top prize, for "unique and artistic production," went to F. W. Murnau's Sunrise, a fable of a farmer tempted to drown his wife, made almost without intertitles. The best actor, Emil Jannings, had already collected his statuette and gone home to Germany. Warner Bros. received a special award for The Jazz Singer, the picture that had made audiences hear a voice. It was kept out of the competitive categories on the grounds that comparing it with silent films would be unfair.

That was a polite way of saying that it had already won. By the night of the dinner, every major studio was converting to sound. Theaters across America were being wired as fast as the equipment could be installed. The orchestras and organists who had accompanied silent pictures were being let go. In February, MGM had released The Broadway Melody, a backstage story about two sisters trying to make it on Broadway, advertised as the first all-talking, all-singing, all-dancing picture. It was creaky and enormously profitable, and it would win the Academy's next top prize. Hollywood took the lesson. Over the course of the year, the silent feature all but disappeared from American studio schedules.

The new technology was crude. Cameras had to be shut inside soundproofed booths so the microphones would not pick up their whirring, which froze them in place. Actors clustered around microphones hidden in flower arrangements and telephones. In Britain, Alfred Hitchcock was halfway through shooting Blackmail as a silent picture when his studio decided it should talk. His leading lady, Anny Ondra, was Czech, and her accent was considered wrong for a London shopkeeper's daughter. There was no way yet to add a voice after filming. So an English actress, Joan Barry, stood just out of frame and spoke Ondra's lines into a microphone while Ondra mouthed them on camera. The result was billed as the first British talkie. A silent version was released alongside it for theaters that had not yet been wired.

Careers came apart. The most famous casualty was John Gilbert, MGM's leading romantic star. His first released talkie, His Glorious Night, opened that autumn, and audiences laughed at his love scenes. The legend that grew up afterward said that his voice was high and thin, and that sound alone destroyed him. Contemporary reviews do not bear that out. Most found his voice perfectly adequate. The dialogue was florid, the picture was bad, and Gilbert had a famously poisonous relationship with Mayer. He made more talkies and never recovered his place. The legend persisted because it was simpler than the truth. A technology had arrived, and some people could not make the crossing.

The irony of 1929 was that silent film had never been better. Freed from the need to explain itself in words, it had spent a decade learning to think in images. In the last year before sound swallowed it, the art reached a kind of summit in several countries at once.

In Berlin, in February, G. W. Pabst released Pandora's Box. It starred an American actress named Louise Brooks as Lulu, a woman who destroys every man and woman drawn to her and is finally murdered by Jack the Ripper. Brooks had left Paramount to make the film. German critics disliked it, and it did poorly. Paramount, meanwhile, had decided to convert one of her last American pictures into a talkie and wanted her back to record her lines. She refused. The studio had another actress dub her voice, and her Hollywood career effectively ended. Decades later, the performance would be recognized as one of the greatest in silent film. In 1929, it was a commercial failure by an actress who had declined to speak.

In the Soviet Union, Dziga Vertov released Man with a Movie Camera. It had no story, no actors, and no intertitles. It followed a cameraman, played by Vertov's brother Mikhail Kaufman, through a single composite day in Soviet cities, from dawn to dusk. Workers, trams, factories, a birth, a funeral, a beach, and finally the audience watching the film itself. Vertov's wife, Yelizaveta Svilova, edited it, and she appears on screen at her editing table. Its double exposures, split screens, freeze frames, and accelerated montage were years ahead of anything else being made. Eisenstein dismissed it as "pointless camera hooliganism." Within a few years Vertov's kind of experiment would be unwelcome in Stalin's Soviet Union, and he would spend the rest of his career largely sidelined.

In Paris, on June 6, two young Spaniards nobody had heard of showed a seventeen-minute film at an invitation-only screening at the Studio des Ursulines. Luis Buñuel had directed it with money from his mother. Salvador Dalí had helped him write it in under a week, working from two dreams, one about ants swarming in a hand and one about an eye being cut open. Their only rule was that no image that could be rationally explained would be allowed. The film opened with a man sharpening a razor and slicing a woman's eye. Picasso, Le Corbusier, Cocteau, and the entire Surrealist circle of André Breton were in the audience. Buñuel later said he had filled his pockets with stones to throw at them if the screening went badly. It went well. Breton embraced him, and Un Chien Andalou went on to run for months at a Paris art house.

These were not the films that Hollywood would follow. In the United States, the next years belonged to musicals, gangster pictures, and filmed plays. The visual vocabulary that Vertov, Pabst, and Buñuel had pushed to its limit would take decades to recover. Much of it survived only by being absorbed, piece by piece, into the sound cinema that had replaced it.

The Academy, for its part, gave its award for unique and artistic production exactly once. The following year it was gone, folded into a single prize for the best picture. That one went to The Broadway Melody. The era of the silent film was ending.

Part III: The Edges of Empire

In the autumn of 1928, a British commission arrived in India to study whether the country was ready for more self-government. It had seven members, all of them British. Indians boycotted it everywhere it went, under black flags and the slogan "Simon, go back." In Lahore that October, police broke up a protest with lathis, the long bamboo staves of the Indian constabulary. Among those beaten was Lala Lajpat Rai, one of the senior figures of the national movement. He died a few weeks later. In December, a group of young revolutionaries set out to kill the police superintendent they held responsible. They shot the wrong man, a twenty-one-year-old assistant superintendent named John Saunders. One of the shooters was a Sikh student named Bhagat Singh. He was twenty-one as well.

The year 1929 opened in India with the government moving against the left. On March 20, police carried out coordinated raids in Bombay, Calcutta, and the Punjab and arrested thirty-one trade unionists and communists. Three of them were Englishmen, sent out by the Communist Party of Great Britain to help organize Indian labor. The charge was conspiracy to deprive the King-Emperor of his sovereignty over India. The prisoners were taken to Meerut, a cantonment town far from any of the cities where they had worked, so that there would be no jury. The trial would last until 1933.

Three weeks later, on April 8, the Central Legislative Assembly in Delhi was voting on two bills. One restricted strikes. The other gave the government power to deport agitators without trial. Shortly after midday, Bhagat Singh and a comrade, Batukeshwar Dutt, stood up in the visitors' gallery and threw two small bombs onto empty benches below. No one was killed. The two men showered the chamber with leaflets explaining that it took a loud voice to make the deaf hear, shouted "Inquilab Zindabad," long live revolution, and waited to be arrested. They had planned it that way. A trial would give them a platform, and they used it.

In prison, they went on hunger strike to demand that Indian political prisoners be treated as well as European ones, with books, newspapers, and decent food. In July, Jatindra Nath Das, a young bomb-maker from Bengal awaiting trial in the same conspiracy case, joined them. The authorities tried to force-feed him. He resisted. He died on September 13, 1929, on the sixty-third day of his fast. He was twenty-four. His body was carried by train from Lahore to Calcutta, and at every station along the route crowds came out to meet it.

The government in London understood that something had shifted. On October 31, the viceroy, Lord Irwin, declared that the natural end of India's constitutional progress was dominion status, the self-governing standing of Canada or Australia, and promised a conference in London to discuss it. The statement was hedged and vague. In Britain it was attacked as a surrender anyway, and official spokesmen quickly explained it away. On December 23, the train carrying Irwin back to Delhi was bombed on the approach to the city. He was unhurt. That afternoon he met Gandhi, Motilal Nehru, Muhammad Ali Jinnah, and other leaders, who asked him for one assurance: that the conference would draft a constitution for dominion status. He would not give it.

A year earlier, Congress had issued an ultimatum. If Britain did not grant dominion status by the last day of 1929, it would demand full independence. The deadline now arrived. The party met in Lahore at the end of December, in a tent city on the banks of the Ravi. Gandhi had declined the presidency and put forward Jawaharlal Nehru instead. Nehru was forty, Cambridge-educated, a socialist, and impatient. Gandhi also insisted, over loud objections, that the session pass a resolution congratulating the viceroy on surviving the bomb. It carried, narrowly.

On the last day of the year, Congress adopted a resolution declaring that its goal was no longer self-government within the empire but purna swaraj, complete independence. At midnight, as the ultimatum expired, Nehru raised the tricolor flag of the movement on the banks of the river. Congress asked Indians to mark January 26, 1930, as their independence day. India would not be independent for another seventeen years. Lahore, where the flag went up, would by then be in another country.

Further west, the British held Palestine under a mandate from the League of Nations. The mandate carried a promise, inherited from the wartime Balfour Declaration, to support a national home for the Jewish people there. For years, one of the sharpest points of conflict had been a single stretch of stone in Jerusalem's Old City. Jews call it the Western Wall, the last remnant of the Second Temple's precinct. Muslims call it al-Buraq, part of the Haram al-Sharif, the compound that holds the Dome of the Rock and al-Aqsa. On Yom Kippur in 1928, Jewish worshippers set up a screen to separate men and women at prayer. British police removed it by force. Each side read the incident as proof of the other's designs, and the dispute escalated through the following year.

In mid-August 1929, members of a Zionist youth movement marched to the Wall, raised the Zionist flag, and sang the Zionist anthem. The next day, Muslims held a counter-demonstration there. A Jewish teenager was stabbed in a quarrel and died. Jewish crowds attacked Arab property in reprisal, and rumors spread on both sides. On August 23, violence broke out in Jerusalem and spread across the country. The worst of it came the next day in Hebron, home to one of the oldest Jewish communities in the world, most of it religious and unconnected to the Zionist movement. Some sixty-seven Jews were killed there. Some of the survivors were sheltered by Arab neighbors. There were more killings in Safed a few days later. In Jaffa, Jews killed an Arab family, and a Jewish policeman was later convicted of murder.

When the week was over, 133 Jews were dead, by the official count, and 116 Arabs, most of the latter killed by British police and troops putting the violence down. The Hebron community abandoned the city. British commissions came to investigate, and their reports pleased no one. What happened in those days, how it began, and even what to call it, are contested to this day.

The pressure was building everywhere. In southeastern Nigeria, in November, a census taker working for a British-appointed warrant chief tried to count a widow's household and livestock. Women across the region had heard that they were about to be taxed, as their husbands had been the year before. The British called what followed the Aba Women's Riots. The Igbo called it Ogu Umunwanyi, the Women's War. Tens of thousands of women gathered at the compounds of warrant chiefs and "sat on" them, an old practice of collective shaming through song, dance, and mockery. They burned native courts, freed prisoners, and looted European trading posts. On December 16, at Opobo, troops fired into a crowd and killed thirty-one women and one man. More than fifty women were killed before the unrest was put down. The tax on women was abandoned.

In Korea, on October 30, Japanese students on a commuter train harassed several Korean schoolgirls at Naju station. Korean students fought back, and the police sided with the Japanese. On November 3, students in Gwangju took to the streets. The protests spread from school to school across the country over the next five months, until tens of thousands of students had joined them. In the Dutch East Indies, on December 29, colonial police arrested a young engineer named Sukarno, the founder of the Indonesian Nationalist Party.

None of these empires fell in 1929. In most places, the colonial authorities restored order within weeks. The prisoners went to trial, and the commissions wrote their reports. But the schisms that 1929 revealed would not disappear.

Part IV: The Great Turn

In January 1929, a special train carried Leon Trotsky out of Alma-Ata, the Central Asian town where he had spent a year in internal exile, toward the Black Sea. At Odessa he and his wife and elder son were put aboard a steamer called the Ilyich, after Lenin's patronymic. The only other passengers were agents of the secret police. On February 12, the ship entered the Bosphorus. Trotsky had commanded the Red Army and stood second only to Lenin in the revolution. Now he was a stateless exile in Constantinople, and he would soon move to a borrowed villa on the island of Prinkipo in the Sea of Marmara. He was the last of Stalin's rivals with a stature to match his own. Stalin had chosen to deport him rather than imprison him, and he would come to regret it.

The opposition that remained inside the country was of a different kind. Nikolai Bukharin, the party's leading theorist, editor of Pravda, and head of the Communist International, had been Stalin's closest ally against Trotsky. He believed in the New Economic Policy that Lenin had introduced in 1921, which tolerated small private trade and left the peasants to farm their own land and sell their grain. The revolution, in Bukharin's view, would grow into socialism slowly, by persuasion, at the pace of the peasant cart. With him stood Alexei Rykov, the head of the government, and Mikhail Tomsky, who led the trade unions. In April 1929, at a plenum of the Central Committee, Stalin denounced the three of them as a "Right Deviation." They lost their posts one by one. Tomsky was removed from the unions, and Bukharin from the Comintern and Pravda. In November, the three signed a public statement admitting their errors. Bukharin was expelled from the Politburo anyway, on November 17. He was allowed to remain in the party.

With the Right out of the way, there was no one left to argue for patience. That spring, the party adopted the First Five-Year Plan. It was officially backdated to the previous October and set in the most ambitious of the versions the planners had drafted. Over the following months its targets were raised again, and then again. The slogan became "the five-year plan in four years." On the empty steppe of the southern Urals, ground was broken for Magnitogorsk, a steel city to be built almost from nothing. Workers who exceeded their quotas were celebrated as shock workers. Factories competed with one another in "socialist emulation."

Even time was reorganized. In May, an economist named Yuri Larin argued that the Soviet economy wasted a seventh of its capacity by stopping every Sunday. Machines should never rest. Beginning that autumn, the urban workforce was moved onto a continuous working week, the nepreryvka. The week became five days long. Workers were divided into five groups, each assigned a color and a different rest day, so that the factories never closed. The last Sunday on which most Soviet workers rested together fell on September 29. Within weeks Pravda was publishing letters from workers complaining that they never had a day off at the same time as their wives or children. The new week also made regular churchgoing nearly impossible. That was not an accident. Earlier that year, a new law on religious associations had confined worship to registered congregations and banned religious teaching outside the home.

The plan needed labor in places where no one would go willingly. On June 27, the Politburo approved a resolution on the use of the labor of criminal prisoners. Until then the secret police had run essentially one large camp complex, on the Solovetsky Islands in the White Sea. Now every prisoner sentenced to three years or more would be transferred to camps run by the secret police in remote regions of the country. There they would colonize the land and extract its timber and minerals. The Politburo directed that the camps no longer be called concentration camps. They would be called corrective labor camps. A government decree formalized the system in July. It would later be known by the acronym of the agency that ran it, the Gulag.

The heart of the matter was the countryside, where four of every five Soviet citizens lived. The plan depended on grain. The state needed it to feed the cities and the new industrial workforce, and to sell abroad to pay for imported machinery. The peasants, offered low prices and few goods to buy, had been holding it back since 1927. The regime's answer through 1928 and 1929 was coercion. Officials imposed quotas, searched barns, and prosecuted those who hoarded. In June, villages were made collectively responsible for meeting their quotas, so that peasants would be set against one another. The long-term solution was the collective farm. Peasant holdings would be merged into large state-directed units that could be mechanized, and above all controlled. Through the first half of 1929, only a small fraction of peasant households had joined one.

On November 7, the twelfth anniversary of the revolution, Stalin published an article in Pravda called "The Year of the Great Turn." He claimed that the middle peasants, the broad mass of the village, were now entering the collective farms of their own accord, "by whole villages, whole districts." It was not true, but it became policy. That month, the Central Committee resolved to send twenty-five thousand politically reliable industrial workers into the countryside to lead the drive. Local officials, competing to report the highest rates, began driving whole villages into collectives at gunpoint.

On December 21, the Soviet Union celebrated Stalin's fiftieth birthday. The date was the one he had adopted. By the parish register he had been born a year earlier. Pravda filled its pages with tributes. Organizations across the country sent their greetings. Portraits appeared in public places where, until then, Lenin had stood mostly alone. Historians have generally dated the cult of Stalin's personality from that week.

Six days later, on December 27, Stalin spoke to a conference of Marxist specialists on agrarian questions. The kulaks, the better-off peasants, had until now been squeezed with taxes and quotas. The party, he announced, was moving from a policy of restricting the kulaks to a policy of eliminating them as a class. Their property would be seized and handed to the collective farms. The term "kulak" had never had a precise definition. In practice it would mean anyone who resisted, anyone a neighbor denounced, anyone who owned a second cow. To those who worried about the fate of the dispossessed, Stalin offered an image. "You do not lament the loss of the hair," he said, "of one who has been beheaded."

Part V: The Crash

By the beginning of 1929, owning stock had become an American pastime. The Dow Jones Industrial Average had risen roughly fivefold since the summer of 1921. Shares of the Radio Corporation of America, which had never paid a dividend, had multiplied many times over. Brokerage offices opened in hotel lobbies, in resort towns, and on transatlantic liners. Much of the buying was done on credit. A customer could put down a fraction of a stock's price and borrow the rest from his broker. The broker borrowed in turn from the banks, and increasingly from corporations with idle cash, which found they could earn more lending to speculators than making things. The loans were "call" loans, repayable on demand. As long as prices rose, the arrangement financed itself.

The Federal Reserve was uneasy. In February, it warned its member banks against lending for speculation. It hoped that pressure on the banks would do what it was reluctant to do with interest rates. In late March, the warning bit. Call money rates spiked toward twenty percent, and on March 26 the market broke on record volume. Charles Mitchell, chairman of the National City Bank and a director of the New York Federal Reserve, announced that his bank would lend twenty-five million dollars into the call market, whatever the Board in Washington thought. Rates eased, and the market rallied. Mitchell was praised as the man who had saved Wall Street. The Fed did not try that again.

Through the summer the market rose almost without interruption. New investment trusts, companies whose only business was owning shares in other companies, often shares in each other, came to market by the dozen, and their own shares were bought on margin. In August, the Ladies' Home Journal published an interview with John J. Raskob, a director of General Motors and chairman of the Democratic Party, under the title "Everybody Ought to Be Rich." Fifteen dollars a month, he explained, invested steadily in good common stocks, would grow in twenty years to eighty thousand dollars. The same month, the Federal Reserve Bank of New York finally raised its discount rate.

The real economy had already begun to turn. Industrial production peaked in the early summer and drifted down. Home building had been falling for two years. Automobile sales were slowing. Farm prices, low for most of the decade, were lower still. None of this was dramatic, and almost no one in the market paid attention to it.

On September 3, the Dow closed at 381.17. It would not close that high again for twenty-five years. Two days later, a statistician named Roger Babson told a business conference in Massachusetts that "sooner or later a crash is coming, and it may be terrific." The market dipped on the news and recovered. On September 20, in London, the financial empire of a promoter named Clarence Hatry collapsed when it emerged that he had been raising money on forged securities. The London market fell sharply. The Bank of England raised its rate to protect the pound, and money that had been flowing to New York began to flow back. Through late September and early October, American stocks slid, rallied, and slid again. Irving Fisher, the most celebrated economist in the country, assured an audience in New York on October 15 that stock prices had reached "what looks like a permanently high plateau."

On Wednesday, October 23, the market fell hard in the last hour of trading. Margin calls went out that evening. On the morning of Thursday, October 24, the sell orders were waiting at the opening bell. Prices fell so fast that the ticker, the machine that printed trades on paper tape for brokers across the country, ran more than an hour behind. Investors in Chicago or Boston or Denver had no idea what their shares were worth. Not knowing, they sold. A crowd gathered on Wall Street outside the Exchange. Police reserves were called in.

At noon, the heads of the largest banks in New York met at the offices of J. P. Morgan & Company, across the street from the Exchange. They agreed to pool their resources and buy. Thomas Lamont, the senior Morgan partner, came out to tell reporters that there had been "a little distress selling" on the Exchange. At half past one, Richard Whitney, the Exchange's acting president, walked onto the floor to the post where United States Steel was traded and bid 205 for a block of its shares, well above the last sale. He went on to other leading stocks. The market steadied and closed well off its lows. More than twelve million shares had changed hands, a record. The ticker did not finish printing the day's trades until long after dark.

The next day, President Hoover told the country that its fundamental business, the production and distribution of commodities, was on a sound and prosperous basis. Friday was quiet. Over the weekend, newspapers reported the bankers' intervention, and brokers sent out more margin calls.

On Monday, October 28, the selling resumed. The bankers met again, but no one went out onto the floor. The Dow fell nearly thirteen percent in a single day. On Tuesday, October 29, it fell nearly twelve percent more, on more than sixteen million shares, a volume that would not be surpassed for almost forty years. There were moments when some stocks could find no buyer at any price. Brokers worked through the night to tally the damage. Margin accounts were wiped out by the thousand. Men who had been rich on paper on Monday morning were in debt on Tuesday night.

The legend that grew up afterward filled the streets of lower Manhattan with ruined speculators throwing themselves out of windows. There were suicides, and a few were very public. Winston Churchill, staying at a hotel on Fifth Avenue, later wrote that a man had jumped fifteen stories under his window. But the national suicide rate in October and November was lower than it had been that summer, when the market was booming. The image endured because it gave the event a single, terrible shape. What actually happened was slower and harder to see.

The market did not stop at the end of October. It rallied, then fell again, and touched bottom for the year on November 13, at just under half its September peak. Then it recovered. By the following spring it had regained a substantial part of what it had lost, and many people concluded that the worst was over. Hoover summoned business leaders to the White House and secured their promise not to cut wages. He asked Congress for a tax cut and urged state and local governments to accelerate public works. Industrial production fell five percent in October and five percent more in November. Unemployment, about a million and a half that summer, would double by the following spring.

Historians and economists have argued ever since about what the crash did. Some see it as the cause of the Great Depression. Others see it as a symptom of weaknesses that were already present. There was the slowing economy, a fragile banking system, farm distress, debts left over from the war, and the gold standard. Still others blame the policy failures of the years that followed for turning a hard recession into a catastrophe. What is not in dispute is that in the autumn of 1929, the faith that had sustained the decade broke. The Dow would not reach its bottom until the summer of 1932, nearly ninety percent below its September peak. By then, the world that had believed in a permanent plateau was gone.

Coda: Outlines of Tomorrow

On the morning of February 11, 1929, in heavy rain, a line of cars drew up at the Lateran Palace in Rome. Cardinal Pietro Gasparri, the papal secretary of state, had come to sign a treaty with Benito Mussolini. For fifty-nine years, since Italian troops had broken through the walls of Rome in 1870 and ended the temporal rule of the popes, no pope had recognized the Italian state. Each had declared himself a prisoner in the Vatican and refused to set foot outside it. The quarrel was called the Roman Question. Liberal governments had tried to settle it for half a century. A fascist one did.

The agreements had three parts. The first created a sovereign state of about 109 acres around St. Peter's, the smallest country in the world, under the absolute rule of the pope. The second, a concordat, made Catholicism the sole religion of the Italian state, put religious instruction into the public schools, and gave church marriages the force of civil law. The third paid the Holy See for the territory it had lost: 750 million lire in cash, and a billion more in government bonds. Gasparri signed with a gold pen that Pius XI had sent for the occasion.

Two days later, speaking to professors and students of the Catholic University of Milan, Pius XI said that the settlement had been possible only because of a man "whom Providence has placed in our path," a man free of the prejudices of the liberal politicians who had come before him. The phrase was repeated across Italy. In March, Mussolini held an election in which voters were offered a single list of candidates chosen by the Fascist Grand Council, to be accepted or rejected as a whole. Priests urged their parishioners to vote. More than ninety-eight percent voted yes. The regime had begun as a gang of black-shirted street fighters burning union halls. Now it had been blessed by the oldest institution in Europe.

In Germany, the year began with what looked like the Republic's best hope. In Paris that spring, a committee chaired by the American industrialist Owen D. Young negotiated a final settlement of German reparations. The total was cut sharply. Payments would be spread across fifty-nine years, until 1988, and Allied troops would leave the Rhineland five years ahead of schedule. Gustav Stresemann, the foreign minister who had spent six years patiently negotiating Germany's way back into the community of nations, carried the plan through the Hague conference in August. He was ill and exhausted. On October 3, he suffered two strokes and died. He was fifty-one. Three weeks later, the market broke in New York, and the American loans that had kept the German economy afloat began to be called home.

To the German right, the Young Plan was not relief but a renewal of Germany's humiliation. It was the Treaty of Versailles extended for another two generations. Its most powerful opponent was Alfred Hugenberg, the leader of the conservative German National People's Party. Hugenberg was a former Krupp director who controlled a large share of the German press. That included a publishing house, a news service that supplied hundreds of provincial papers, and UFA, the country's largest film studio. In July, he formed a national committee to force a referendum against the plan. He brought in the leaders of the Stahlhelm, the great veterans' league, and of the Pan-German League. He also invited Adolf Hitler.

Hitler's party was then a small, noisy movement on the fringe of the right. In the Reichstag election of 1928, it had won less than three percent of the vote. Respectable conservatives had kept their distance from it since the failed putsch in Munich in 1923. Hugenberg thought he could use the Nazis' energy, their rallies and their young men, as a drum to beat for his own campaign. Hitler accepted, on condition that his party keep its independence and receive a share of the funds.

The committee drafted a "Law against the Enslavement of the German People." It repudiated the war guilt clause of the Versailles treaty, and it provided that any minister who signed an agreement accepting new reparations could be prosecuted for treason. The petition to put it to the voters gathered just over ten percent of the electorate, barely the number required. The Reichstag rejected the law at the end of November. On December 22, it went to a referendum. It needed the votes of half of all registered voters to pass. It received less than fourteen percent.

For Hugenberg, it was a defeat. For Hitler, it was the most valuable campaign of his career to that point. For six months his speeches had been carried in newspapers read by millions of Germans who had never paid attention to him. He had appeared on platforms beside generals, industrialists, and the leaders of the established right, as one of them. That August, the party had held its rally in Nuremberg on a scale it had never managed before. Money and members came in. On December 8, in the state election in Thuringia, the Nazis won more than eleven percent of the vote. When the new state government was formed a few weeks later, its conservative partners could not command a majority without them. The price was the ministry of the interior, which controlled the police, and the ministry of education. The man the Nazis sent to take them was Wilhelm Frick, who had stood trial with Hitler after the Munich putsch.

The referendum had failed. What it had opened, the world would spend the next decade and a half trying to close again.
