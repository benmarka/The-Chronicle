Equilibrium

Covers Death, Immigration, Jazz Themes Consolidation, Suspension, Contradiction

Intro: Between States

Thomas Mann and E.M. Forster both began their novels in 1913. Both men set their work aside when the war came, and both returned to it changed. Mann's book grew from a planned novella into a thousand-page meditation on a Europe that no longer existed by the time he finished it. Forster's took even longer to finish than it did to conceive, and he later admitted he had nearly abandoned it more than once. Neither man could have known, in 1913, what kind of book he was actually writing. Both books appeared in 1924, within months of each other, and both are, at bottom, about the same problem: what happens when two incompatible things refuse either to merge or to separate.

The Magic Mountain opens with a young engineer named Hans Castorp arriving at a Swiss sanatorium for a three-week visit to his tubercular cousin. He stays seven years. The mountain air disorders his sense of time almost immediately, and Mann gives him a body to match: Castorp's temperature settles at 37.6 degrees, a reading that is neither sickness nor health, and he can never quite work out which side of the line he is on. The sanatorium itself runs the same way. It snows in August. The heat turns from stifling to freezing with no season to explain it. Nothing in the book resolves into a normal condition, and nothing is quite permitted to become abnormal either.

Castorp's real education comes from two men who court his allegiance for the length of the novel. Settembrini is an Italian humanist, a believer in reason, progress, and the Enlightenment inheritance of the West. Naphta is a Jesuit with communist sympathies and a taste for revelation over argument, a man who thinks the rule of law is a symptom of decline rather than a cure for it. The two of them argue for years, over Castorp's sickbed and everywhere else, about reason against faith, liberty against authority, the individual against the collective — the entire menu of the coming century's quarrels, rehearsed a decade early in a sanatorium dining room. Mann does not let either man win. He lets them fight a duel instead. Settembrini fires into the air. Naphta, denied even the dignity of an opponent willing to kill him, turns the pistol on himself.

Mann finished the novel's philosophical center, a chapter called "Snow," in the first half of 1923, in the middle of the German currency's collapse. Lost in a blizzard and half convinced he is dying, Castorp dreams his way to a single conclusion, and Mann sets it apart from the rest of the book in spaced, italicized type, the only sentence in nine hundred pages given that treatment: for the sake of goodness and love, man shall grant death no dominion over his thoughts. It reads like an answer to Naphta and Settembrini both, a modest peace treaty between reason and faith, offered to readers who had spent the year watching their savings evaporate and their streets fill with paramilitaries. It is easy to overstate what the gesture accomplishes. Castorp himself forgets the vision almost as soon as he wakes, and by the time the mountain empties him back into the flatlands, he is marching into the war that no dream could have prepared him for. The duel still ends in a corpse. But the attempt itself — reaching for synthesis in a novel that spends nine hundred pages proving synthesis nearly impossible — says something about what an exhausted, currency-stabilizing Germany wanted from its fiction that year.

A Passage to India reaches its own point of suspension by different means. Forster's plot turns on an expedition to the Marabar Caves, where an Englishwoman named Adela Quested believes she has been assaulted by her Indian host, Dr. Aziz, in the dark. What actually happened in the cave is never explained. Forster said as much himself: to specify the cause of Adela's terror would fix the book to one answer and destroy every other reading it could support. The caves have an echo that returns every sound as the same flat syllable, erasing the distinction between a question and its answer before either can be heard clearly. Adela recants her accusation at the trial that follows, but the damage to Aziz, and to his friendship with the Englishman Fielding, is not undone by an acquittal.

The novel's final pages try, gently, to let the two men reconcile, and the land itself will not permit it. Their horses swerve apart on the road. The temples, the jail, the birds refuse the meeting Fielding proposes, and Forster gives the landscape its own verdict: not yet, and not there. It is a strange thing for a physical setting to have an opinion, and Forster clearly means for it to. The empire that built the road the two men are riding on is not ready to produce the friendship it claims to make possible, and the book ends by saying so without saying so directly.

André Breton wanted something neither Mann nor Forster was willing to grant their characters. His Manifesto of Surrealism, published that October, defines the movement as a kind of automatic writing — thought recorded without the interference of reason or judgment, borrowed loosely from Freud's account of dreams and pressed into service as an artistic method. But the manifesto's real ambition sits a layer beneath the technique. Breton writes of his belief in the eventual resolution of dream and reality, two states he calls only apparently contradictory, into a single absolute condition he names surreality. Where Mann's duelists cannot be reconciled and Forster's landscape refuses reconciliation outright, Breton insists that reconciliation is coming — that the wall between the sleeping mind and the waking one is provisional, and will fall.

He does not claim to have gotten there. In the same breath as the promise, Breton admits he expects never to reach the condition he is describing, and pursues it anyway. That admission is worth sitting with. The most ambitious literary movement of 1924, the one that names fusion as its explicit goal and stakes its whole program on achieving it, arrives at the same place as the sanatorium debate and the ride through the Marabar hills: a state held open, unresolved, worked at rather than won. Three very different books, written across a war none of them mention directly, keep finding their way back to the same unfinished sentence.

Part I: Succession

Lenin died on January 21, at his estate in Gorki outside Moscow, after months of paralysis and silence following a fourth stroke. He was fifty-three. The Politburo announced the cause as an incurable disease of the blood vessels and left it at that. Within hours the machinery of mourning was already moving: a special train to carry the body to Moscow, three days of public viewing at the House of Unions, a funeral in Red Square on the 27th, attended by tens of thousands in temperatures cold enough to kill. Stalin helped carry the coffin. He also gave one of the funeral orations, a litany of vows — to the party, to the peasantry, to the union of nations — that sounded less like grief than a coronation liturgy, delivered in a register Lenin himself never used.

Leon Trotsky was not there. He was recuperating on the Black Sea coast, at Sukhumi, for a lingering fever, and by his own later account he had been given a funeral date that made the journey back seem pointless. The story hardened over the years into a parable of Stalinist cunning: the rival maneuvered out of the frame at the one moment when his presence would have mattered. The archival record is less tidy. The funeral's date appears to have shifted legitimately in the days after Lenin's death, and Trotsky had time to reach Moscow regardless of which date he was told, had he chosen to interrupt his convalescence. What is not disputed is the result. Stalin stood over the coffin. Trotsky did not. In a contest that would shortly come down to appearances as much as arguments, that photograph was worth more than either man's speeches.

The Politburo had known this reckoning was coming for over a year. In December 1922 and January 1923, as the strokes accumulated, Lenin dictated a series of notes to his secretaries assessing the men most likely to succeed him. He was unsparing with all of them. Trotsky, brilliant but arrogant. Bukharin, the party's favorite but no real dialectician. Zinoviev and Kamenev, forever marked by their opposition to the October seizure of power. Stalin fared worst of all. Lenin called him too rude for the post of General Secretary and recommended, in a postscript added after Stalin was abusive to Lenin's wife by telephone, that the party find someone else for the job — someone more patient, more loyal, less capricious.

Nadezhda Krupskaya held the document until after Lenin's death, then sent it to the Central Committee ahead of the Thirteenth Party Congress that May. What might have been Stalin's undoing became, instead, a formality to be managed. Zinoviev and Kamenev, who had formed a ruling triumvirate with Stalin specifically to keep Trotsky from power, needed him too much to let Lenin's judgment cost him his post. The compromise they arranged had the testament read aloud to each regional delegation separately, with no note-taking permitted and no mention of it at the full Congress. Stalin, for his part, played the wounded servant of the party and offered to resign. He would make the same gesture more than once that year, always confident of the answer. Every time, his rivals — the very men the testament had asked to remove him — talked him out of it, because none of them could imagine governing without him and none of them yet understood what governing with him would cost.

Ten days after the funeral, on January 31, the Second Congress of Soviets ratified the first constitution of the USSR, formalizing a union that had existed on paper since the founding treaty of December 1922. It gave the new state its institutional skeleton — a Congress of Soviets as supreme authority, a Central Executive Committee to govern between sessions, a nominal right of each republic to secede that none would be permitted to exercise. The document said nothing about who would lead any of it. The country Lenin built acquired its founding law in the same month it lost the one man whose authority had never needed writing down.

The coincidence of dates produced one small, telling detail. On the evening Lenin's death was announced, a British cabinet official leaving his Whitehall office saw two newspaper placards side by side: Lenin Dead, and Ramsay MacDonald Premier. MacDonald took office the following day, Britain's first Labour government, a minority administration dependent on Liberal sufferance and freighted with exactly the anxieties that headline pairing implied. In February he extended diplomatic recognition to the Soviet Union his opponents were already calling a Bolshevik contagion. By October his government was gone, brought down by a vote of no confidence and finished off, in the public's mind if not in the actual arithmetic of the election, by a letter published four days before the polls opened — supposedly from the Comintern's Zinoviev, urging Britain's communists toward revolution, almost certainly forged, and never proven either way to the satisfaction of historians who have spent a century arguing about it. Labour's vote went up in that election. Its seats did not. Britain's first experiment in socialist government did not fall because voters rejected it outright; it fell because the Liberal center collapsed beneath it, and because the specter of Moscow, real or invented, was enough to make that collapse look like a verdict.

Two governments, then, both testing how much of the old order could bend before it broke, in the same twelve months and under the same rhetorical shadow. One inherited a revolution its founder was no longer alive to arbitrate, and settled the argument by refusing to have it in public — a document that named the danger sealed inside an envelope, then filed. The other inherited nothing but suspicion, and lasted just long enough to prove that suspicion was enough. Lenin's body was already being prepared for a mausoleum his colleagues insisted on despite his own reported wish for an ordinary burial. He would spend the rest of the century on display, embalmed, unaging, presiding wordlessly over a succession he no longer had any way to settle.

Part II: The New Republics

On April 13, Greek voters went to the polls to decide, for the second time in four years, whether they wanted a king. Seventy percent said no. It looked like a verdict. The National Assembly had already proclaimed the Second Hellenic Republic three weeks earlier, on March 25, choosing the date deliberately: it was the anniversary of the war of independence a century before, a way of framing the end of the monarchy as a second liberation rather than a rupture. The numbers were lopsided enough to suggest the argument was finally over.

It was not. Greece had spent the previous decade oscillating between crown and republic so many times that contemporaries called it the National Schism, and nothing about 1924 actually stopped the pendulum. King Constantine had been forced out once in 1917, restored by referendum in 1920, and forced out again in 1922 after the army he sent into Asia Minor was destroyed and Smyrna burned. His son George II inherited a throne nobody particularly wanted him to have, survived a royalist coup attempt in his own name that he had no part in, and was asked to simply leave the country in December 1923 so the nation could decide its future without him standing in the room. He complied, and refused to abdicate, which meant the republic proclaimed in March was voted into being over a king who was still, technically, a king.

The vote itself came wrapped in more than a decade's accumulated exhaustion. The 1923 Treaty of Lausanne had settled the border war with Turkey by trading populations wholesale: more than a million Greeks from Asia Minor arrived destitute in a country of six million, while Muslims resident in Greece were sent the other way. The republic inherited that displaced population along with the throne it discarded, and the newcomers, embittered by a war the monarchy had gambled and lost, became one of the republic's most reliable constituencies. The government that oversaw the referendum was taking no chances with the result either way: a decree passed the same year made it a criminal offense, punishable by at least six months in prison, to publicly advocate the monarchy's return or dispute the vote's legitimacy. A settlement that needs a law against arguing with it is not yet a settlement.

The eleven years that followed the "settled" question produced twenty-three governments and more coup attempts than years. The monarchy came back in 1935, restored by a plebiscite even less credible than the one that had ended it. Nineteen twenty-four did not resolve Greece's regime question. It only gave the country a name for its condition before returning, a decade later, to the condition itself.

Turkey settled a comparable question with a permanence Greece never managed, and it did so by first holding two incompatible states in place for over a year before collapsing them into one. When the Grand National Assembly abolished the Ottoman sultanate in November 1922, it did not abolish the caliphate alongside it. Instead it split the two offices that had been fused in one person for four centuries, stripped the caliphate of all political authority, and installed Abdülmecid II — a mild, cultivated man who painted, played the cello, and had shown no interest in power — as caliph in name only. He kept a modest court in Istanbul. He had no army, no treasury, no say in law. He was allowed to exist as a religious symbol precisely because he no longer had the capacity to be anything else. For sixteen months, Turkey ran a real experiment in whether a purely symbolic caliphate could coexist with a secular nationalist republic.

The trigger that ended the experiment came from outside the country. In November 1923, two leaders of India's Khilafat movement, campaigning to keep the caliphate alive as a rallying point for Muslims under British rule, wrote to the Turkish government urging it to preserve the office. Ankara read the letter as foreign interference in Turkish sovereignty, which is precisely what Mustafa Kemal needed it to be. He told the caliph's own secretaries, in terms that left no room for negotiation, that the office was nothing more than a historical relic with no justification for existing. On March 3, the Assembly voted to abolish it. Abdülmecid was given a few hours' notice, put on the Orient Express the next morning, and sent into exile along with more than two hundred members of the Ottoman dynasty. The same session of parliament passed a law folding all religious schools into a single secular education system, so that the caliphate and the institutions that might have sustained a rival authority to the republic were dismantled in the same afternoon.

Unlike Greece's monarchy, the caliphate did not come back. Candidates lined up for years afterward — the Sharif of Mecca, the king of Afghanistan, the king of Egypt, at least a dozen others — and conferences convened in Cairo and Jerusalem to try to settle on a successor. None produced a consensus the wider Muslim world would accept, and the office has stood vacant ever since. An institution that had run continuously, in one form or another, since the seventh century ended not with a war or a revolution from below but with a vote taken in an afternoon, over the objections of a man who by then had no power to resist it.

Both countries spent 1924 trying to convert exhaustion into permanence. Greece did it by ballot, twice, and found that a landslide is not the same thing as an ending — the same argument would still be running, and the same king would still be waiting in Bucharest, a decade later. Turkey did it by fiat, and the finality held, in part because Atatürk had spent the previous year building an office with no functioning power to defend itself, an equilibrium engineered to be broken the moment it became inconvenient. Two multiethnic land empires, dismantled by the same war, produced two different answers to the same question of what comes after a dynasty: one country's vote proved provisional, and the other's law proved absolute, and in 1924 there was no way yet to tell which was which.

Part III: Fascist Consolidation

On April 6, Italy held the freest-looking election it would see for over twenty years, and it was rigged before a single vote was cast. The Acerbo Law, passed the previous November under pressure from Mussolini's government, guaranteed two-thirds of the seats in parliament to whichever list won a plurality of at least a quarter of the vote. Mussolini's National List, an alliance of fascists and the liberal notables willing to lend them respectability, cleared that bar by a wide margin on its own merits in some regions and by naked intimidation in others — blackshirts wrecking opposition offices, watching polling stations, killing at least one rival candidate outright. The result was a landslide of roughly two-thirds of the vote, translated by law into a chamber the fascists now dominated outright. Mussolini had come to power in 1922 through a negotiated capitulation dressed up as a march. Eighteen months later, he had a parliamentary mandate that looked, on paper, like the real thing.

Giacomo Matteotti did not accept the premise. On May 30, the Socialist deputy stood in the Chamber and read out, in detail, the fraud and violence that had produced the government's majority. He told colleagues afterward they could start preparing his funeral. Eleven days later, a squad of Fascist Party enforcers forced him into a car in broad daylight in Rome. He was stabbed to death and buried in a shallow grave outside the city; his body was not found for two months. The public reaction was immediate and, for a moment, genuinely threatening to the regime. Fascist Party badges disappeared from lapels overnight. The waiting room outside Mussolini's office, usually crowded with supplicants, stood empty. On June 27, the opposition parties walked out of parliament entirely, in a protest they called the Aventine Secession, and refused to return until the murder was answered for.

For the rest of the summer and into the autumn, Mussolini did not look like a dictator consolidating power. He looked like a man who might lose it. Rank-and-file fascists quietly stopped attending party meetings. The regime's own extremists, embarrassed by the murder and impatient with Mussolini's equivocating, began muttering about replacing him with someone willing to finish what the March on Rome had started. King Victor Emmanuel III, who alone had the constitutional authority to dismiss the government, declined to act — not out of confidence in Mussolini, but out of fear that removing him by royal fiat would look like a coup and hand the army and the Blackshirts an excuse to fight each other in the streets. The Aventine secessionists were counting on exactly the intervention the King was determined not to make. By late December, a former Fascist Party official's testimony directly implicated Mussolini in ordering the killing, and on New Year's Eve his own paramilitary chiefs gave him an ultimatum: act, or be replaced.

He acted, though not until January 3, days into the following year. Addressing the Chamber, Mussolini declared that he alone bore the political, moral, and historical responsibility for everything that had happened in Italy since the fascists took power, and dared anyone in the room to prosecute him for it under a constitutional provision he knew a fascist-dominated parliament would never invoke. No one did. Within weeks, the government had shuttered the opposition press, banned rival parties in all but name, and formalized the dictatorship the Aventine Secession had tried and failed to prevent. The election that opened 1924 had given fascism a legal mandate. The murder that followed nearly cost it everything. The speech that closed the affair, arriving just after the calendar turned, converted six months of paralysis into the thing Mussolini had never quite had before: total, undisguised control.

Adolf Hitler spent the same months on the opposite trajectory, arriving in roughly the same place by falling rather than climbing. His attempt to seize power by force — storming a Munich beer hall the previous November, firing a pistol into the ceiling, declaring the national revolution begun — had collapsed within a day, ending in gunfire outside the Feldherrnhalle and Hitler's own arrest two days later, discovered hiding in an attic in his nightclothes. His trial for high treason opened on February 26, while Italians were still lining up to vote under the Acerbo Law, and ran into April. It should have finished him. Instead the presiding judge, sympathetic to a defendant he considered a patriotic German rather than a criminal, allowed Hitler to turn the courtroom into a stage. His opening statement ran nearly four hours. He insisted the coup had been his idea alone and his responsibility alone, and the newspapers that printed his speeches verbatim made him a national figure for the first time. He was convicted on April 1 and sentenced to five years at Landsberg, a fortress prison reserved for offenders the state considered misguided rather than dangerous. He was out by December 20, having served well under a year, in a cell furnished more like a study than a cell, dictating the first volume of Mein Kampf to fellow inmate Rudolf Hess.

Where Mussolini spent 1924 defending a position he already held, Hitler spent it absorbing the lesson of having tried to seize one by force and failed. The putsch convinced him the state could not be toppled by an armed handful marching on a city hall; it would have to be captured from the inside, through the same ballot boxes and parliamentary seats Mussolini had just demonstrated could be bent to fit a movement's ambitions without ever breaking the law that governed them. Hitler left prison banned from public speaking in most of Germany, his party still technically outlawed, his following diminished by a year out of view. He also left with a manuscript, a theory of how power was actually taken, and the conviction — which he would repeat for the rest of his life — that the failure of the putsch had been the best thing that ever happened to him.

Two men, then, arriving at the same conclusion from opposite directions inside the same twelve months. One nearly lost a government he had already built, and saved it by admitting, in public, to everything his enemies suspected. The other lost an armed uprising outright, and used the defeat to abandon uprisings altogether. Fascism did not consolidate in Italy in 1924 so much as survive its own near-collapse and call the survival a mandate. In Germany it barely existed yet as anything but a rebuilding plan, sketched out by a man with nothing to do in a comfortable cell but reconsider how patient he was willing to be.

Part IV: American Contradictions

On May 26, Calvin Coolidge signed the Immigration Act into law, and the United States closed its door more deliberately than it ever had before. The law set quotas for each nationality at two percent of that group's presence in the country as of the 1890 census — not the most recent count, a deliberately outdated one, chosen at the urging of a eugenicist-led subcommittee specifically because it undercounted the waves of Italian, Jewish, and Slavic immigrants who had arrived since. Immigration from Asia was barred outright, with no quota at all. Japan took the exclusion as the insult it was plainly designed to be. Its ambassador warned Washington of the grave consequences the measure would bring, a sentence one senator chose to read as a threat rather than a warning, which helped push the bill through. A Japanese citizen took his own life outside the American embassy in Tokyo in protest. The bill's chief sponsor in the House had said the country needed to stop being an asylum. Congress agreed with him by a vote of 323 to 71; among the small handful who did not was a freshman congressman from Brooklyn named Emanuel Celler, a Jewish American who would spend the next four decades in the House trying, and repeatedly failing, to get the law undone.

One week later, on June 2, Coolidge signed a second bill that ran in the opposite direction. The Indian Citizenship Act declared every Native American born within the country's borders a citizen of it, closing a gap that had persisted since the Constitution itself excluded "Indians not taxed" from the population it counted. Roughly a quarter of a million Native Americans gained citizenship they had not asked for and, in a number of cases, explicitly did not want. The Onondaga Nation wrote to Coolidge that December formally protesting the law as a violation of an eighteenth-century treaty, passed without consulting the people it claimed to elevate. The reasoning behind it was not principally generous. Reformers had spent decades trying to dissolve tribal land holdings and governance in favor of individual assimilation, and a number of the law's backers saw citizenship as a mechanism for finishing that work by other means — if Indians were citizens of the United States first, tribal sovereignty would have that much less to stand on. The right to vote that citizenship was supposed to carry remained, in practice, a matter of state law, and several states continued to deny it to Native Americans for decades afterward.

Both bills were signed against the backdrop of a government still absorbing the largest corruption scandal in its history. Teapot Dome had been building since a Senate investigation opened in October 1923, but 1924 was the year it actually broke Harding's cabinet. In January, oilman Edward Doheny testified that his son had personally delivered $100,000 in cash to former Interior Secretary Albert Fall in a small satchel, ostensibly as a loan, shortly after Fall had quietly leased the Elk Hills naval oil reserve to Doheny's company without competitive bidding. On January 31, the Senate declared that the Teapot Dome and Elk Hills leases had been executed under circumstances indicating fraud and corruption. Navy Secretary Edwin Denby, who had signed the leases, resigned in February. Attorney General Harry Daugherty, already under suspicion for unrelated graft, held on until Coolidge personally demanded his resignation on March 28, after Daugherty's own brother refused a Senate subpoena related to the case. The government spent the rest of the year suing to void the leases in federal court and would spend the rest of the decade prosecuting the men involved; Fall would not be convicted of accepting a bribe until 1929, becoming the first American cabinet officer sent to prison, while the men who paid him were acquitted. What 1924 actually produced was not justice so much as a clean sweep of the cabinet that had allowed the corruption to happen, and a president eager to prove, in an election year, that the housecleaning was thorough.

It was in that housecleaning that, on May 10, Coolidge's new attorney general put a twenty-nine-year-old Justice Department lawyer in charge of the Bureau of Investigation. Harlan Fiske Stone had just forced out the previous director, implicated along with Daugherty in the Teapot Dome fallout, and he wanted the bureau's reputation for political favors and dirty tricks ended. J. Edgar Hoover looked like the answer: young, meticulous, untouched by the scandal, openly contemptuous of the patronage system that had produced it. He spent his first months firing agents he considered unqualified or compromised, building a merit system to replace them, and in July establishing a national fingerprint identification division, consolidating records that had previously been scattered across separate state and municipal files. What Stone may not have fully reckoned with was that Hoover had learned his trade running the Bureau's General Intelligence Division under a previous attorney general, compiling files on tens of thousands of suspected radicals and helping execute the mass roundups of immigrants and leftists that made his former boss a national byword for federal overreach. The reformer sent in to clean up a bureau corrupted by political abuse of power had spent his twenties building one of the more effective machines for exactly that, and now had a growing national archive of identifying records to build on top of it.

All three decisions were still fresh when the country went to the polls that November, in the first presidential election in which Native Americans, newly declared citizens five months earlier, were nominally entitled to vote everywhere in the country. Coolidge won in a landslide anyway — fifty-four percent, against twenty-nine for the Democrat John Davis and a remarkable sixteen and a half for Robert La Follette's Progressive ticket, the best showing for a third party since Theodore Roosevelt split the Republican vote in 1912, though well short of Roosevelt's own 27 percent that year. Coolidge campaigned on the same two words that defined his presidency, prosperity and restraint, and barely campaigned at all in the conventional sense, letting a booming economy argue on his behalf. Both of his opponents took the trouble to denounce the Ku Klux Klan, then in the middle of its national resurgence. Coolidge did not, despite direct appeals from Black civic organizations asking him to. The New York Times noted the silence at the time and could not decide whether it reflected calculation or simple indifference.

Read together, the year's federal actions describe a government drawing the boundaries of American belonging with unusual precision and very little consistency. It narrowed, by law and by explicit design, who could become American by arriving. It expanded, without asking, who already counted as American by birth, largely as a way of dissolving the separate status that birth had previously guaranteed. It installed a national police power under a reformer whose own record should have made the reform look less reassuring than it did. And it elected, by a wide margin, a government willing to legislate all of that while staying carefully silent on the one form of American exclusion that was organizing itself in bedsheets and burning crosses in public. The country was, by any legal measure, defining itself more explicitly in 1924 than it had in decades. It was mostly deciding who was out.

Coda: Rhapsody in Blue

George Gershwin forgot he had agreed to write it. His brother Ira read about the piece in a newspaper in early January, weeks before the concert, and had to ask George what a jazz concerto for Paul Whiteman's orchestra was. Gershwin remembered a conversation. He had not understood it as a commitment. With five weeks left, working around a Broadway show that was about to open, he started composing on January 7, sketched the main theme on a train, and handed pages to Whiteman's arranger, Ferde Grofé, as fast as he could finish them. Grofé orchestrated the whole thing in about a week. Nobody involved had time to be careful, which turned out not to matter.

The piece premiered on February 12 at Aeolian Hall, under the title Whiteman had billed as "An Experiment in Modern Music," second to last on a long, didactic program meant to walk a skeptical audience from the crudest early jazz toward something respectable. The earlier numbers had titles like "True Form of Jazz" and dragged. The ventilation system failed. People were leaving. Then a single clarinet stretched a scale into a wail that climbed nearly three octaves before the rest of the band came in, and the room, by every account, sat back up.

That opening had not been written that way. In rehearsal, the clarinetist Ross Gorman had played the passage as a joke, exaggerating the slide between notes for a laugh. Gershwin liked it enough to ask him to do it again at the concert, and louder. What became the most recognized handful of seconds in American concert music — critics would later rank it with the opening of Beethoven's Fifth — started as somebody clowning around in a room nobody expected to matter. The piano part carried the same kind of built-in uncertainty on purpose. Gershwin never finished writing it down. He left blank pages in the score marked simply "wait for nod," played the solo from memory at the premiere, and improvised through it live, meaning no one — not the band, not Grofé, not Gershwin himself in later years — could say for certain how the piece had actually sounded the first time anyone heard it.

Whiteman's stated purpose was to prove that jazz could be lifted to the level of the concert hall, which was a slightly different ambition than proving the two idioms were equals. He wanted the classical world to bless the popular one, not quite to meet it as a peer, and much of the classical establishment spent the following decades declining to grant the blessing anyway. Composers who might have shaped the direction of American concert music for a generation dismissed Gershwin as a gifted amateur who had wandered into their tradition without earning it. The fusion the afternoon seemed to prove possible remained, for the people whose approval Whiteman had actually wanted, an open question for the rest of the century.

None of that mattered to the room on February 12. Whatever the piece was supposed to accomplish in the abstract, in the moment it simply worked: a single continuous score that let a popular idiom and a concert tradition occupy the same eighteen minutes without either one giving way, played from a script that was still, in its most important section, unfinished. Breton had written that same year of an eventual resolution between dream and reality into a single condition he could name but not yet reach. Mann's sanatorium settled its argument between reason and faith with a suicide rather than a synthesis. Forster's ending had the land itself refuse to let two men's friendship complete. Gershwin, with five weeks, a deadline, and a joke he decided to keep, found his own resolution of form.
