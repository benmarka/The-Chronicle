HIGHS AND LOWS

Covers Movies, Literature, Communists Themes Innovation, Interiority, Betrayal

Intro: A Day in New York

The stadium in the Bronx holds close to seventy thousand people, and on a good afternoon in 1927 it fills. The team on the field will finish the season with a record no one will match for decades — Murderers' Row, four and five and six hitters deep, a lineup that turns batting practice into a threat. When Ruth connects, the sound arrives before the ball lands: seventy thousand voices compressing into one syllable, then the longer roar that follows the arc out toward the bleachers. A woman in the crowd is on her feet with everyone else. She will not remember, later, whether the ball actually cleared the wall. She will remember the noise.

She leaves before the ninth, the way people do when they already know how it ends. The subway back into Manhattan is full of men in shirtsleeves still arguing about the count. By the time she surfaces near Fifth Avenue, the game has already become something that happened somewhere else, to someone else's afternoon.

She changes into a black dress that would have scandalized her mother — jersey, unstructured, cut to the knee, the waist dropped to the hip so the whole line of the body reads straight down, no curve announced. Coco Chanel's doing, four thousand miles away, though the dress in the shopwindow doesn't say so. She bobs nothing further; her hair is already short. She walks.

Fifth Avenue is still being built into something it hasn't finished becoming. The Fred F. French Building went up this year, thirty-eight stories with a tower of glazed terra-cotta at the top, Babylonian griffins and Assyrian color on a Manhattan office block, the tallest thing on the avenue for now. She doesn't look up at it so much as feel its shadow cross her as she passes. Further south and west, past the theaters, the Paramount Building's globe and clocktower have just gone up over Times Square — Paramount Pictures built it as its eastern headquarters, thirty-three stories of setbacks lit from below, the tallest thing on Broadway. It is, whether she registers this or not, a movie studio's name lettered onto the skyline in stone. The pictures have arrived at the size of architecture.

A newsboy's stack on the corner is still turning over headlines from the spring — LINDBERGH DOES IT, the Times had run in May, the largest type the paper owned, Le Bourget and thirty-three and a half hours and a hundred thousand people surging past soldiers with fixed bayonets to reach a mud-streaked airplane. Months on, the phrase hasn't worn out. It has become a kind of shorthand for the whole feeling of the year — the sense that speed, distance, and attention all just got renegotiated at once, and nobody has fully priced in what that means yet.

She doesn't buy a paper. She's read it already, everyone has, and there's somewhere to be. Down a side street, past a door with no sign, a man checks her face through a slot before he opens it. Prohibition has been the law for seven years and has, by 1927, organized itself into an entire shadow infrastructure of doors like this one — a nationwide arrangement for getting around a nationwide rule, hidden in plain sight, tolerated by exactly the people it was meant to reform. Inside, someone is already playing.

It is a good year to be young, moneyed or near it, and in New York. The papers have never sold better. The buildings have never gone up faster. A man flew an ocean by himself and the whole world found out within hours. The door closes behind her, and for a moment the year holds still at its own high-water mark — before it starts, in the months that follow, to ask what all that noise was covering for.

Part I — Spectacle

Warner Bros. was not, in 1927, one of the major studios. It was a mid-tier operation that had bet its survival on Vitaphone, a sound-on-disc system it licensed from Western Electric when the larger studios declined to take the risk seriously. Sam Warner, the brother who championed the technology and pushed The Jazz Singer into production, died of complications from a mastoid infection on October 5th — one day before the premiere he had spent years trying to make happen. The film that opened at the Warner Theatre the next night was, for most of its running time, still a conventional silent picture: intertitles, a synchronized orchestral score, the grammar audiences had had three decades to learn. Then Al Jolson, playing a cantor's son chasing a career in popular music against his father's wishes, turned toward the camera in a nightclub scene and simply talked. "Wait a minute, wait a minute, you ain't heard nothin' yet." A few lines, maybe two minutes of synchronized dialogue in a ninety-minute film, recorded onto a wax disc spinning in time with the projector. It was not the first experiment with sound — Vitaphone itself had already been used for musical shorts, and competitors were tinkering with their own systems — but it was the first time a feature-length narrative film put a human voice at the center of a scene and let an audience hear it speak.

The industry's response was not admiration so much as panic. Within a year, theaters across the country were being wired for sound at considerable expense, whether their owners wanted to make the investment or not, because the alternative was showing pictures nobody wanted to watch anymore. Actors whose voices didn't match their screen presence quietly vanished from studio rosters over the next two or three years; dialect coaches became a necessary studio expense; entire film grammars built around intertitles and expressive pantomime were abandoned, not gradually but within a production cycle or two. Jolson performed much of The Jazz Singer in blackface, a continuation of a stage act he'd been doing on Broadway for years and a convention of American popular entertainment the period did not yet treat as disqualifying. The controversy was the voice.

Broadway produced its own reordering two months later, smaller in scale but sharper in what it attempted. Show Boat opened at the Ziegfeld Theatre on December 27th, adapted by Jerome Kern and Oscar Hammerstein II from Edna Ferber's novel, and it did something the American musical had mostly avoided: it asked an audience that came for spectacle and melody to sit with material the form had no real precedent for handling seriously — a mixed-race woman passing as white under threat of Virginia's anti-miscegenation statute, a husband who abandons his family rather than face what he's done, a life measured out mostly in loss. Jules Bledsoe sang "Ol' Man River" in the original production, a song built to carry the show's argument in a few minutes of melody — the river kept moving, indifferent to the people stranded on its banks, who did not get to move the way it did. The production was still a product of its era's assumptions about who narrates and who is narrated; later stagings, and later decades, would revise it considerably, and some of what the original did would not survive now. But something in the American musical shifted here that did not shift back. The form had shown it could hold weight without buckling, and the industry did not fully forget it.

Fritz Lang had already made the argument that spectacle and dread were not opposites, a year earlier and an ocean away. Metropolis premiered in Germany in January 1926, a project so vast and expensive — nearly the entire budget of UFA, the studio producing it — that its cost effectively bankrupted the company that made it. The American release that reached audiences in 1927 was a different film almost entirely: distributors, convinced the original's sprawl and strangeness would not sell, cut it down by nearly a third, trimming subplots and softening ambiguities until what remained was a more conventional spectacle than Lang and his co-writer, Thea von Harbou, had built. The full version would not resurface for decades; some of what was lost is, as far as anyone knows, lost for good. What survived in the American cut was still legible enough to unsettle: a city stratified into two literal tiers, laborers running the machines underground that let a leisure class live in towers above them, a workers' uprising, and at the center, a robot built in the image of a beloved woman and sent to manipulate the workers she resembled into violence that would destroy them. Weimar Germany was a handful of years from its own catastrophic unraveling, and in hindsight Lang's anxieties about mechanized control curdling into manipulation read like a rehearsal for fears the country had not yet learned to name. In 1927 the film mostly read as spectacle — astonishing to look at, and easy enough not to take at its word.

None of this was new in kind. The machinery of mass persuasion and mass attention had been assembling itself for years — tabloids, newsreels, radio, all competing for the same limited span of public attention, truth already a negotiable commodity in a marketplace built to reward whatever sold. What 1927 contributed was scale and permanence. A few minutes of synchronized dialogue ended an entire mode of filmmaking within two years. A musical about a boat on the Mississippi changed, lastingly, what a Broadway musical was permitted to be about. A German film too strange and too expensive for its moment got carved down to something more palatable and still could not be fully suppressed — its ambition survived even the mutilation. The spectacle was not incidental to the era. It was becoming the era's preferred way of telling itself what it was, at a scale and with a permanence the previous decade's newspapers and newsreels had not yet managed.

Part II — Interiority

Virginia Woolf published To the Lighthouse in May, built loosely from her own childhood summers at Talland House in Cornwall — the Ramsays stood in, not quite exactly, for her parents, and the lighthouse itself for a destination promised to children and endlessly deferred by adult circumstance. The novel's middle section, "Time Passes," was its most audacious structural choice: it emptied the house of its family and let roughly a decade go by across a few dozen pages, seasons moving through empty rooms, dust settling, a World War compressed into the margins of the narration. Mrs. Ramsay's death — the emotional center of the book's first section — was reported in this middle section almost as an aside, a few words in brackets, dispatched with a flatness that was more devastating than elaboration would have been. Consciousness, in Woolf's hands, was not a stable instrument recording a stable world. It was partial, drifting, constantly revising what it thought it had just perceived, and the novel's form bent itself around that instability rather than smoothing it into a conventional plot.

Four months earlier, in a different discipline entirely, Werner Heisenberg had published a paper making an adjacent claim about the physical world. The uncertainty principle held that a particle's position and momentum could not both be measured with arbitrary precision — not because instruments were imperfect, but because the act of measurement itself disturbed the thing being measured. In October, the fifth Solvay Conference convened in Brussels under the heading "Electrons and Photons," and the group photograph from that meeting is now famous mostly for who was in it — Bohr, Einstein, Heisenberg, Marie Curie, Schrödinger, Dirac, Pauli, twenty-nine physicists, a majority of them past or future Nobel laureates, arguing in the same rooms about what quantum mechanics actually implied. This is where the long dispute between Bohr and Einstein properly began — Einstein devising thought experiments meant to show the theory was incomplete, that reality had to be definite even when unobserved, Bohr answering each one in turn. Neither man converted the other, then or ever. But the fight itself said something about the year: the uncertainty principle and "Time Passes" were not making the same argument, but they were tuned to the same unease. The observer was not standing safely outside the thing observed, in a laboratory in Copenhagen or in a drawing room in a Woolf novel, and the clean separation between a mind and what it perceives was turning out to be a comforting fiction rather than a fact anyone could still rely on.

Hermann Hesse's Steppenwolf, published in Germany the same year, took the instability inward and made it the entire subject. Hesse wrote the novel out of his own crisis — a failed marriage, a period of depression, a stretch of Jungian analysis that shapes the book's symbolism throughout — and its protagonist, Harry Haller, experienced himself as two creatures permanently at war: one bourgeois, domesticated, ashamed; one wolfish, contemptuous of everything domestication required of him. The novel's strangest set piece, the Magic Theatre, offered Haller a corridor of doors, each opening onto a different possible version of himself, a hallucinatory structure that refused to resolve into a single stable self at all. It was a far less controlled book than Woolf's — more willing to be feverish, more willing to risk looking unhinged — but it worked from the same premise. The self a person presents to others is a negotiated surface over something considerably less organized underneath, and art's task, for both Hesse and Woolf, was to go under that surface rather than describe it politely from above.

Willa Cather's Death Comes for the Archbishop sat at an angle to both. Loosely based on the life of Jean-Baptiste Lamy, the first Catholic bishop of the New Mexico Territory, the novel followed a French missionary establishing a diocese across a landscape too vast and too old to be fully mapped or converted on any human timetable. Cather's method was nearly the opposite of Woolf's — spare, chronological, almost unwilling to dramatize, episodic in a way that deliberately resisted the shape of a conventional plot. But its interiority was just as real; it was simply carried by landscape rather than monologue. The desert's indifference, the slow accumulation of a life against terrain too large to be known, a faith practiced patiently rather than argued — this was Cather's version of the same turn the other two books were making, away from the confidently externalized lives the nineteenth-century novel had been largely content to narrate from the outside.

None of these three books argued directly with the spectacle of Part I so much as ignored it. The same year produced synchronized dialogue and a studio headquarters built like a cathedral to its own industry, and in those same twelve months three major works whose entire project was to insist that the most interesting and least certain territory wasn't on a screen or a stage at all — it was somewhere just behind the eyes, unstable and underlit.

Part III — Consolidation

The fight for control of the Soviet Communist Party had been running since Lenin's death in January 1924, when his ambiguous final testament — critical of several potential successors, Stalin included, but naming no clear heir — left the Party's senior figures to sort out among themselves who actually inherited his authority. Trotsky entered that fight with credentials none of his rivals could match: founder of the Red Army, the man who had organized the Bolshevik victory in the Civil War more or less from nothing, Lenin's closest collaborator through the Revolution's most dangerous years. None of it mattered against the position Stalin had spent those same years quietly accumulating — General Secretary of the Party, a post that sounded administrative and was in fact the control point for every appointment, transfer, and promotion in the apparatus. Trotsky had the prestige of 1917. Stalin had the machinery of the Party itself, and by 1927 the machinery had won.

By then the argument was no longer just personal, if it had ever been only that. Trotsky's doctrine of permanent revolution held that socialism could not survive in Russia alone — that without revolution spreading to the industrial economies of Western Europe, the Soviet state would be strangled by its own isolation and backwardness. Stalin's answer, "socialism in one country," rejected that premise entirely: the Soviet Union would build itself up on its own terms, in isolation if it had to, without betting its survival on revolutions elsewhere that might never come. It was a genuine disagreement about strategy and not merely a pretext, but by 1927 it had curdled into something closer to a loyalty test, and losing the argument, for Trotsky, meant losing allies along with it. Zinoviev and Kamenev, former rivals of his own and, until recently, Stalin's own allies against him, joined Trotsky in the United Opposition less out of agreement than out of common cause against a threat they had helped create and could no longer control. A coalition built on shared danger rather than shared conviction rarely holds, and this one did not.

November 7th was the tenth anniversary of the October Revolution, and the United Opposition organized a demonstration of its own to run alongside the official parade — its own banners, its own slogans, a visible, public claim that the revolution's legacy still belonged to more than one faction. It was put down within the day, state security and loyal Party members breaking up the march before it could become anything more than a gesture. The consequences followed fast. Trotsky and Zinoviev were expelled from the Central Committee within the month; in December, the Fifteenth Party Congress expelled them from the Party altogether, along with dozens of their remaining allies, many of whom were sent into internal exile in Siberia and Central Asia over the following months rather than simply stripped of their posts. Trotsky's own exile to Alma-Ata, deep in Soviet Kazakhstan, followed in January 1928 — by then closer to paperwork than to drama, since the year itself had already decided the outcome. He would be expelled from the Soviet Union entirely within two years, and murdered in Mexico a decade after that, but 1927 was the year the door closed. What followed afterward was mostly enforcement.

The isolation ran outward as well as inward. In May, British police raided the London offices of Arcos, the Soviet trade delegation, acting on suspicion of espionage and the distribution of subversive propaganda within Britain. They found some evidence — though considerably less than the government had hoped to justify the raid's scale — and used it anyway as grounds to sever diplomatic and trade relations with Moscow two weeks later. It was a genuine rupture rather than a gesture: the two countries would not fully restore relations until 1929. Coming in the same year as the purge of the Party's left flank, the Arcos affair completed something like a pincer. The Soviet Union closed ranks against its own internal dissent at almost the same moment it was being pushed into deeper isolation from the outside, and consolidation, approached from either direction, tended to look remarkably alike — fewer voices permitted, fewer avenues left open, the narrowing presented, always, as a form of strength rather than what it more plainly resembled, which was fear.

What made 1927 the hinge of Stalin's ascent rather than merely one more year of Party infighting was less the events themselves than their finality, and one of the ironies the Party could not yet see was unfolding at exactly the same moment four thousand miles away. China policy had been set, for years, by Stalin and the Comintern apparatus he now controlled — and it was Stalin's own insistence that the Chinese Communist Party remain folded inside Chiang Kai-shek's Kuomintang, rather than build an independent base of its own, that left the CCP with no army, no fallback, and no warning when Chiang turned on it that April. Trotsky had argued for years that the policy was a catastrophe waiting to happen, one more front in his broader case against Stalin's judgment. By the time Shanghai proved him right, he no longer had standing in the Party to say so.

Part IV — Betrayal

The Northern Expedition had set out from Guangzhou in 1926 as a joint undertaking — Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalist Revolutionary Army marching north to break the regional warlords who still carved up most of China, with Communist organizers doing much of the unglamorous work of building support in the cities and countryside the army passed through, and Soviet advisors, chief among them Mikhail Borodin, embedded directly inside the Kuomintang's command structure on Stalin's orders. The First United Front, assembled in 1923, had always been a marriage of convenience rather than conviction, bridging two parties that agreed on almost nothing except the warlords standing in both their ways. By early 1927, with the campaign succeeding faster and more completely than anyone had expected, the convenience was running out, and it was running out unevenly — the Kuomintang's left wing, gathered around a rival government in Wuhan, still maintained a working alliance with the Communists even as Chiang's own faction, converging on Shanghai, had already decided the partnership no longer served him.

It ended on April 12th, not through negotiation but through violence Chiang had spent weeks quietly arranging. Troops loyal to his faction moved through Shanghai's labor unions and Communist Party cells alongside members of the Green Gang, a criminal syndicate led by Du Yuesheng that Chiang had cultivated specifically for this operation, rounding up and executing Communist organizers and union leaders who had, only weeks earlier, helped deliver the city to Chiang's forces by rising up against the warlord garrison on his behalf. The workers' militias that had armed themselves for that uprising were disarmed and turned on with their own momentum still intact. The death count is contested and will likely remain so — estimates run from the hundreds into the low thousands. What is not contested is the outcome, or how fast it spread: within weeks, similar purges swept Communist organizers out of city after city under Chiang's control, a campaign that would come to be called the White Terror. An alliance that had called itself revolutionary turned its own revolutionary base into a target within a matter of days, and did not stop at one city.

Borodin, recalled to Moscow not long after, carried with him the wreckage of a policy Stalin had insisted on for years and would not easily admit had failed. The Wuhan government, the Kuomintang's left wing, held out in uneasy alliance with the Communists for a few months longer before it too broke with them in July, leaving the CCP with no faction of the old partnership still willing to shelter it anywhere in China. What followed was less a single event than a forced redirection. Mao Zedong and the surviving Communist leadership did not try to rebuild the urban, labor-based organizing the United Front had depended on — the cities had just demonstrated, violently and in sequence, that they were no longer safe ground anywhere Chiang's reach extended. At an emergency Party conference in August, the leadership began turning toward the countryside instead, toward a model of rural, peasant-based guerrilla organizing that the Moscow-trained Party establishment had mostly dismissed, before this, as insufficiently revolutionary — a shift Mao himself had already been arguing for after months spent studying peasant uprisings in his native Hunan. The Autumn Harvest Uprising later that year and the retreat to the Jinggangshan mountain base that followed it were still months off, but the turn started here, forced into existence rather than chosen from strength.

Chiang, meanwhile, formally established Nanjing as his government's capital in April, inaugurating what would be remembered as the Nanjing decade — a nationalist China consolidating around a single party with its Communist faction violently excised, the Wuhan splinter eventually reabsorbed, and the warlords who still held the north not yet fully subdued but no longer in any position to challenge him as a bloc. The outcome, if not the method, mirrored what Stalin was doing to his own former allies in Moscow at almost exactly the same time, and the two events were not merely parallel — Stalin's China policy had helped produce the very catastrophe that Chiang then inflicted on the CCP, which makes the mirroring less a coincidence of timing than a single miscalculation playing out on two fronts of the same decade.

The word that belongs here is betrayal rather than merely conflict, because the United Front was not two enemies finally coming to blows — it was two allies, bound by a common Soviet sponsor and a shared enemy in the warlords, in which one partner turned on the other the moment the partnership had served its purpose and the other had become, if anything, dangerously useful in its own right. China's twentieth century was not decided in a single April afternoon in Shanghai, but the war that would consume the next two decades of the country's history — nationalist against communist, interrupted by a Japanese invasion and resumed after it, on and off until 1949 — started its clock here. Both halves of this year's consolidation, Stalin's and Chiang's, ran on the identical logic: an alliance or a faction is useful exactly until it isn't, and the moment it isn't, dissolving it cleanly is never the preferred method. The preferred method is to make sure it cannot come back.

Coda

The crime itself was almost beside the point by August 1927, seven years and an entire global movement removed from it — a 1920 armed robbery at a shoe factory in South Braintree, Massachusetts, in which a paymaster and a guard were shot dead and roughly fifteen thousand dollars in payroll taken. Nicola Sacco and Bartolomeo Vanzetti, a shoe-factory worker and a fish peddler, both Italian immigrants, both self-declared anarchists, were arrested within weeks and convicted the following year on ballistic and eyewitness evidence that would not have survived serious scrutiny. Judge Webster Thayer, who presided over the trial, was reported by multiple witnesses to have made disparaging remarks about the defendants and about anarchists generally outside the courtroom during proceedings — the kind of detail that, on its own, might have been enough to overturn a conviction in a less politically charged case. It was not enough here. Appeal after appeal failed over the following seven years; a special advisory committee convened by Governor Alvan Fuller in 1927, headed by Harvard's president, reviewed the case and declined to recommend clemency.

The global response, by the time the executions neared, was enormous and entirely unable to change the outcome. Demonstrations turned to riots outside American embassies and consulates in Paris, London, Buenos Aires, Tokyo. Albert Einstein, George Bernard Shaw, and a long list of writers and public figures signed appeals for clemency. None of it moved the Massachusetts courts or Governor Fuller, who allowed the executions to proceed. Sacco and Vanzetti were put to death by electric chair at Charlestown State Prison just after midnight on August 23rd. Vanzetti's last public statement, given to a reporter before his execution, maintained his innocence and framed his death as something that might, eventually, be made to mean more than his life alone could have.

It is worth sitting, for a moment, with what kind of low this was, set against the other lows this year had offered. Stalin's purge and Chiang's massacre were violence exercised by power consolidating itself — men who already held the machinery of a state or a party turning that machinery against rivals who had, until very recently, held some version of power themselves. Sacco and Vanzetti held nothing of the kind. They were a shoe-factory worker and a fish peddler, and the apparatus that killed them was not seizing power from anyone. It was the ordinary apparatus of a state functioning exactly as designed, arriving at a verdict a considerable portion of its citizens appeared to have already settled on well before the evidence was fully weighed.

Massachusetts would not officially concede that the trial was unfair for another fifty years — a 1977 proclamation by Governor Michael Dukakis, long after it could do either man any good, and still carefully short of declaring them innocent. In August 1927, what the executions produced instead was protest without remedy: riots in cities that had no stake in Braintree, vigils held by people who never knew either man and never would, and a widening sense, well beyond Massachusetts, that something in the machinery had been wrong.
