Intro: Montevideo

The stadium was supposed to be ready for the opening match. Ground was broken in July 1929, and for eight months crews worked day and night through the southern winter, pouring a concrete bowl meant to hold ninety thousand people. Two of its stands were named Colombes and Amsterdam, after the stadiums where Uruguay had won Olympic gold in 1924 and 1928. The whole project was timed to a birthday. On July 18, 1930, Uruguay's constitution would turn one hundred, and the country meant to celebrate by hosting the first World Cup in a building named for the occasion: the Estadio Centenario.

Then it rained. It kept raining. When the tournament opened on July 13, the centerpiece of the celebration was still a construction site.

Uruguay had reasons to want an audience. It was small and prosperous, rich in beef and wool, and proud of being the exception on its continent. It had a functioning democracy, a precocious welfare state, and the best football team in the world. FIFA awarded it the tournament partly on the strength of a generous offer: Uruguay would pay the visiting teams' travel and lodging.

It was not enough to bring Europe. The crossing took roughly three weeks each way. Most players were amateurs or semi-professionals with employers who expected them back. The economies paying their wages had begun to slide. Italy, Spain, Germany and most of the continent declined. The British associations were not even members of FIFA. In the end four European teams made the trip. France and Belgium came largely at the urging of the Frenchman and the Belgian who ran FIFA. Yugoslavia came as the team of a kingdom that had renamed itself the year before, after its king dissolved parliament and took power into his own hands.

Romania came because of a different king. Carol II had given up his right to the throne in 1925 to live abroad with his mistress, leaving his young son to reign under a regency. In June 1930 he flew back to Bucharest and took the crown. Among his first priorities was getting a football team to South America. He picked the squad himself. When an oil company declined to release some of its employees for three months, the king reportedly threatened to shut it down. The company reconsidered.

On June 21 the Romanians boarded the Italian liner Conte Verde at Genoa. The French and Belgians joined along the way, and the Brazilians came aboard at Rio. Jules Rimet, FIFA's president, sailed with them. The trophy, a small gold figure of winged Victory, traveled in his luggage. The players trained on deck.

The football that followed was splendid and ridiculous in roughly equal measure. The first match was played at a modest club ground called Pocitos, because the real stadium was not done. France beat Mexico 4–1. Lucien Laurent scored the first goal in World Cup history in the nineteenth minute. The French goalkeeper was kicked in the jaw and could not continue. Substitutes were not allowed, so a midfielder put on the gloves. Across town, at the same hour, the United States beat Belgium.

Two days later France played Argentina. Down a goal, the French pushed forward, and with six minutes left their winger Marcel Langiller broke clear toward the Argentine goal. The Brazilian referee blew for full time. French players surrounded him. Fans poured onto the pitch, and mounted police had to clear it. The referee eventually conceded his watch was wrong and called the teams back from the dressing rooms to play the missing minutes. Nothing happened in them. Argentina won 1–0.

It did not get much tidier. Argentina's match against Mexico was refereed by the coach of Bolivia, who awarded three penalties. Bolivia and Brazil kicked off against each other both wearing white. Peru played Romania in front of a crowd generally put at about three hundred, the smallest in the tournament's history. A Peruvian became the first player ever sent off at a World Cup. In the semi-final against Argentina, as the story is usually told, the American trainer ran onto the field to treat an injured player, dropped his bottle of chloroform, and knocked himself out with the fumes.

The Centenario opened on July 18, the anniversary itself. Uruguay beat Peru 1–0 there. The crowds came after all, tens of thousands at a time. Both semi-finals ended 6–1, and the final put the hosts against their neighbors across the River Plate.

Argentina and Uruguay had met in the Olympic final two years earlier. Uruguay had won that one too. On July 30, Argentine supporters crossed the estuary from Buenos Aires by the boatload. Accounts say they were searched for weapons at the docks. The Belgian referee, John Langenus, reportedly agreed to take the match only with guarantees for his safety and a clear route to a ship afterward. More than sixty-eight thousand people filled the stadium.

Before kickoff the two sides could not agree on a ball. Each wanted its own. The version everyone repeats is that Langenus split the difference: Argentina's ball for the first half, Uruguay's for the second. It is a very tidy story, and the referee's own account does not quite support it. What is certain is the scoreline. Argentina led 2–1 at half-time. Uruguay scored three in the second half. The last goal came a minute from time from Héctor Castro, a forward who had lost most of his right arm in a childhood accident with a saw. Uruguay won 4–2.

The next day was declared a national holiday in Uruguay. In Buenos Aires a crowd stoned the Uruguayan consulate, and the two football federations broke off relations.

It had been a festival of a hundred years of national stability, and the guests had come from countries where stability was running out. Peru's government fell in August. Argentina's fell in September, five weeks after the final, to a general on horseback. Brazil's fell in October. Of the governments that sent teams to Montevideo, three did not survive the year.

The stadium, at least, was finished.

Part I: Passed the Worst

On March 7, 1930, Herbert Hoover told reporters that the worst effects of the crash on employment would be over within sixty days. The forces of recovery, he said, were gaining strength. Seasonal unemployment would ease with spring. The White House handed out the statement in mimeographed copies.

The market seemed to agree. After its November low, the Dow Jones had climbed all winter. On April 17 it closed at 294, about where it had been at the start of 1929. Shares that had been unloaded in panic were being bought back. Brokers who had survived the autumn began to talk about a correction rather than a collapse. Two weeks later, on May 1, Hoover addressed the United States Chamber of Commerce. The crash, he reminded the businessmen, had happened only six months before. "I am convinced," he said, "we have now passed the worst."

The Dow did not reach that April level again for many years. Through the spring and summer it slid, and kept sliding.

Hoover was not a fool, and he was not idle. He was an engineer and a famous organizer of relief, and he had reacted to the crash faster than any president before him had reacted to a panic. He had called industrialists to the White House and extracted promises to hold wages. He had asked governors and mayors to speed up public works. He believed the economy was fundamentally sound and that confidence was the scarce commodity. If the president said recovery was coming, people would act as if it were, and it would come. Talking down the crisis was not a lapse in his policy. It was the policy.

The phrase that came to stand for the whole attitude, that prosperity was just around the corner, is usually pinned on Hoover. He seems never to have said it in those words. It did not matter much. His administration produced so many versions of the sentiment that the public assigned him authorship anyway. One story, told years later and impossible to confirm, has a delegation coming to the White House in June to plead for a larger public works program. Hoover is supposed to have told them they had come sixty days too late, because the depression was over.

That same month he signed the tariff.

It had begun as a modest campaign promise. In 1928 Hoover had pledged to help farmers, who had been in trouble all through the boom, with higher duties on agricultural imports. Congress took the idea and ran. Over more than a year of bargaining, every industry with a lobbyist and every member with a factory in his district added items to the schedule. Senator Reed Smoot of Utah and Representative Willis Hawley of Oregon gave the bill their names. By the time it reached its final form, it raised duties on goods from sugar and wool to watches, chinaware and shoes.

The warnings were unusually loud. In May, 1,028 American economists signed a petition asking Hoover to veto it. They argued that it would raise prices for consumers, invite retaliation, and make it harder for foreign countries to repay their debts to the United States. Thomas Lamont of J.P. Morgan later said he had almost gone down on his knees to beg the president to veto it. Foreign governments had been sending formal protests since the previous year. Canada, the largest customer for American goods, did not wait for the bill to pass. In May it raised its own duties in anticipation.

The final bill cleared the Senate by two votes. Hoover had doubts. He signed it anyway, on June 17. His own party wanted it, and he believed a flexible provision in the law would let him correct the worst rates later. Retaliation followed through the rest of the year, from Europe and elsewhere. How much the tariff deepened the Depression is still argued. That it was passed over nearly every warning available is not.

On the ground, the numbers kept getting worse, though nobody was counting very well. The country had no reliable monthly unemployment figure. The census taken that spring produced a count so low that critics accused the administration of minimizing it. By most later estimates, the jobless total roughly doubled over the course of the year, to several million. A drought spread across the Midwest and the South that summer, among the worst on record, and crops burned in the fields.

In the autumn, apples appeared on the street corners of New York. A growers' association with a surplus came up with the idea of selling the fruit on credit to the unemployed, who resold it for a nickel apiece. Within weeks, thousands of men in overcoats stood beside crates on the sidewalks of Manhattan. It was meant as a dignified alternative to begging. It became one of the year's most recognizable images of how bad things were.

Above the apple sellers, the skyline kept rising. Both great projects of the year had been conceived at the top of the boom. The Chrysler Building opened on May 27. For a few months its steel spire made it the tallest building in the world. In March, a few blocks away, demolition crews had finished clearing the old Waldorf-Astoria, and construction on the Empire State Building began. It went up at a pace of several floors a week. Its backers had planned for a city that needed more office space. They were building it for a city that would not fill it for years.

The banks gave way last. Small rural banks had been failing all through the twenties, and they failed faster in 1930. In November the collapse of Caldwell and Company, a Nashville investment house that controlled the largest chain of banks in the South, pulled down dozens of institutions across Tennessee, Arkansas and Kentucky. Then the trouble came to New York.

The Bank of United States was a private commercial bank. Its name was a piece of marketing, and it had nothing to do with the government. It served mostly Jewish immigrant families in the Bronx, Brooklyn and the Lower East Side, and it had grown fast through mergers and real estate. On December 8, a rescue merger with other New York banks fell apart. Two days later, a businessman on Southern Boulevard in the Bronx was reportedly refused when he asked to sell his shares. By afternoon thousands of depositors were outside. The state closed the bank on December 11. It had held the savings of some four hundred thousand people. By deposits, it was the largest bank failure in American history to that point.

More than thirteen hundred banks suspended operations that year. In November, voters had already registered their own view of the recovery. The Republicans lost more than fifty seats in the House and eight in the Senate.

That year Edward Hopper painted Early Sunday Morning. It shows a row of storefronts on an empty street. The shades are half drawn and the sun is low. There is no one there.

Part II: Dizzy with Success

On June 17, 1930, the same day Hoover signed the tariff, the first tractor came off the assembly line at Stalingrad. The factory was the showpiece of the Five-Year Plan, a vast plant on the Volga meant to mechanize Soviet agriculture. It had been designed in Detroit by Albert Kahn, the architect of Henry Ford's factories. Its steel frame had been fabricated in the United States and shipped across the ocean for assembly. American engineers supervised the construction. The tractor itself was a copy of a McCormick-Deering model. Stalin sent a telegram of congratulations. For months afterward, the plant could barely produce anything.

The tractors were meant for the collective farms. By the time the first one rolled out, the collective farms had nearly collapsed.

The decision to collectivize Soviet agriculture by force had been announced at the end of 1929. In 1930 it was carried out. Through January and February, party activists, secret police detachments and brigades of urban workers fanned out into the villages. Peasant households were told to surrender their land, their tools and their animals to the new collective farms. Those who resisted were labeled kulaks, the supposedly wealthy peasants the regime had pledged to eliminate as a class. In practice the label went to anyone who argued, anyone who had a slightly better house, anyone a neighbor disliked. One secret police report from 1930 found that barely half of the "kulaks" in one district met even the official definition.

The numbers moved fast. In October 1929, about four percent of peasant households belonged to collective farms. By March 1930, according to official figures, the share was close to sixty percent. In the black earth region, the most fertile land in the country, it was over eighty.

The peasants did not accept this quietly. Rather than hand their animals over to the collective, millions of them slaughtered their cattle, pigs and horses, ate what they could and sold the rest. Village women, who calculated that the authorities would hesitate to beat them, blocked the carts that came to take the livestock away. Officials who arrived to seize grain were attacked. Some were killed. By the secret police's own count, there were more than thirteen thousand mass disturbances in the countryside that year. A sizable share of them were in Ukraine.

On March 2, Pravda published an article by Stalin under the title "Dizzy with Success." Its argument was simple. Collectivization had been a triumph, but some local officials, carried away by the movement's success, had gone too far. They had forced peasants into collectives against their will. They had seized chickens and cottages that were never meant to be seized. They had pulled down church bells. This was a distortion of party policy, which had always held that collectivization must be voluntary.

The article did not mention who had set the quotas, or who had ordered the speed. The local officials it blamed had been acting under instructions from Moscow and under threat of punishment if they fell short. Now they found themselves denounced as the cause of the trouble. The peasants read the article differently. Many took it as permission to leave. Within weeks they began walking out of the collectives, taking back whatever animals had survived. By June, the share of households in collective farms had fallen to under a quarter. In the black earth region it dropped below twenty percent.

The retreat was partial and it was temporary. The deportations, slowed in the spring, did not stop. Families classified as kulaks were loaded onto freight trains and sent to the Urals, Siberia, Kazakhstan and the far north. Many were dumped in forests or on open steppe and told to build settlements for themselves. In 1930 alone the secret police deported hundreds of thousands of people, and its own records show roughly twenty thousand death sentences handed down by its tribunals. In April, the secret police created a central administration for its growing network of labor camps. By the end of the year it was known by its acronym: GULAG.

Soviet culture had to absorb the campaign as it happened. In April, the Ukrainian director Oleksandr Dovzhenko released Earth, a silent film about collectivization in a Ukrainian village. It was meant to be on the regime's side. A young activist brings the first tractor to his village. When the radiator runs dry on the road, the peasants refill it by urinating into it. Later, walking home alone at dusk, he dances down a dirt road out of pure happiness and is shot by a kulak. The film lingers on apples in the rain, on wheat, on the faces of the old. Days before its release, Demyan Bedny, a poet close to Stalin, attacked it over three columns in Izvestia as defeatist and counterrevolutionary. Dovzhenko was made to cut it. He wrote later that he went gray overnight.

On April 14, Vladimir Mayakovsky shot himself in his Moscow apartment. He was thirty-six. He had spent more than a decade as the loudest poet of the revolution, writing slogans, advertisements and agitprop. That winter, his new satirical play about Soviet bureaucracy had been badly received. An exhibition marking twenty years of his work had been largely ignored by the officials he had hoped would come. He left a note asking that no one gossip about his death. The note blamed no one.

In the summer the party held its Sixteenth Congress. The opposition was gone, its former leaders reduced to public recantation. The slogan of the hour was that the Five-Year Plan would be fulfilled in four. That autumn the weather cooperated, and the harvest of 1930 was excellent. It was taken as proof that the method worked. The second collectivization drive began after the harvest, and this time there was no retreat.

If the plan was faltering, someone had to be responsible. On November 25, eight engineers and economists went on trial in the Hall of Columns in Moscow. They were accused of forming a secret Industrial Party, sabotaging Soviet industry since 1926, and conspiring with émigré capitalists and the French general staff to prepare a foreign invasion. All eight confessed. The chief defendant, the heat engineer Leonid Ramzin, described meetings in Paris and London in elaborate detail and ended his testimony by asking to be shot. The proceedings were filmed.

There was a problem with the script. Two of the émigré industrialists Ramzin said he had plotted with in 1928 had been dead and buried in France for years. Russian émigrés in Paris pointed this out. The prosecution adjusted its account and carried on.

On December 7, five defendants were sentenced to death. The next day the sentences were commuted to ten years. Ramzin was allowed to keep working in prison, on boilers.

Part III: A Pinch of Salt

Salt was an odd choice for a revolution. When Gandhi announced early in 1930 that civil disobedience would begin with the salt laws, many in his own party were baffled. The Indian National Congress had just declared that its goal was complete independence. Its leaders had expected a campaign against land revenue, or a boycott of the legislatures, or something with obvious political weight. Salt seemed trivial. The British agreed. The viceroy's government considered the plan faintly ridiculous and decided not to arrest Gandhi. An arrest, it reasoned, would only make him a martyr. A campaign about salt would fizzle on its own.

Gandhi had chosen salt precisely because it was trivial. Every Indian ate it. The government held a monopoly on its manufacture and taxed it, so that even the poorest family in the poorest village paid the Raj every time it seasoned a meal. Along much of India's coastline, salt formed naturally where seawater evaporated. Gathering it was a crime. No one needed a lecture on constitutional theory to understand that.

On March 2, he wrote to the viceroy, Lord Irwin. The letter opened "Dear Friend." It set out the case against British rule in India, named the salt tax among its cruelties, and explained exactly what Gandhi intended to do and when. If his demands were not met, he would set out in nine days to break the salt laws. Irwin's reply came through a secretary. It regretted that Mr. Gandhi was contemplating a course of action clearly bound to involve a violation of the law. "On bended knee I asked for bread," Gandhi said, "and I have received stone instead."

He left his ashram at Sabarmati at dawn on March 12, with seventy-eight followers. He was sixty years old. The destination was Dandi, a village on the Gujarat coast about 240 miles away. The route was published in advance. The marchers walked through the hot season for twenty-four days, sleeping in the open and stopping at village after village, where Gandhi spoke against the salt tax and against the drink and the foreign cloth he considered tools of the same subjugation. Officials along the way resigned their posts. Crowds lined the roads. Foreign correspondents followed, and so did newsreel crews from three Bombay film companies. The column of seventy-eight became thousands.

On the morning of April 6, at Dandi, Gandhi walked down to the shore and picked up a lump of natural salt from the mud. That was all. It was enough. Within days, people across India were boiling seawater in pans, gathering salt from the flats, and selling it openly in the streets. The movement spread beyond salt to a boycott of British cloth, the picketing of liquor shops, and refusals to pay taxes. Women, who had rarely taken part in public protest, joined in large numbers, and took over much of the picketing.

The government's patience lasted roughly a month. Then it began filling the jails. By the end of the year, by most counts, some sixty thousand people had been imprisoned. Jawaharlal Nehru was arrested in April. Gandhi was arrested in the early hours of May 5, in a mango grove near Dandi, after writing to the viceroy again to announce his next target. He was taken without charge or trial, under a regulation dating from 1827, so that no courtroom could give him a platform.

The next target went ahead without him. It was the government salt works at Dharasana, about 150 miles north of Bombay, guarded by barbed wire and several hundred police under British officers. On May 21, about twenty-five hundred volunteers marched on the works. They were led by Sarojini Naidu, a poet and a former president of the Congress, who reminded them before they set out that they must not use violence under any circumstances. They came forward in columns. The police met them with steel-tipped lathis, heavy bamboo staves. The volunteers did not raise their arms to defend themselves. They went down, were carried off on stretchers, and the next column stepped forward. This went on for hours.

The only foreign correspondent present was Webb Miller of the United Press. He watched the volunteers fall like ninepins and counted the wounded in a makeshift hospital: more than three hundred, by his count, and two dead. The authorities in India held up his dispatch. When it finally got out, it ran in newspapers around the world. Few events of the year did as much damage to Britain's claim that it governed India for India's benefit.

Nonviolence was not universal, and neither was restraint. In April, in Chittagong in Bengal, a group of armed revolutionaries under a schoolteacher named Surya Sen raided the police and auxiliary armouries and held out in the hills before being hunted down. On April 23, on the North-West Frontier, the British arrested Abdul Ghaffar Khan, a Pashtun leader who had organized a nonviolent movement among a people famous for their warriors. Crowds gathered in Peshawar's Qissa Khwani Bazaar, the old storytellers' market. Armored cars drove into the crowd. Troops opened fire. The number of dead is still disputed, ranging from a few dozen in official accounts to several hundred in nationalist ones.

Two platoons of the Royal Garhwal Rifles, a Hindu regiment with a distinguished record in the Great War, refused to go into Peshawar to fire on the Muslim crowds. Their leader, a non-commissioned officer named Chandra Singh, was court-martialed with his men and sentenced to a long prison term. To the authorities, the refusal was more alarming than the riot. The Indian Army was the foundation of British rule, and part of it had declined to shoot.

That autumn, Time magazine prepared to name Gandhi its Man of the Year for 1930. He was in Yeravda prison.

The British answer to the crisis was a conference. On November 12, King George V opened the first Round Table Conference in the House of Lords. Its purpose was to work out a new constitution for India. The delegates were princes from the princely states, representatives of the Muslim League and other communities, liberals, and B. R. Ambedkar, speaking for the untouchables. The Indian National Congress, the organization that had just mobilized India against the Raj, was not there. Its leaders were in jail. The conference talked for weeks about federation and safeguards, and adjourned in January with much left to settle.

On December 29, in Allahabad, the poet Muhammad Iqbal gave the presidential address at the annual session of the All-India Muslim League. The session was sparsely attended. Iqbal argued that India's Muslims were a distinct people, and proposed that the Muslim-majority provinces of the northwest be joined into a single state, inside the British Empire or outside it. Few people took much notice.

Part IV: The Bill Comes Due

On the morning of September 6, five weeks after Argentina lost the World Cup final, military planes flew low over Buenos Aires and dropped leaflets announcing a revolution. Below them, a few hundred cadets from the national military college and scraps of other units marched on the center of the city behind a retired general named José Félix Uriburu. There was a brief exchange of fire near the Congress, and two cadets were killed. Most of the army stayed in its barracks and waited to see what would happen. By evening the government had fallen. Crowds celebrated in the Plaza de Mayo. Others broke into the president's house and threw his belongings into the street.

The president was not there. Hipólito Yrigoyen was seventy-eight years old and had handed his powers to his vice president, citing illness, the day before. He resigned in La Plata. It was the first successful military coup in Argentina since the constitution of 1853. That year it was not unusual.

Latin America's economies had grown rich in the 1920s by selling a few commodities to the industrial world: Argentine beef and wheat, Brazilian coffee, Peruvian copper and cotton, Bolivian tin, Cuban and Dominican sugar. Much of the rest had been financed with American loans, floated on Wall Street during the boom and spent on railways, ports, boulevards and, frequently, the friends of whoever was in power. In 1929 the loans stopped. In 1930 the prices collapsed. Governments that depended on export taxes and foreign credit discovered they had neither. Salaries for soldiers and civil servants went unpaid. Currencies sagged. The economic crisis became a political one almost everywhere at once.

Mexico began the year with a warning. On February 5, Pascual Ortiz Rubio was inaugurated as president and was shot in the face by a young man in the crowd as he left the ceremony. He survived. Real power remained with the former president Plutarco Elías Calles, the self-styled Jefe Máximo, who had picked him. A popular joke held that the president lived in the castle at Chapultepec, but the man who gave the orders lived across the street.

The Dominican Republic went next, and more quietly. In February, a revolt broke out against the ailing president, Horacio Vásquez. The army's commander, Rafael Trujillo, declared his troops neutral, which in practice meant he let the rebels win. In May he stood for president. His opponents withdrew after a campaign of intimidation. The official count reportedly exceeded the number of eligible voters. He was inaugurated in August. Two weeks later a hurricane leveled much of Santo Domingo and killed thousands of people. The new president ran the reconstruction under emergency powers.

In Bolivia, falling tin prices and a president's attempt to extend his term brought students and soldiers into the streets in June, and a military junta took over. In Peru, Augusto Leguía had ruled for eleven years. He had modernized Lima on borrowed money and had himself reelected without serious opposition. On August 22, a lieutenant colonel named Luis Sánchez Cerro rose with the garrison at Arequipa. He announced his revolt partly by radio, then a novelty. Within three days Leguía had resigned. He tried to escape by sea aboard a navy cruiser. The ship was ordered back to port, and the man who had run Peru since 1919 was taken to prison.

In Argentina, the collapse had a particular texture. Yrigoyen was the first president elected under universal male suffrage. He had come back for a second term in 1928 with a large majority. He was also aging, reclusive and famously unwilling to delegate. He saw visitors for hours on minor matters while the business of government piled up. As the crisis deepened his opponents, in the press, in the conservative parties and in his own divided party, painted a picture of a senile old man cut off from reality. One story that went around Buenos Aires in the weeks before the coup had his aides printing a special newspaper for him alone, filled with good news. No evidence for it has ever turned up. That it was so widely believed did his government nearly as much harm as if it had been true.

The coup's legal sequel was less dramatic and lasted longer. On September 10, the Supreme Court issued a formal statement recognizing Uriburu's provisional government. It reasoned that the new authorities had possession of the military and police force needed to keep order, and that their title therefore could not be challenged in court. In support, the court cited, among other authorities, an American treatise on de facto officers. The court reserved the right to hold the new government to its promise to respect the constitution. Uriburu dissolved Congress, declared a state of siege, and placed the provinces under federal control.

Brazil's collapse was the largest. The country produced most of the world's coffee, and for decades the federal government had kept prices up by buying and storing the surplus. When world demand fell away in 1929, the warehouses were already full. Prices fell by half and kept falling. Planters begged for credit. President Washington Luís, determined to protect the currency, refused.

He also broke the old arrangement under which the presidency alternated between the coffee state of São Paulo and the dairy state of Minas Gerais. Instead of handing over to a mineiro, he named another paulista, Júlio Prestes, as his successor. The opposition rallied around Getúlio Vargas, the governor of Rio Grande do Sul in the far south. In the election of March 1, the official results gave Prestes a comfortable victory. Few believed the official results. In July, Vargas's running mate, João Pessoa, was shot dead in a pastry shop in Recife. The motive appears to have been a personal feud, but the opposition turned him into a martyr.

On October 3, Rio Grande do Sul rose in revolt. The rising spread north and through Minas Gerais, while the government's forces and its credit drained away together. On October 24, the senior generals in Rio de Janeiro deposed Washington Luís themselves, rather than wait for the rebels to arrive. Prestes, the president-elect, never took office. He went into exile.

Vargas came north by train. On November 3, he was sworn in as head of a provisional government. The gaúcho soldiers who had come with him from the southern plains had promised that they would tie their horses to the obelisk at the end of Rio's grandest avenue. They did.

Not every government fell. In Cuba, where the price of sugar had collapsed, President Gerardo Machado had already extended his own term and held on by force. On September 30, police fired on a student demonstration in Havana, and a law student named Rafael Trejo was killed. That spring, a Cuban dance orchestra had played a song called "El Manisero" at a theater on Broadway. The record, sold in the United States as "The Peanut Vendor," was a sensation. By the end of 1930, Americans who had never heard of Machado were dancing the rumba.

Coda: Next Sunday

On September 7, at a small conference on the philosophy of mathematics in Königsberg, a twenty-four-year-old Austrian logician named Kurt Gödel made a brief remark during a closing discussion. He said he had found a way to show that any consistent formal system rich enough to contain arithmetic would include true statements it could not prove. Almost no one in the room understood what he meant. John von Neumann, who did, took him aside afterward. The published summary of the discussion left Gödel's comment out.

The next day, in the same city, David Hilbert, the most famous mathematician in the world, accepted the honorary citizenship of his hometown. He used the occasion to defend his life's project, the effort to place all of mathematics on a complete and certain foundation. He rejected the old idea that there were things science could never know. Afterward he repeated the end of his speech on the local radio. It closed with six words: Wir müssen wissen. Wir werden wissen. We must know. We will know.

Germany voted six days later.

The election had not been necessary. In March, the last parliamentary majority of the Weimar Republic had collapsed over a dispute about unemployment insurance. President Hindenburg appointed Heinrich Brüning, a Catholic economist and war veteran, as chancellor, with the understanding that he would govern by emergency decree if the Reichstag would not cooperate. Brüning cut spending and raised taxes in the face of rising unemployment. When the Reichstag voted down his decrees in July, he had it dissolved. That same summer the last French troops left the Rhineland, years ahead of schedule. The country celebrated. Brüning expected the voters to reward the government for steady hands.

On the evening of September 14, a sixty-four-year-old Berliner named Heinz Arzt sat down and wrote a short account of election day. He noted the posters and leaflets littering the streets and wondered who would clean them up. He did not take the National Socialists very seriously. He ended with a prediction: "And next Sunday, no one will be thinking of the election anymore."

Turnout was the highest in more than a decade. The Nazis, who had won twelve seats in 1928, won 107. More than six million people voted for them. They were now the second-largest party in the Reichstag. The Communists gained too. Foreign lenders, who had kept the German economy afloat through the twenties, began withdrawing their money.

Eleven days after the election, Adolf Hitler appeared in the witness box of the Reich Supreme Court in Leipzig. Three young army lieutenants were on trial for treason, accused of spreading Nazi cells within the Reichswehr, and the defense had called him to testify about the party's aims. Under oath, Hitler swore that his movement would come to power only by legal means. In the same testimony, he said that once it did, a new court would try the men responsible for the revolution of 1918, and heads would roll. The Berlin stock exchange fell. Officials, diplomats and much of the press focused on the first part of his answer. It was what they wanted to hear.

When the new Reichstag opened in October, the 107 Nazi deputies arrived in brown uniforms, in defiance of a ban on political uniforms. They marched in together and answered the roll call with "Heil."

On December 5, Universal's film of All Quiet on the Western Front, Erich Maria Remarque's novel of a German schoolboy who goes to the trenches and does not come back, played at the Mozart Hall in Berlin. Partway through the screening, Nazis who had bought blocks of tickets began shouting at the screen. They threw stink bombs from the balcony, scattered sneezing powder, and released white mice into the aisles. The show was stopped. Goebbels, who had organized the evening, recorded his satisfaction in his diary. For several nights the disturbances spread to other theaters.

On December 11, the national film censorship board banned the picture. It found that the film was a threat to Germany's reputation abroad.
