Under Strain

Intro: The Nucleus

In January 1932, word reached the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge of a strange result from Paris. Irène Curie and Frédéric Joliot had been bombarding beryllium with alpha particles from a powerful polonium source. The beryllium gave off a radiation so penetrating it passed through lead. When the Joliot-Curies placed paraffin wax in its path, the radiation knocked protons out of the wax at tremendous speed.

They concluded the radiation was light: gamma rays of extraordinary energy. It was an awkward answer. A proton is nearly two thousand times heavier than an electron, and the energy needed to send one flying that way strained belief. When James Chadwick brought the paper to Ernest Rutherford, Rutherford, by Chadwick's account, said simply that he did not believe it.

Rutherford had a reason for his doubt. In 1920 he had proposed that the nucleus might contain a neutral particle, something with the weight of a proton and no charge. Chadwick had spent much of the following decade looking for it and finding nothing. Now he suspected that Paris had found it without knowing.

Chadwick was not a man given to haste. He had spent the First World War interned in a camp outside Berlin, where he had gone to study physics on the eve of the war, and he had learned patience there. But he worked quickly now. His apparatus was modest: a small cylinder holding a polonium source and a beryllium target, with a detector to catch whatever came out. He aimed the radiation at hydrogen, then at helium, nitrogen and other light elements, and measured how hard each was struck. Light could not account for the numbers. A particle could.

After about two weeks he wrote to Nature. The letter was dated February 17 and ran barely more than a column. Its title was careful: "Possible Existence of a Neutron." The radiation was not light at all. It was a particle with roughly the mass of a proton and no electrical charge. Because it carried no charge, nothing in the atom pushed it away. It could pass through the electron cloud and into the nucleus itself. By May, Chadwick had dropped the hedge. His next paper was called "The Existence of a Neutron."

The atom had been understood as two particles, the proton and the electron. Now there were three. Within months, Werner Heisenberg in Leipzig and Dmitri Ivanenko in Leningrad had independently proposed what the neutron implied. The nucleus was not a single solid kernel. It was a cluster of protons and neutrons, packed together in a space too small to imagine.

Elsewhere in the Cavendish, two younger men were trying to get into that space by force. John Cockcroft and Ernest Walton had spent years building a machine that eventually filled a room. A tower of rectifiers and capacitors stacked voltage upon voltage. At its center ran a glass tube some eight feet long, emptied of air, down which protons could be driven at speed.

Their theory came from George Gamow, a young Soviet physicist who had passed through Cambridge a few years before. Classical physics said a proton would need enormous energy to break into a nucleus against the repulsion of its charge. Quantum mechanics said otherwise. A particle could sometimes tunnel through a barrier it lacked the energy to climb. The wall did not have to be battered down. Now and then, something would simply appear on the other side.

On April 14, Walton placed a thin layer of lithium in the path of the beam. Then he crawled into a small darkened hut at the foot of the tube, built in part from a tea chest, and looked through a microscope at a fluorescent screen. It was sparkling. Each flash was a helium nucleus. A lithium nucleus had absorbed a proton and burst into two pieces. Cockcroft came to look, then Rutherford. Within days the result was in a letter to Nature.

One element had been turned into another by a machine two young men had built. The fragments flew apart with more energy than the proton had carried in. The surplus was consistent with the tiny amount of mass that had gone missing, as Einstein's equation of 1905 said it should be. The phrase that stuck was that they had split the atom. That was not quite right. Rutherford himself had transmuted nitrogen with natural alpha particles more than a decade earlier. What was new was that the bullet had been made by machine.

In August, the third discovery came from six thousand miles away. In Pasadena, a 26-year-old Caltech physicist named Carl Anderson, the son of Swedish immigrants, was studying cosmic rays. He had built a cloud chamber wrapped in a giant electromagnet. Charged particles passing through it left trails of droplets, and the magnet bent those trails one way for positive charges and the other for negative. To tell which way a particle was moving, he set a lead plate across the middle of the chamber. A particle lost energy crossing the plate, and its path curved more sharply afterward.

On August 2 he photographed a track that came up from below, crossed the plate, and curved the way a positive charge should. It could not be a proton. It was far too light. It had the mass of an electron.

The theorist Paul Dirac had predicted such a particle from pure mathematics: an electron with its charge reversed. Anderson was not looking for it and did not frame his result that way. Others had seen tracks like it and explained them away. Even Anderson's own mentor, Robert Millikan, had argued that such tracks were ordinary electrons moving the other way. Anderson published a brief note in Science in September, to some skepticism. By the end of the year, work at Cambridge had confirmed it. Matter had a mirror image. When a particle met its opposite, both vanished in a flash of energy.

By December the nucleus looked like a different object. It was not a solid core but a crowd of particles. That raised a problem no one could solve. Protons carry positive charge. Like charges repel, and at the distances inside a nucleus the repulsion is ferocious. By every force then known, the nucleus should have flown apart. It did not. Something was holding it together, something stronger than electricity, working only across the smallest distances. It had no name and no theory.

At the heart of every atom, then, sat a knot of forces pulling against each other, held in place by something no one understood. It had always been so. What changed in 1932 was that people noticed the strain, and learned, in a modest way, how to break it.

Part I: The Orbits

In January 1931, El Salvador held what was widely considered the first free presidential election in its history. Arturo Araujo, a reformist landowner who had studied in England, won it. He lasted eleven months. In December the army removed him, and his vice president and minister of war, General Maximiliano Hernández Martínez, took his place. Martínez was a theosophist and a believer in reincarnation. He is said to have remarked that killing an ant was a greater crime than killing a man, because a man is reborn and an ant is not.

El Salvador lived on coffee. Coffee was almost all of what it sold abroad, and after 1929 the price collapsed. Planters cut wages on the estates, then cut workers. The pain fell hardest in the west, in the coffee highlands around Sonsonate and Ahuachapán, where most of the population was indigenous Pipil. Two generations earlier, liberal reforms had abolished the communal lands their villages held. Much of that land had become coffee estates. Many of the men who had once farmed it now picked coffee on it for wages, and in 1931 the wages stopped.

The Communist Party of El Salvador was small, young and energetic. Its best-known figure was Agustín Farabundo Martí, who had served as Augusto Sandino's secretary in the Nicaraguan mountains before breaking with him. In the municipal elections of early January 1932, the party won in several western towns. The government refused to recognize the results. The party began planning a rising.

The army knew. On January 18, Martí and two student leaders, Alfonso Luna and Mario Zapata, were arrested and their plans seized. The rising went ahead anyway on the night of January 22. Its leaders were already in jail, and its strength came less from the party than from indigenous communities with their own grievances and their own leaders. The most prominent was Feliciano Ama, of Izalco. Peasants armed mostly with machetes took Izalco, Juayúa, Nahuizalco and other towns. They killed perhaps several dozen people, perhaps around a hundred. Within three days the army had retaken every town.

Then the killing began. For weeks, soldiers and civilian militias moved through the western departments. They shot men for carrying machetes, which every farm worker carried. They shot men for wearing indigenous dress, or for being indigenous. Prisoners dug the trenches they were then shot into. On January 28, Feliciano Ama was hanged in front of the church at Izalco. On February 1, Martí, Luna and Zapata were shot by firing squad. Nobody counted the dead. Estimates run from about 10,000 to 40,000, in a country of perhaps a million and a half.

Salvadorans call it La Matanza, the slaughter. Its aftermath is harder to measure than its toll. Many indigenous families stopped wearing traditional dress and stopped speaking Nahuat where others could hear. Historians disagree over how much of that loss the massacre caused and how much it hastened. What is not in dispute is the political result. Martínez, who had been an uncertain usurper in December, was now the man who had saved the republic from communism. He ruled until 1944.

Two thousand miles to the south, two of the poorest countries in the hemisphere were arguing over a wasteland. The Chaco Boreal is a vast plain of thorn scrub between the Andes and the Paraguay River. In the rainy season much of it floods. In the dry season it is close to desert, and water decides everything. Bolivia and Paraguay had disputed it for half a century. Both had reasons to care more than the land itself justified. Bolivia had lost its Pacific coast to Chile in the 1880s and saw the Chaco as a route to the sea along the Paraguay River. Paraguay had been devastated in the war of the 1860s against Brazil, Argentina and Uruguay, losing territory and a large share of its people. It was not willing to lose more. There were also rumors of oil. Standard Oil held concessions in eastern Bolivia, and the idea that the war was fought by oil companies through proxies became popular almost at once. Historians have largely discounted it, though it has never quite gone away.

Both countries had spent years building small forts, called fortines, deeper into the disputed land. At dawn on June 15, 1932, eighteen Bolivian soldiers occupied a Paraguayan outpost on the shore of Lake Pitiantuta. Its garrison of six fled into the scrub. Paraguay retook the position in July. Bolivia's president, Daniel Salamanca, a stern civilian with a weak hold on his own politics, ordered retaliation. Bolivian troops seized three Paraguayan forts, Corrales, Toledo and Boquerón.

Paraguay mobilized. Its forces were commanded by José Félix Estigarribia, a lieutenant colonel trained at the French war college. In September he surrounded Boquerón, which sat on one of the few reliable water sources in the region. The Bolivian garrison held for three weeks, short of water, food and ammunition, while relief columns failed to break through. On September 29 the survivors surrendered. There were about 240 of them, most of them wounded. Each side suffered thousands of casualties. In La Paz, crowds rioted when the news came. Salamanca brought back Hans Kundt, a German general who had trained Bolivia's army before the Great War, to take command. Neither country had formally declared war. Both were now fighting one.

Nine days after the Bolivians took Pitiantuta, the absolute monarchy of Siam ended in a single morning.

In April 1932, the Chakri dynasty had celebrated its 150th anniversary. King Prajadhipok opened a new bridge across the Chao Phraya river in Bangkok, crowned by a statue of the dynasty's founder. The celebrations were more modest than planned. The Depression had wrecked the price of rice, Siam's chief export, and the government had cut civil-service salaries and dismissed officials to balance its budget. The cuts fell on the educated men who ran the state, and many of them had been abroad.

Some of them had been plotting for five years. In 1927, a small group of Siamese students and young officers in Paris had founded what became the People's Party. They called themselves the Promoters. Among them were Pridi Banomyong, a law student, and Plaek Phibunsongkhram, an artillery officer. Back in Siam they recruited senior officers, the most important of them Colonel Phraya Phahon. Prajadhipok himself had considered granting a constitution, and had had drafts prepared, before his advisers talked him out of it.

Before dawn on June 24, troops gathered in the Royal Plaza in Bangkok, many of them apparently believing they had been called out for an exercise. The Promoters seized key buildings and arrested senior princes, including the king's powerful uncle Prince Paribatra, holding them in the Ananda Samakhom Throne Hall. The king was not in the capital. He was at his seaside palace at Hua Hin, called Klai Kangwon, which means "far from worries." The Promoters sent him an ultimatum. He could stay on as a constitutional monarch, or they would find another arrangement.

Prajadhipok replied that he did not want bloodshed. He took the royal train back to Bangkok. On June 26 he received the leaders of the coup and signed an amnesty for everyone involved. On June 27 he signed a provisional constitution. A permanent one followed on December 10. The Chakri dynasty had lasted 150 years as an absolute monarchy and two months more. Almost no one was killed. Prajadhipok remained on the throne, though not for long. Within three years he would abdicate.

In India, the year began with Gandhi in jail. He had returned from the Round Table Conference in London at the end of December 1931 with nothing to show for it. On January 4, 1932, he was arrested. The government outlawed the Congress, and civil disobedience resumed. By the spring, tens of thousands of people were in prison.

The question that would define his year had been raised in London. There, B. R. Ambedkar had demanded separate electorates for the untouchables, whom the British called the Depressed Classes. Ambedkar was himself born untouchable, a Mahar from the Bombay Presidency, and held doctorates from Columbia and the London School of Economics. His argument was simple. In any general electorate, caste Hindus would choose the untouchables' representatives for them. Only a separate electorate would let untouchables choose their own. Gandhi accepted separate electorates for Muslims and Sikhs. He would not accept them for untouchables, whom he insisted were Hindus. Splitting them off, he said, would divide Hinduism itself. He told the conference he would resist it with his life.

On August 16, Ramsay MacDonald announced the Communal Award, which gave the Depressed Classes their separate electorate. From his cell in Yerwada prison near Poona, Gandhi wrote that he would fast unto death unless it was withdrawn. The fast began on September 20. He was 63.

The pressure fell on one man. If Gandhi died, Ambedkar would be blamed, and he feared that untouchable villages across India would pay for it. Negotiators gathered at Poona and shuttled between Ambedkar and the prison. Ambedkar bargained hard. On September 24, he and Madan Mohan Malaviya, speaking for caste Hindus, signed what became known as the Poona Pact. Gandhi did not sign it. The separate electorate was abandoned. In its place, untouchables received 148 reserved seats in the provincial legislatures, more than twice the 71 the British had offered, to be filled through a joint electorate. On September 26, Gandhi broke his fast with a glass of orange juice handed to him by his wife, Kasturba.

Across India, some temples opened their doors to untouchables for the first time. Gandhi threw himself into a campaign against untouchability. Ambedkar had won more seats than the British had given him. He had lost the principle he had come for: the right of untouchables to choose their representatives without caste Hindus deciding the outcome. He would spend years saying that the pact had been signed under duress, by a man who had been given a choice between his demand and another man's life.

Part II: Negative Energy

In the first two weeks of March 1932, three things happened that seemed to have nothing to do with each other.

On the night of March 1, someone set a ladder against the Lindbergh house near Hopewell, New Jersey, climbed into the nursery, and took the twenty-month-old son of the most famous man in America. A ransom note was left on the windowsill. On March 7, several thousand unemployed workers marched from Detroit toward Henry Ford's River Rouge plant in Dearborn, and the Dearborn police and Ford's private guards shot them. On March 12, in his apartment in Paris, the Swedish financier Ivar Kreuger shot himself through the heart.

Kreuger was known as the Match King. Over the previous decade he had built a business of startling ambition. He lent money to governments short of cash, among them France, Germany, Poland and a dozen others, and in return they granted his company a national monopoly on matches. By the late 1920s he controlled a majority of the world's match production, along with mines, banks, timber and telephone companies. American investors had poured money into his securities, which paid steady and generous dividends through the first years of the Depression. After his death the auditors went through his books. They found the dividends had been paid out of new money. Assets had been invented. Among his papers were Italian government bonds worth many millions of dollars, forged. Kreuger's empire turned out to be among the largest frauds in history to that point.

In April it was Chicago's turn. Samuel Insull had begun as Thomas Edison's secretary and had become the most powerful utilities magnate in America. His companies supplied electricity and gas across thirty-odd states. They were organized as holding companies stacked on holding companies, each controlling the one below with a sliver of its stock, so that a small investment at the top commanded an enormous structure beneath. In a falling market the structure worked in reverse. Middle West Utilities went into receivership in April, and Insull's other companies followed. Hundreds of thousands of small investors, many of whom had bought the stock because Insull's own companies sold it to them, lost their savings. In the autumn Insull left the country for Europe.

Kreuger and Insull had been two of the great reassuring figures of the 1920s. Their collapse suggested that some of the decade's prosperity had been built on nothing at all.

By the summer of 1932 roughly a quarter of the American workforce had no job. Industrial production was about half what it had been in 1929. Banks were still failing by the hundreds. The shantytowns at the edges of cities had a name now, Hoovervilles. Herbert Hoover had not been idle. In January he had signed the Reconstruction Finance Corporation into being, and it was lending hundreds of millions of dollars to banks, railroads and insurance companies. To millions of people out of work, it looked like help for everyone but them. On July 8, the Dow Jones Industrial Average closed at 41.22, about 89 percent below its peak of September 1929. It would never close that low again. No one knew it at the time.

Detroit had been hit as hard as any city in the country. The auto industry's sales had collapsed. Ford had cut wages and then workers, and Michigan had some 400,000 people out of work. The march of March 7 was organized by the Detroit Unemployed Council and the Communist Party. The marchers had a list of demands for Ford: jobs, food for the families of laid-off workers, coal for the winter, and the right to organize. The morning was bitterly cold. Somewhere between 3,000 and 5,000 people walked the mile from the Detroit line toward the plant.

At the Dearborn line, police fired tear gas. The marchers threw stones and frozen mud. As they neared the factory gates, the police and Ford's Service Department, the company's private security force, opened fire. Three marchers were killed. The organizers called a retreat. Harry Bennett, Ford's security chief, drove up and fired into the crowd, and his car was battered with stones. He was taken to the hospital. More shooting followed, including from a machine gun. A sixteen-year-old named Joe Bussell was killed. Dozens were wounded. A fifth marcher, Curtis Williams, died of his injuries in August. No police officer was shot. No one was ever charged. On March 12, the day Kreuger died in Paris, tens of thousands of people followed the coffins of the four dead down Woodward Avenue under red banners.

Six weeks later, Diego Rivera arrived in Detroit with his wife, Frida Kahlo. William Valentiner, the director of the Detroit Institute of Arts, had commissioned him to paint the museum's central courtyard, and Edsel Ford, Henry Ford's son, had put up the money. Rivera spent the spring and summer at the River Rouge, the same plant the marchers had tried to reach. He sketched its blast furnaces, its stamping presses and its assembly lines, and the men who worked them. He was captivated by it. In July he began painting, and he worked in fresco through the winter. The murals that resulted are a vast hymn to industry and to the workers who made it run: huge, crowded and alive. That summer, at the Henry Ford Hospital, Kahlo lost a pregnancy. She painted that too.

The Lindbergh case consumed the country. Charles Lindbergh had been the great American hero of 1927, and now his son was gone. Thousands of tips came in. Al Capone, newly in prison for tax evasion, offered to help find the child if he were released. In April a retired schoolteacher acting as go-between handed $50,000 in ransom to a stranger in a Bronx cemetery. The child was not returned. On May 12, a truck driver stopped by the side of a road a few miles from the Lindbergh house and found a small body in the woods. The boy had been dead since the night he was taken. In June, Congress made kidnapping across state lines a federal crime.

That spring, veterans of the Great War began arriving in Washington. In 1924 Congress had voted them a bonus for their service, in the form of certificates that could not be cashed until 1945. The veterans wanted the money now. They came by freight car and by truck and on foot, many with their wives and children. They called themselves the Bonus Expeditionary Force. By early summer there were perhaps 15,000 to 20,000 of them, perhaps more. Most camped on the mud flats across the Anacostia River in shacks built from scrap lumber and packing crates. The District's police chief, Pelham Glassford, had been a brigadier general in the war. He helped them find food.

On June 15 the House passed a bill to pay the bonus early. On June 17 the Senate rejected it, 62 to 18, while thousands of veterans waited outside the Capitol. Most did not go home. Many had no home to go to.

On July 28, on the administration's orders, police moved to clear veterans from buildings they were occupying near Pennsylvania Avenue. There was a scuffle, then bricks, then shots. Two veterans, William Hushka and Eric Carlson, were killed. Hushka was a Lithuanian immigrant who had sold his butcher shop to enlist. Hoover called out the army.

The army's chief of staff, General Douglas MacArthur, took personal command. His aide was a major named Dwight Eisenhower. The cavalry was led by another major, George Patton. In the late afternoon the troops advanced down Pennsylvania Avenue with tanks, drawn sabers, fixed bayonets and tear gas, driving the veterans and their families ahead of them while office workers watched from the windows. By evening the veterans had retreated across the river to the Anacostia camp. What happened next remains disputed. Hoover's instructions appear to have been to stop at the river. One of MacArthur's aides later said MacArthur disobeyed them. Eisenhower said MacArthur never received them. Either way, that night the troops crossed the bridge and cleared the camp, and the shacks burned. The glow could be seen from the White House. Hoover and MacArthur insisted that the camp had been full of criminals and communists. Most of the men in it were veterans. Hushka and Carlson were buried at Arlington.

Four weeks earlier, the Democrats had met in Chicago. On July 1, after a deal with the Speaker of the House, John Nance Garner, who became his running mate, Franklin Roosevelt won the nomination on the fourth ballot. The next morning he did something no nominee had done before. He boarded a trimotor plane in Albany and flew to Chicago through rough weather to accept in person. His speech was loose on specifics. But in it he made a promise that would give the coming decade its name: "a new deal for the American people."

Hoover campaigned on the claim that the worst was over and that Roosevelt's experiments would make things worse. He was booed in Detroit. On November 8, Roosevelt carried forty-two of the forty-eight states and won the electoral vote 472 to 59.

He would not take office until March. For four months, the country had a president who had been rejected and a president-elect who held no power, and the banks kept failing.

Part III: Death by Hunger

On October 10, 1932, the Soviet Union switched on the largest hydroelectric station in Europe. The dam across the Dnieper river, in Ukraine, had taken five years to build. It was designed with the help of an American engineer, Hugh Cooper, built by tens of thousands of workers, and equipped with American turbines. It was the showpiece of the First Five-Year Plan, which was nearing the end of its fourth year. That year the first blast furnace was lit at Magnitogorsk, the new steel city in the Urals. Tractors were rolling out of Stalingrad and Kharkiv. In the official telling, a backward country had been turned into an industrial power in less than half a decade.

Upstream and downstream of the dam, in the villages of Ukraine, people were running out of food.

Collectivization had been forced on the countryside since the winter of 1929. The peasants had resisted, slaughtering their livestock rather than give it up. The most prosperous and most stubborn had been deported as kulaks. The new collective farms were badly run and the people working them had little reason to work hard. In 1931 the state had taken so much of the harvest that many villages entered the spring of 1932 without enough to eat. The 1932 harvest was poor. The procurement quotas for Ukraine remained far beyond what the land had produced. Ukrainian party leaders asked Moscow to lower them. The quotas were reduced, and remained unachievable.

Stalin did not see a crop failure. He saw sabotage. Peasants were hiding grain. Local officials were protecting them. Nationalists were exploiting the moment. In August he wrote to his deputy Lazar Kaganovich that if nothing was done, "we may lose Ukraine."

On August 7 the government issued a decree on the protection of socialist property. Stalin had drafted it himself. Theft from a collective farm was now punishable by death, or, in mitigating circumstances, ten years in a camp. Peasants called it the law of five ears, because people were prosecuted under it for gleaning a handful of grain from fields that had already been harvested. Watchtowers went up over the fields. Children were among those caught. By mid-January 1933, more than a hundred thousand people had been sentenced under the law. Thousands were condemned to death, though many of those sentences were later reduced.

In the autumn, Moscow sent its heaviest men. Vyacheslav Molotov went to Ukraine. Kaganovich went to the North Caucasus, to the Kuban, a grain region settled largely by Ukrainian-speaking Cossacks. Brigades of party activists went from house to house with iron rods, probing walls, floors and gardens for hidden grain. They took what they found and often whatever else could be eaten. Villages that failed their quotas were placed on blacklists. They were cut off from manufactured goods, and in some cases from all trade. In the Kuban, whole Cossack settlements were deported to the north. One, Poltavskaya, was emptied of nearly its entire population in December.

On December 14, a secret decree from the Politburo and the Central Committee linked the grain crisis to the policy of Ukrainization, the encouragement of Ukrainian language and culture that Moscow had supported through the 1920s. Counterrevolutionaries, it said, had used Ukrainization as cover. In the Kuban and the rest of the North Caucasus, it was halted. In Ukraine, the purge of the party that had overseen it began.

The Soviet Union went on exporting grain. Less than before, but still more than a million tons in 1932.

Ukraine was not the only place starving, and it was not the first. In Kazakhstan the famine had begun in 1930. Most Kazakhs were nomadic herders. Moscow and the republic's party chief, Filipp Goloshchyokin, set out to collectivize their herds and settle them on farms. Livestock was requisitioned for the cities or died in the process, and the herds on which Kazakh life depended collapsed. People had nothing to eat. Hundreds of thousands fled, into Siberia, into Central Asia, across the border into China. Border guards shot some of those who tried. By the time the famine ended, around 1.5 million people in the republic had died, the large majority of them Kazakhs. Most estimates put the loss at roughly 38 to 42 percent of all Kazakhs, the highest proportion of any people in the Soviet famines.

In Ukraine the worst was still ahead. The famine there would peak in the spring of 1933. Most estimates of its dead fall between about three and a half and five million. Ukrainians call it the Holodomor, death by hunger. Whether it was genocide, a deliberate attempt to destroy Ukrainians as a nation, or a catastrophe of policy that Stalin made far worse with deliberate cruelty, remains one of the sharpest disputes in the historiography of the period. Several governments have recognized it as genocide. Many historians agree. Others argue that the famine struck grain regions across the Soviet Union, and that its deliberate escalation in Ukraine was punitive and political rather than an attempt at extermination. The December decree, the blacklists and the measures of the following months sit at the center of that argument.

Inside the party, almost no one spoke. One man tried. In the summer of 1932 Martemyan Ryutin, a former Moscow party official, circulated a long document among a small group of party members. It condemned collectivization as a disaster and called for Stalin's removal, describing him in terms no one had dared put on paper. In September the group was arrested. Ryutin was expelled from the party and sentenced to ten years. Accounts written later say Stalin wanted him shot and that the Politburo refused. The evidence for that is disputed. Ryutin was executed in 1937.

The writers were brought into line in the same year. In April, a Central Committee decree dissolved the independent literary associations, including the militant proletarian groups that had dominated the late 1920s, and announced a single union of Soviet writers. That spring a new phrase entered official use: socialist realism. Art would show life not as it was but as it was becoming, in its revolutionary development. On October 26, Maxim Gorky, home from Italy, hosted a gathering of writers at his mansion in Moscow. Stalin came. Over the evening he told them that writers were the engineers of human souls.

Two weeks later, on the evening of November 8, Stalin and his wife, Nadezhda Alliluyeva, attended a dinner in a Kremlin apartment to mark the fifteenth anniversary of the revolution. She was 31. She had married him at seventeen, and they had two children. By then she was studying at the Industrial Academy in Moscow, among young party members, some of whom had seen the countryside. The marriage had been unhappy for a long time. At the dinner Stalin flicked bread or orange peel at her, or called across the table to her by a rude interjection rather than her name. Accounts differ. She left. Sometime in the early hours of November 9, in her room, she shot herself with a small pistol her brother had brought her from Berlin.

The newspapers announced that she had died of appendicitis. Her children were told the same. Some of those close to the family later said she had been troubled by what she had heard about the famine. That cannot be confirmed. Molotov later said it was the only time he saw Stalin cry. She was buried at Novodevichy Cemetery. Stalin walked behind the coffin for part of the way, and then he went back to work.

On December 27, the Soviet government introduced internal passports. Every city dweller over sixteen had to carry one, and residence in the major cities required registration. Collective farmers were not issued passports. Without one, a peasant could not legally move to a city or take work there. In the weeks that followed, as the hunger deepened, the borders of Ukraine and the Kuban were closed to peasants trying to leave in search of bread.

In the first days of January 1933, Stalin announced that the First Five-Year Plan had been fulfilled in four years and three months.

Part IV: Clashes

On January 8, 1932, as Emperor Hirohito's carriage passed the Sakurada Gate of the Imperial Palace in Tokyo, a young Korean named Lee Bong-chang threw a hand grenade at it. The grenade damaged a carriage behind the emperor's. Hirohito was unhurt. Lee was arrested on the spot, and was hanged that October. He had been sent by the Korean Patriotic Organization, a small band working under the Korean provisional government in exile, which had its headquarters in Shanghai.

Shanghai was already tense. Since the Japanese seizure of Manchuria the previous autumn, Chinese merchants and students had organized a boycott of Japanese goods, and it was hurting. Japan had some 25,000 residents in the city, concentrated in the Hongkou district of the International Settlement. On January 18, a group of five Japanese Buddhist monks and followers was attacked by a Chinese crowd near a towel factory in Zhabei. One later died. Years afterward, a Japanese intelligence officer named Tanaka Ryūkichi testified that he had arranged the attack himself, to give Japan a pretext. His account has been widely accepted, though it cannot be fully verified.

Japanese residents rioted. The Japanese consul-general demanded an apology, compensation, punishment of the attackers and suppression of the anti-Japanese associations. Japanese warships gathered in the Huangpu river. On the afternoon of January 28, the mayor of Shanghai accepted every demand. Shortly before midnight, Japanese marines advanced into Zhabei anyway. They met the Chinese 19th Route Army, and it fought back.

The next morning, Japanese navy planes began bombing Zhabei, a crowded Chinese district of tenements, workshops and mills. It was one of the first deliberate air attacks on a dense civilian neighborhood in history, and the foreign residents of the International Settlement could watch it from their rooftops. Among the first buildings hit was the Commercial Press, the largest publishing house in China, which printed most of the country's school textbooks. Its plant burned to the ground. On February 1, fire consumed the Oriental Library across the road. It had been the largest library in China, holding more than 460,000 volumes. Only about 5,000 rare books, kept off-site in a bank vault, survived.

The 19th Route Army was a Cantonese force, poorly equipped, with a history of independence from Chiang Kai-shek's government in Nanjing. Its commanders, Cai Tingkai and Jiang Guangnai, had decided to fight largely on their own initiative. The Japanese had expected the Chinese to collapse within days. They did not. The fighting moved block by block through Zhabei and out toward Jiangwan and Wusong. Japan poured in reinforcements, first marines, then army divisions. Three times it replaced its commander. Chiang, having resigned the month before and returned in January, sent the Fifth Army to support the 19th Route, including two divisions trained by German advisers. The capital was moved temporarily from Nanjing to Luoyang.

On February 22, near the village of Miaohangzhen, three Japanese privates carried a long explosive tube into the Chinese barbed wire and were killed when it exploded, opening a gap. Within days, the Japanese press had named them the Three Human Bullets. They became a national sensation. There were songs, plays, at least half a dozen films, statues, and sweets named after them. Later accounts suggested that the men may not have intended to die at all, and that their fuse had simply been cut too short. By then the legend was fixed. Self-sacrifice for the emperor had become something Japanese audiences paid to watch.

On March 3, with a fourth Japanese division landing behind the Chinese lines, the Chinese armies withdrew and a ceasefire took hold. Somewhere between 10,000 and 20,000 Chinese civilians had been killed, by most estimates. Chinese military casualties ran into the thousands. Japanese losses were smaller and are also disputed.

On April 29, the Japanese community in Shanghai held a celebration in Hongkou Park for the emperor's birthday. Japanese officials and commanders stood on a reviewing stand while thousands of troops paraded. As the anthem was sung, a young Korean named Yun Bong-gil threw a bomb disguised as a water flask onto the platform. General Shirakawa Yoshinori, the Japanese commander in Shanghai, died of his wounds a month later. The minister to China, Shigemitsu Mamoru, lost a leg. Admiral Nomura Kichisaburō lost an eye. Yun was taken to Japan and shot in December. Thirteen years later, Shigemitsu would sign Japan's surrender aboard the USS Missouri. Nomura would be Japan's ambassador in Washington on the morning of Pearl Harbor.

A truce was signed on May 5. Japanese troops withdrew to the Settlement. China agreed not to station troops in a zone around Shanghai, its own largest city. The 19th Route Army was sent south to Fujian to fight communists.

Shanghai had been, among other things, a distraction. On March 1, while the world watched Zhabei burn, the Japanese army in Manchuria proclaimed a new state: Manchukuo. On March 9, Puyi, the last emperor of the Qing dynasty, who had been taken from his exile in Tianjin by Japanese agents the previous November, was installed as its Chief Executive in Changchun. The city was renamed Xinjing, the new capital. Manchukuo had a flag, a constitution, ministries, and a set of slogans about the harmony of five races and the Kingly Way. Its ministers were Chinese and Manchu. Each had a Japanese vice-minister, and the vice-ministers decided policy. Behind them stood the Kwantung Army.

Not all of Manchuria accepted the arrangement. Remnants of the old Chinese armies, local militias, secret societies and bandits fought on as anti-Japanese volunteer armies, sometimes tens of thousands strong. The Kwantung Army answered with what it called pacification. On the night of September 15, a band of irregulars attacked Japanese installations at the coal-mining city of Fushun. The next morning Japanese troops went to the nearby village of Pingdingshan, which they accused of harboring the attackers. They gathered the villagers at the foot of a hill, telling them they were to be photographed, and machine-gunned them. Then they burned the village. Chinese accounts put the dead at about 3,000. Japanese accounts give a lower figure, in the hundreds. On the day before the massacre, Japan had formally recognized Manchukuo.

At home, Japan was recovering faster than almost any other industrial country, and coming apart. In December 1931 the new finance minister, Takahashi Korekiyo, had taken Japan off the gold standard. The yen fell sharply. Exports boomed. Takahashi spent heavily, much of it on the army and navy, and industry revived. But the countryside was in misery. Prices for rice and silk, the two pillars of rural income, had collapsed. In the poorest districts of the northeast, families sold their daughters to brokers for the brothels and factories of the cities. Many of the young army officers came from these villages. They blamed the politicians, the zaibatsu conglomerates and the financiers who seemed to be prospering while the villages starved.

In early 1932 a Nichiren Buddhist priest named Inoue Nisshō organized a small circle of young men, many from the countryside, into what became known as the League of Blood. Its program was a list of names and a principle: one man, one killing. On February 9, a member shot Inoue Junnosuke, the former finance minister, outside a school in Tokyo where he was about to give a campaign speech. On March 5, another shot Baron Dan Takuma, head of the Mitsui conglomerate, on the steps of the Mitsui Bank. On May 15, a group of young naval officers went further. They forced their way into the official residence of Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi and shot him dead.

The consequences of that killing reached beyond Inukai. His party, the Seiyūkai, held a large majority in the Diet. It did not get to choose his successor. The elder statesman Saionji Kinmochi, who advised the emperor on such appointments, recommended a retired admiral, Saitō Makoto, to head a cabinet of national unity. Saitō took office on May 26. No party politician would lead a Japanese government again until after the Second World War. Nobody abolished party government. It simply stopped.

The League of Nations had sent a commission to investigate Manchuria, under the British diplomat Lord Lytton. It spent the spring and early summer touring China and Manchuria, interviewing officials while Kwantung Army minders kept witnesses away. Its report was published on October 2. It was careful and moderate. It acknowledged Japan's long-standing interests in Manchuria and the disorder of Chinese rule there. But it concluded that the Japanese action of September 1931 could not be regarded as self-defense, and that Manchukuo was not the product of a genuine independence movement. It recommended an autonomous Manchuria under Chinese sovereignty.

Japan had already recognized Manchukuo. In December, its delegate to the League, Matsuoka Yōsuke, told the Assembly in Geneva that Japan was prepared to be crucified, as Jesus had been, and that the world would one day understand it. Within months Japan would leave the League altogether.

Chiang Kai-shek knew that China could not fight Japan and win, not yet. His formula was blunt: internal pacification first, then resistance to the outside. The internal enemy was the Communist Party, which had built a string of rural base areas across south and central China, the largest of them the Jiangxi Soviet. In the summer and autumn of 1932, while the Japanese consolidated Manchukuo, Chiang threw several hundred thousand troops against the communist base on the borders of Hubei, Henan and Anhui and drove its army out. In October, at a conference in the Jiangxi town of Ningdu, the communist leadership stripped one of its own commanders of military authority for being too cautious. His name was Mao Zedong.

At the end of the year, the Kwantung Army was looking south. Its troops had reached the Great Wall at Shanhaiguan, where the Wall meets the sea. Beyond it lay the province of Rehe, and beyond Rehe, Beijing. The Chinese armies best placed to stop them were in Fujian and Jiangxi, pointed at other Chinese.

Part V: Machinations

In February 1932, Adolf Hitler was not a German citizen. He had been born in Austria, had renounced his Austrian citizenship in 1925, and had been stateless since. Several attempts to naturalize him had failed or ended in embarrassment. On February 25, the state of Brunswick, where the Nazis shared power, appointed him to a minor civil-service post: a government councillor attached to its legation in Berlin. German civil servants were automatically German citizens. He never did any work for Brunswick. The next day he was sworn in, and he was eligible to run for president.

The presidential election was set for March 13. Paul von Hindenburg was 84. He was a field marshal of the old empire and a monarchist, and in 1925 he had been elected as the candidate of the right. In 1932 he ran as the candidate of the Republic. The Social Democrats, the Catholic Centre and the remaining liberals backed him because he was the only man who could beat Hitler. The Social Democrats' slogan put it plainly: vote Hindenburg to defeat Hitler. The parties that had built the Weimar Republic were now asking their voters to re-elect a man who had never believed in it.

Hitler campaigned as no German politician had before. In the runoff he chartered a plane and flew from city to city, sometimes speaking in four or five in a single day, under the slogan "Hitler over Germany." Goebbels organized rallies, films, gramophone records and posters by the million. On March 13 Hindenburg took 49.6 percent of the vote, just short of the majority he needed. Hitler took 30.1 percent. The Communist leader Ernst Thälmann took 13.2. In the runoff on April 10, Hindenburg won with 53 percent. Hitler rose to 36.8.

Two weeks later came state elections across most of Germany. In Prussia, which held three-fifths of the country's population, the Nazis became the largest party in the state parliament. The Social Democratic government of Otto Braun, which had governed Prussia for most of the Republic's life and controlled its police, lost its majority. It stayed on as a caretaker.

Hindenburg owed his re-election to the Social Democrats and the Catholics, and he resented it. The men around him did not want a chancellor who depended on the left. The most important of them was General Kurt von Schleicher, the army's political fixer, a man who preferred to work from behind other people. In April, Defence Minister Wilhelm Groener had banned the SA and SS, the Nazi paramilitaries, which by then numbered several hundred thousand men. Schleicher, Groener's own protégé, undermined the ban and then Groener himself, who resigned in May. Then Schleicher turned on Chancellor Heinrich Brüning.

Brüning had governed for two years by emergency decree, cutting wages, pensions and spending to defend the currency while unemployment passed six million. He believed he was close to getting reparations cancelled. The East Prussian landowners around Hindenburg were alarmed by a plan to settle unemployed workers on bankrupt estates, and called it agrarian Bolshevism. At the end of May, Hindenburg refused to sign any more of Brüning's decrees. Brüning resigned on May 30. He later wrote that he had fallen a hundred meters from the finish line.

His successor was Franz von Papen, a Catholic aristocrat, former cavalry officer and a nobody in the Centre Party, whom Schleicher had selected precisely because he had no following of his own. The Centre Party expelled him. His ministers were so heavily titled that the press called it the cabinet of barons. Papen dissolved the Reichstag and lifted the ban on the SA, which was the price of Hitler's toleration.

That summer, Papen collected the reward Brüning had worked for. At Lausanne, in July, the Allies agreed to replace reparations with a single final payment. It was never made. Reparations, the grievance at the center of German politics for thirteen years, were effectively over. Hardly anyone in Germany noticed.

The SA came back into the streets. In the weeks before the July election, political violence killed nearly a hundred people in Prussia alone. On Sunday, July 17, the SA marched through Altona, a working-class district of Hamburg that was then in Prussia, and the Communists fired on them from the rooftops. Police fire killed many of the eighteen people who died, though exactly who shot whom remains disputed.

Papen used Altona. On July 20, claiming that the Prussian government could no longer keep order, he deposed it by emergency decree and appointed himself Reich commissioner for Prussia. Its police, the largest armed force in Germany outside the army, passed to the Reich government. The Prussian interior minister, Carl Severing, had said he would yield only to force. That evening a police president and two officers came to his office, and he left. The Social Democrats had a paramilitary of their own, the Reichsbanner, and still controlled the trade unions. They called no general strike, with six million out of work and the army behind Papen. They appealed to the courts. In October the State Court delivered a split decision that changed nothing. The last bastion of the Republic had fallen without a fight.

On July 31, the Nazis won 37.3 percent of the vote and 230 seats. They were now the largest party in the Reichstag by a wide margin. The Communists took 14.3 percent. The liberal parties of the middle, which together had once held nearly a fifth of the vote, were reduced to a few percent between them. The Catholic Centre held its ground. Almost everyone else had gone to the extremes. The Nazis and Communists, sworn enemies, together held a majority of the seats. No government could command a majority without one of them.

On August 13, Hitler was received by Hindenburg. Papen and Schleicher had been negotiating with him for weeks and were prepared to offer him the vice-chancellorship. Hitler demanded the chancellorship and full power. Hindenburg refused. He told Hitler he could not, before God and his conscience, hand the government to a single party, let alone one so intolerant. The audience lasted about twenty minutes. Afterward the government issued a communiqué presenting Hitler as a man who had demanded total power and been turned away. He took it as a public humiliation.

Four days earlier, Papen's government had decreed the death penalty for political murder. It took effect at midnight on August 9. A few hours later, in the Upper Silesian village of Potempa, five SA men broke into the home of Konrad Pietrzuch, a miner of Polish background and a Communist sympathizer. They dragged him from his bed and beat and kicked him to death in front of his mother. Within two weeks they had been tried and sentenced to death. The local SA leader led a crowd that smashed the windows of Jewish shops. Hitler sent the murderers a telegram. He addressed them as comrades and told them that their freedom was now a question of the movement's honor. In September Papen's government commuted their sentences to life imprisonment.

The new Reichstag met on August 30. By custom, its oldest member presided over the opening. She was Clara Zetkin, 75, a Communist veteran of the pre-war labor movement, who had come back from Moscow for the occasion. Half blind and barely able to stand, she told the chamber that she hoped to live to open the first congress of a Soviet Germany. Then the Reichstag elected Hermann Göring as its president.

When the Reichstag met again on September 12, Papen arrived with a dissolution order already signed by Hindenburg. Göring ignored him and called a vote on a Communist motion of no confidence. It passed 512 to 42. The Reichstag was dissolved anyway, and Germany went back to the polls.

That autumn the Nazi movement began to show strain. It had run four national campaigns in eight months, and the money was gone. Its voters were restless. In early November, in the last days of the campaign, the Nazis joined the Communists in supporting a wildcat strike of Berlin transport workers. It frightened the middle-class voters the party needed. On November 6, the Nazi vote fell to 33.1 percent. They lost two million votes and 34 seats. The Communists rose to 100 seats. Goebbels confided to his diary that the party was in a desperate position.

It was still the largest party in the Reichstag by far, and Papen still had no majority. He resigned on November 17. On November 19 and again on the 21st, Hindenburg received Hitler. This time he offered him the chancellorship, on one condition: Hitler had to assemble a working parliamentary majority. Hitler wanted the powers of a presidential chancellor, governing by decree. He refused the condition. Also in November, a group of industrialists, bankers and landowners, among them the former Reichsbank president Hjalmar Schacht, signed a petition urging Hindenburg to appoint Hitler. Most of Germany's leading industrialists did not sign. How much big business wanted Hitler, and how much it mattered, remains disputed.

Papen hoped to come back. He proposed to govern without the Reichstag, ban the Nazis and Communists, and back it with the army. On December 2, Schleicher sent an officer named Eugen Ott to the cabinet to present the results of an army war game. In the event of a general strike combined with Nazi and Communist uprisings, and perhaps a Polish attack in the east, the army could not maintain order. The ministers turned against Papen. Hindenburg let him go, reportedly with tears, and appointed Schleicher, the man behind every government of the year, chancellor in his own right. Schleicher was sworn in on December 3.

Schleicher had a plan. He would split the Nazis. Gregor Strasser was the party's head of organization, its second figure, and the leader of a wing that wanted to enter government now rather than wait for all or nothing. Schleicher offered him the vice-chancellorship. On December 4, in local elections in Thuringia, the Nazi vote collapsed by as much as 40 percent from the summer. On December 8, after a bitter confrontation with Hitler, Strasser resigned all his party offices.

For a few days, the movement seemed about to break apart. Goebbels wrote in his diary that night that Hitler had said that if the party fell apart, he would end it all with a pistol in three minutes. Instead Hitler summoned the party's regional leaders and its Reichstag deputies, spoke to them for hours, and wept. One by one they pledged loyalty to him. Strasser did not fight. He went on holiday to Italy. The party held.

Other things were leaving Germany that year. In August the Nazi-led council in Dessau voted to close the Bauhaus. Its director, Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, moved what was left of the school to Berlin, into an empty telephone factory, and carried on as a private institution. On December 10, Albert Einstein sailed for California to teach at Caltech for the winter. By his own later account, he told his wife as they left their summer house at Caputh to take a good look at it, because she would never see it again. He did not return.

At the end of the year, Hitler was out of power. His party was in debt and had lost two million voters, and its most capable organizer had walked out. Many commentators believed his chance had passed. But Papen had been dismissed by a man he considered a friend, and he wanted revenge. Hindenburg still adored him. In mid-December, through a Cologne banker named Kurt von Schröder, a meeting between Papen and Hitler was arranged for the first week of January.

Coda: New Worlds

In October 1932, a few weeks before the election, a revue called Americana opened on Broadway. It was a modest show and closed after a short run. Its one lasting contribution was a song written by Yip Harburg, who had lost his business in the Crash and turned to lyrics, and the composer Jay Gorney. It was called "Brother, Can You Spare a Dime?" By the end of the year Bing Crosby and Rudy Vallée had both recorded it, and it was on the radio everywhere.

The song is sung by a man in a breadline. He is not a tramp or a drifter. He is the man who did everything the country asked of him. He laid railroad track and raised a tower. He put on a uniform and went to France. He did his part in building the country, and the country had told him that his part would be honored. Now he stands on a corner asking a stranger for ten cents. The song does not blame anyone, and it does not demand anything. It is not angry. It is bewildered. A promise has been broken, and the man to whom it was made cannot understand how. The tune is in a minor key, and it sounds like a lament for something that once seemed permanent.

The same month, Paramount released Ernst Lubitsch's Trouble in Paradise. It opens in Venice at night, on a gondolier singing an aria as he poles his gondola along a canal. The gondola turns out to be a garbage scow. Gaston, who calls himself a baron, and Lily, who calls herself a countess, meet for a candlelit supper in a grand hotel. Over dinner they discover that both are thieves. He has picked her pocket. She has picked his. They fall in love.

The two of them go to Paris and attach themselves to Mariette Colet, the beautiful widowed heiress of a perfume company, intending to rob her. Gaston becomes her private secretary. He discovers that the respectable chairman of her board has been embezzling from the company for years. The real thief at the table was the man in the dinner jacket all along. The aristocrats are frauds, the businessmen are crooks, and the only honest people in the film are the two who make no secret of being dishonest. It is among the most elegant comedies ever made. It is also merciless about what lay under the glamour of the old European order, which the film treats as a set of manners anyone could learn and anyone could steal. After the Production Code began to be enforced in 1934, the film was judged too immoral to be shown, and it went unseen for decades.

Aldous Huxley had published his own vision at the start of the year. He had visited the United States in 1926, and the encounter had horrified and fascinated him in equal measure. He had read Henry Ford's autobiography on the voyage. In 1931, in a rented house on the coast of southern France, he wrote Brave New World.

It is set six centuries in the future, in a World State that dates its calendar from the year Ford introduced the Model T. Its citizens make the sign of the T where earlier generations crossed themselves, and they swear "by Ford." Children are not born. They are decanted from bottles in hatcheries, chemically conditioned in the bottle and psychologically conditioned afterward into five fixed castes. Each caste is engineered to love its work. Sex is plentiful and meaningless. Unhappiness is cured with a drug called soma, which has none of the drawbacks of alcohol or religion. Old things are destroyed. History is banned. Art has been replaced by entertainment. The motto of the World State is Community, Identity, Stability.

It is a world in which nothing is under strain. There is no unemployment, because every worker is made for a job. There are no strikes, no hunger marches and no breadlines. There are no wars, no revolutions and no elections. No one is afraid, and no one wants anything he cannot have. The cost is everything that makes people human: love, grief, art, faith, freedom, the ability to choose and to suffer for the choice. Huxley meant it as a warning. A few readers took it as one. Others noticed that the World State had solved every problem that was tearing their own world apart.

Near the end of the novel, the World Controller, Mustapha Mond, explains to a young man raised outside the system why the trade was worth making. People had tried freedom, he says, and it had led to collapse. They had chosen stability instead. In 1932, with the markets at the bottom, the factories idle, the famine spreading and the governments falling one after another, that argument did not sound like science fiction to everyone. A great many people were ready to make some version of the trade. In the year to come, several of them would get the chance.
