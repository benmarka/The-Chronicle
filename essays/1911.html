Intro: A Daring Heist

On the morning of Monday, August 21st, 1911, a short Italian man in a white worker's smock walked into the Louvre through the employees' entrance, nodded at nobody in particular, and made his way to the Salon Carré. It was a maintenance day — the museum closed to the public every Monday — and the usual complement of 166 guards had been reduced to 12. Vincenzo Peruggia had worked at the Louvre three years earlier, installing the protective glass cases around some of the more valuable paintings, including the one he'd come back for. Nobody had thought to ask for his smock back when the contract ended.

He lifted the Mona Lisa off the wall, carried it to a service staircase, stripped away the frame and the glass casing he himself had helped build, tucked the wooden panel under his arm, and walked toward the exit. He got briefly stuck behind a locked door. A plumber passing through the corridor unlocked it for him without comment, and Peruggia walked out into the Paris morning. He took a bus home, got off at the wrong stop, and hailed a taxi.

Nobody at the Louvre noticed anything was wrong for more than twenty-four hours. The following morning, a painter named Louis Béroud arrived at the Salon Carré to sketch the Mona Lisa, as was his habit, and found four iron pegs on the wall where it had hung. He asked a guard. The guard shrugged and suggested it was probably at the photography studio — there was an ongoing project to photograph the collection, and the cameras of the day required so much light that each painting had to be carried to the roof. Béroud waited. When the painting still hadn't materialized by mid-morning, someone thought to call the photography studio. The photography studio said it didn't have it. The alarm went up around noon.

The director of all French national museums, Théophile Homolle, had left on summer holiday having just assured the press that the Louvre was perfectly secure. Le Petit Parisien ran the story under a sarcastic sub-headline: We still have the frame. Critics noted that unlike other European galleries, which bolted their paintings to the wall, most of the Louvre's collection hung unsecured and largely unsupervised — a fact that a man named Joseph Géry Pieret had been exploiting for years, sneaking small artifacts out of the museum and selling them to local artists, a habit he cheerfully confessed to a newspaper the week after the Mona Lisa vanished.

The painting itself, before all this, had not been especially famous. It was well-regarded — Leonardo's work was always well-regarded — but it was not the Louvre's star attraction, not the painting tourists specifically came to see. What the theft did was create an absence, and the absence turned out to be more compelling than the presence had ever been. Crowds came to the Louvre to stand in front of the empty wall. Postcards of the four iron pegs sold briskly. The painting was on the front page of newspapers across Europe and America for weeks, its image reproduced endlessly in a world that had mostly never paid it much attention. By the time Peruggia was caught — twenty-eight months later, when he contacted a Florentine art dealer offering to sell it for 500,000 francs — the Mona Lisa was the most famous painting in the world. The theft had done what four centuries of critical appreciation had not.

The trial, held in Florence in 1914, was its own kind of theater. Peruggia stood in the dock and described himself as a poor Italian patriot, driven by grief at seeing his country's cultural heritage displayed in foreign museums. He had seen, he said, how many works Napoleon's armies had looted from Italy, and felt he could not rest until at least one was returned. His first choice had actually been Raphael's La Belle Jardinière — but it was too large to carry. The Mona Lisa, conveniently, fit under an arm.

There were problems with this story. Leonardo had brought the painting to France himself, in 1516, at the invitation of Francis I. Napoleon had nothing to do with it. The prosecution also noted that Peruggia had not, upon his return to Italy, donated the painting to a museum — he had hidden it in a trunk with a false bottom for over two years and then tried to sell it. The court acknowledged these inconsistencies, declined to fully credit the patriotic defense, and sentenced him to one year and fifteen days. He served seven months. The Italian public, which had largely embraced him as a national hero from the moment of his arrest, seemed satisfied. Peruggia went on to serve in the Italian army during the war, was captured by Austria-Hungary, spent two years as a prisoner of war, returned to France, married, had a daughter, and died of a heart attack in Paris in 1925 at the age of forty-three.

What he is remembered for produced a peculiar irony: the Mona Lisa's modern fame — the bulletproof glass, the permanent crowd, the place of inexplicable honor at the center of Western visual culture — rests almost entirely on the fact that a not-very-sophisticated handyman walked out of a poorly guarded museum with it under his arm, got on the wrong bus, and kept it in a trunk for two and a half years before trying to sell it to the wrong person. The painting became a masterpiece, in the popular sense, because it went missing. The myth required the theft, and the theft required the Louvre to be exactly as careless as it was — which, as it turned out, required almost no effort at all.

Part I: The World Shrinks

The race to the South Pole was not supposed to be a race. Robert Falcon Scott, Royal Navy captain and veteran of an earlier Antarctic expedition, had been planning a return to the ice since 1907. The journey was conceived as a scientific venture as much as an act of exploration — the last great terrestrial journey remaining to be made, the final substantial blank space on the map of the world. Then, in October 1910, a telegram arrived at Scott's ship while it was docked in Melbourne. It was from Roald Amundsen, who had been publicly preparing a North Pole expedition. The telegram contained one sentence: "Beg to inform you Fram proceeding Antarctic. Amundsen."

Scott chose to proceed as though the telegram had not arrived. He had his plans; he would execute them. This was, depending on how you read it, either admirable stubbornness or the first of many fatal miscalculations.

The two men represented something broader than themselves. Scott was a naval officer of the old school — literary, stoic, given to grand statements, operating within a tradition that understood suffering as its own form of achievement. Amundsen was pragmatic to the point of ruthlessness, a man who had spent years living with Arctic peoples and learning how they survived, who had no patience for any method that worked less well than another method. Scott brought ponies, motorized sledges, and dogs. The ponies struggled in the cold and had to be shot. The motorized sledges broke down almost immediately. The dogs were eventually sent back to base. Scott and his men ended up in harnesses, pulling their supply sledges by hand across hundreds of miles of ice — man-hauling, the British called it, and there was in the phrase a certain pride, a sense that the hard way was the right way. Amundsen used dogs throughout, killed and ate the weakest ones on schedule to supplement his rations, and had his team on skis the entire journey.

Amundsen made a false start in September 1911, turned back when temperatures dropped to sixty-eight below zero, and set out again on October 20th. Scott left on November 1st. On December 14th, Amundsen planted the Norwegian flag at the South Pole and spent three days confirming his position before heading back toward the coast. He and his four companions returned to base camp on January 25th, 1912, ninety-nine days after departing, healthy and largely unbothered. In the tent they left behind at the Pole, Amundsen placed a note addressed to the King of Norway, a dated record of the achievement, and a brief message to Scott: that Scott was probably the first to find the tent, that he was welcome to whatever supplies remained, and that Amundsen wished him a safe return.

Scott reached the Pole on January 17th. His party had spotted a black flag in the distance the day before — one of Amundsen's trail markers — and understood what it meant before they arrived. "The worst has happened," Scott wrote. When they found the tent, he recorded: "Great God! This is an awful place and terrible enough for us to have laboured to it without the reward of priority. All the day dreams must go; it will be a wearisome return."

The photograph taken of Scott's party at the Pole that day has become one of the defining images of the century. Five men in wool and canvas, faces raw with cold and misery, standing beside a flag they'd hauled nearly eight hundred miles to plant second. Among them, at the last minute, Scott had been unwilling to exclude the plucky Henry Bowers from the final push — meaning his five-man party had four pairs of skis between them. Bowers walked the last stretch in his boots, plunging deep into the snow at every step.

The return journey was a slow catastrophe. Edgar Evans died first, done in by injury and exposure. Lawrence Oates, his feet frostbitten and gangrenous, was slowing the others down. On March 16th, he told them he was going outside and might be some time. He walked out into the blizzard and didn't come back. Scott, Wilson, and Bowers made it eleven miles from a food depot before a nine-day storm pinned them in their tent. They had fuel for two cups of tea. Scott kept writing. His last full entry, dated March 29th: "We shall stick it out to the end, but we are getting weaker, of course, and the end cannot be far. It seems a pity, but I do not think I can write more." Then, below it, a final note: "For God's sake, look after our people."

When the search party found them the following November, Scott's arm was stretched across Wilson's body. They were still carrying thirty-five pounds of geological specimens they had collected on the way back from the Pole, lugged hundreds of miles across the ice because the scientific work, Scott had decided, would not be abandoned. Whether this was nobility or madness probably depends on who is doing the judging.

By then, Amundsen was on a lecture tour of the United States, speaking under the billing: "Discoverer of the South Pole and Winner in the International Race for the Southern Extremity of the Earth." He had returned from Antarctica to find himself somewhat overshadowed in Britain by the story of Scott's death, a situation he regarded with bafflement. He had won. He had lost no men. He had done everything correctly. The British, in response, built Scott dozens of monuments and named the joint research station at the Pole after both of them — Amundsen-Scott, winner and loser sharing a hyphen, which is perhaps the most British possible resolution to the matter.

What died with Scott in that tent was something harder to name than a man or even an expedition. The heroic age of Antarctic exploration — that peculiar late-Victorian idea that the earth's last wild places were there to be conquered by the force of individual character, that suffering ennobled the endeavor, that the journey mattered as much as the arrival — ended here. Amundsen had simply been right: preparation mattered, technique mattered, indigenous knowledge of how to survive in extreme cold mattered, and a willingness to kill your dogs on a practical schedule mattered more than any amount of stoicism. The twentieth century had given its verdict on the nineteenth century's virtues, and the verdict was not kind.

The other thing that ended was the blank spaces themselves. There were none left now worth speaking of. The poles claimed, the continents mapped, the last terra incognita filled in — the world had become, in a quite literal sense, fully known. What this meant was not yet apparent. But a world with no blank spaces is a world in which every piece of territory already belongs to someone, in which every claim has been staked, in which there is no frontier left to absorb the overflow of ambition or population or grievance. The explorers had congratulated themselves on completing the map. Nobody had yet thought hard about what a finished map implied.

Part II: Growing Crisis

In the summer of 1911, Morocco was in revolt. Not against France — against its own Sultan, Abdelhafid, whose authority had been collapsing for years under the weight of debt, foreign pressure, and the particular humiliation of a ruler who owed his throne partly to European sponsorship. Tribal forces were closing on Fez, the imperial capital, and in April the French sent twenty thousand troops to prop the Sultan up. This was presented as a humanitarian intervention, a matter of protecting European nationals and restoring order. It was also the effective annexation of a country, carried out while everyone agreed to call it something else.

Germany objected — not on behalf of Morocco, but on behalf of Germany. On July 1st, the gunboat SMS Panther anchored off the port of Agadir, officially dispatched to protect German nationals in the surrounding region. There were, at the time, four German nationals in the surrounding region. The Panther was a message, not a military threat: Germany wanted compensation for France's expansion, specifically a large portion of the French Congo, and intended to sit in Moroccan waters until it got some. In Berlin, Foreign Secretary Kiderlen-Wächter had calculated that France would negotiate quietly rather than risk confrontation. What he had not calculated was Britain.

On the evening of July 21st, David Lloyd George rose to speak at a bankers' dinner at Mansion House in London. Lloyd George was the Chancellor of the Exchequer, a Welshman from a modest background, and he was known across Europe as the most anti-imperialist, pacifist member of the British cabinet — a man who had opposed the Boer War at considerable political cost and consistently argued for spending on social reform rather than naval armament. When he said what he said next, the chancelleries of Europe paid attention precisely because of who was saying it.

"If a situation were to be forced upon us," Lloyd George told the assembled bankers, "in which peace could only be preserved by the surrender of the great and beneficent position Britain has won by centuries of heroism and achievement — by allowing Britain to be treated where her interests were vitally affected as if she were of no account in the Cabinet of nations — then I say emphatically that peace at that price would be a humiliation intolerable for a great country like ours to endure."

Germany received it as a war warning. The newspapers in Berlin ran it under headlines about national honor. The negotiations between Paris and Berlin, which had been proceeding fitfully through the summer, effectively collapsed. In September, when the French ambassador fell ill and talks broke off entirely, the Berlin stock market crashed. For several weeks, Europe was closer to a general war than it had been in a decade.

Then, as it always had before, the system held. Germany accepted French control over Morocco in exchange for 275,000 square kilometers of the French Congo — largely swampland, as it turned out, where sleeping sickness was widespread. France got what it had been taking anyway. Morocco became a French protectorate, its sovereignty liquidated in a convention signed in Berlin on November 4th, with no Moroccan present at the table and no Moroccan consulted about the outcome. The Sultan who had invited the French in to protect him found himself presiding over a country that no longer controlled its own affairs. The tribal forces who had been fighting him were now fighting a colonial administration that would prove considerably more durable.

In Germany, the settlement was received as a national humiliation. The Kaiser's own son applauded openly in the Reichstag when critics of the agreement spoke. The hawks who had wanted a harder line felt confirmed in their view that diplomacy with France and Britain meant perpetual concession. Kiderlen-Wächter, who had engineered the whole episode and considered it a modest success, found himself widely denounced as weak. The lesson Germany drew from Agadir was not that the system of crisis management worked, but that it worked against Germany. Three years later, faced with another crisis, different men in Berlin would decide not to back down again.

Italy had been watching. The Agadir crisis, which occupied European attention through the summer and autumn, provided useful diplomatic cover for a war Italy had been planning since the turn of the century. The Ottoman provinces of Tripolitania and Cyrenaica — the territory that would become Libya — had long been identified in Rome as Italy's natural colonial compensation, the piece of Africa that would establish it as a serious imperial power. In September, while the great powers were still absorbed in the Moroccan negotiations, Italy issued an ultimatum to Constantinople demanding Turkish withdrawal from Libya within forty-eight hours.

The Ottomans offered a compromise: they would surrender effective control of Libya if Italy allowed formal Ottoman sovereignty to remain, the same arrangement that existed in Egypt under British occupation. It was a reasonable offer. Italy refused it and declared war on September 29th.

The war went badly at first. Italian forces occupied the coastal towns easily enough, but encountered fierce resistance inland from both Ottoman troops and the local population, who had been told they would welcome their liberators and did not. Operations were largely confined to the coast. But in the air, something new was happening.

On October 23rd, Captain Carlo Piazza flew a reconnaissance mission over Turkish lines — the first time an airplane had been used for military observation in wartime. Nine days later, on November 1st, Second Lieutenant Giulio Gavotti loaded four modified hand grenades into a leather pouch in the cockpit of his Etrich Taube monoplane, a wood-and-canvas aircraft that had been flying for less than two years, and flew toward the Ottoman encampment at Ain Zara, east of Tripoli. At six hundred feet he screwed in the detonators and began dropping them over the side, one by one — three over the oasis at Tagiura, one over Ain Zara. Nobody is certain whether anyone was killed. Gavotti wrote in his diary that evening: "I come back really pleased with the result."

The Ottoman Empire filed a formal complaint with the international community. The Hague Convention of 1899 had prohibited the dropping of explosives from balloons. The Italians replied that an airplane was not a balloon, that the convention's language covered only lighter-than-air craft, and that Gavotti had therefore broken no law. The great powers of Europe — Britain, France, Germany, Russia, the United States — launched inquiries and reached the finding that the bombs had probably caused few if any casualties, having either failed to detonate or landed in uninhabited desert. They did not address the legal question. The Ottoman complaint was filed and forgotten.

What nobody said plainly, because it did not yet need to be said, was that the laws of war had just been outrun by the pace of invention, and that the gap between what technology could now do and what the existing rules prohibited would only widen from here. The Ottomans, who had no anti-aircraft weapons, became the first military force to shoot down an airplane — using rifle fire. It worked. But rifle fire against aircraft would not work for long, and everyone who was paying attention understood that. A Berlin newspaper noted thoughtfully that while aircraft could not yet be used effectively for destruction, they had obvious reconnaissance value. A few years later, German Zeppelin airships would drop bombs on London. The age of aerial warfare, which would kill hundreds of thousands of civilians across the following century, had been inaugurated by a man who filed a positive diary entry and was home in time for dinner.

Part III: The End of Empire

The idea that a dynasty could lose the right to rule was as old as Chinese civilization itself. The Mandate of Heaven — the political theology that had legitimized imperial authority for over two thousand years — held that heaven conferred sovereignty on a ruler, and could withdraw it. The signs of withdrawal were legible: floods, famines, peasant rebellions, military defeats, the general sense that the cosmos had turned its face away. Dynasty after dynasty had risen on this logic and eventually fallen by it, the cycle turning slowly over centuries, each collapse followed by a new mandate, a new family, a new claim to the center of the world.

By 1911, the Qing dynasty had been failing the test for decades. The Opium Wars had forced open the country to foreign trade at gunpoint. The Taiping Rebellion, which killed somewhere between twenty and thirty million people in the 1850s and 1860s, had required foreign mercenaries and regional warlords to suppress — the dynasty saving itself with other men's armies. The Sino-Japanese War of 1895 produced a defeat so humiliating, at the hands of a nation China had historically regarded as a cultural vassal, that it shocked the court into a brief paroxysm of reformism that lasted a hundred days before Empress Dowager Cixi staged a coup and had the reform edicts reversed. The Boxer Rebellion of 1900 ended with foreign troops marching through Beijing and the court fleeing the capital in disguise. Each catastrophe confirmed what more and more educated Chinese were concluding: the Mandate had been withdrawn. The Qing were ruling on borrowed time.

The immediate trigger, when it came, was almost absurdly mundane. The Qing court, attempting to centralize control of the railways, announced in 1911 that lines partly financed by provincial gentry would be nationalized. The Sichuan gentry, who had invested heavily in local rail development and stood to lose their investment, erupted in protest. Riots spread. The court dispatched troops from neighboring Hubei to restore order — a decision that left Hubei's own garrison thin and its barracks full of discontented soldiers who had been quietly infiltrating revolutionary reading groups for years.

On the evening of October 9th, a bomb went off prematurely in a revolutionary safe house in the Russian Concession in Hankou. Qing authorities arrived within hours and seized what they found: weapons, manifestos, and membership lists. The conspirators facing certain arrest made the only decision available to them. They moved that night.

The mutiny began at seven o'clock on the evening of October 10th, when troops from the engineering battalion seized the arsenal at Wuchang. By morning the city was in rebel hands. The uprising spread by telegraph — province after province declaring independence in the weeks that followed, the Qing's authority dissolving with a speed that surprised even the revolutionaries. The men who had planned the uprising for years were not there to lead it: Sun Yat-sen, the ideological architect of Chinese republicanism who had made ten previous attempts at revolution and failed every time, was in the United States raising funds when he read about Wuchang in a newspaper. He took his time returning.

The rebels needed a senior officer to give the new government a credible face. The man they settled on was Li Yuanhong, a Qing brigadier general who had, on the night of the uprising, killed several of the mutinying soldiers before the revolution swept past him. When the rebels found him, he was hiding under his wife's bed. They dragged him out at gunpoint and informed him he was the military governor of the new Hubei government. He initially refused. They made their position clearer. He accepted.

This was the revolution's founding gesture: improvised, coerced, accidental, and somehow adequate to the moment.

What followed was not a clean transfer of power but a negotiated one, shaped less by ideology than by the simple fact of who had an army. Yuan Shikai, the Qing dynasty's most capable general, had been dismissed from his post three years earlier by the dying court and had been ostentatiously tending his garden in retirement. Now both sides needed him. The Qing needed him to suppress the revolution; they recalled him to the premiership. The republicans needed him to make the abdication happen; Sun Yat-sen, who had returned from America to become provisional president of the new republic in Nanjing, offered Yuan something more valuable than any imperial appointment: the presidency itself, in exchange for bringing the dynasty down.

Yuan was not a republican. He believed in centralized authority, strong executive power, and order — he was not particular about the institutional form these took, as long as he was at the center of them. But he understood the arithmetic. The dynasty had no money and no popular support. The revolution had the provinces. He went to Empress Dowager Longyu, who was managing affairs on behalf of the six-year-old emperor Puyi, and made the situation plain.

On February 12, 1912, Longyu signed the abdication edict on Puyi's behalf. The document was a remarkable piece of political theology in reverse: it vested sovereignty in the people, abolished imperial rule, and called for a republican constitution. Two thousand years of the Mandate of Heaven, dissolved in a few paragraphs. The Articles of Favorable Treatment guaranteed Puyi his imperial title for life, a pension of four million silver dollars annually, and the right to go on living in the northern half of the Forbidden City as though the dynasty had not ended.

Nobody told Puyi. He was six years old, and his daily routine did not change. The eunuchs were still there, still deferring to his every whim. The forty-course meals continued. He was still addressed by the old honorifics, still educated as an emperor, still the center of a court that had simply ceased to govern anything. Outside the Forbidden City's walls, China was becoming a republic. Inside them, it remained 1644.

Sun Yat-sen resigned the presidency as promised. Yuan Shikai was elected in his place, inaugurated in Beijing rather than Nanjing as the republicans had stipulated, having arranged a convenient mutiny among his own troops as a pretext for not traveling south. Within months he was working to dismantle the parliamentary system the revolution had produced. Within two years the republican leader Song Jiaoren was shot dead at a Shanghai railway station by an assassin whose trail led to Yuan's cabinet. Within four years Yuan would attempt to declare himself the founder of a new imperial dynasty, the cycle of the Mandate resuming its ancient rotation, before collapsing under the weight of his own generals' opposition and dying of kidney failure in 1916.

The revolution had ended two millennia of imperial rule and produced, in its place, a power vacuum. The republic would spend the next several decades being pulled apart by warlords, invaded by Japan, and fought over by nationalists and communists, until 1949 settled the question by other means. What 1911 had accomplished was immense and real: the institutional form that had organized Chinese civilization since before the Roman Empire was gone, and gone for good. What it had not accomplished was anything resembling the stable democratic republic its architects had imagined. The Mandate of Heaven had been abolished on paper. The habits of power it had shaped for two thousand years proved considerably harder to dissolve.

Part IV: The Tiger Was Loose

Porfirio Díaz had been governing Mexico, with one brief interruption, since 1876. He was eighty years old in 1911, a general who had fought the French intervention and turned that military reputation into three decades of absolute authority. Under the Porfiriato, Mexico had been modernized on terms that suited foreign investors and the thin layer of Mexican elite who served them — railways built, mines opened, land concentrated, the indigenous and mestizo poor kept in place by a rural police force called the Rurales who did not ask questions before shooting. It had worked, in the sense that the trains ran and the books balanced and the foreign capital kept coming. The price was everything else.

The man who finally unseated him was almost comically unsuited to the task. Francisco Madero was short, slender, pale, and soft-spoken. He was a vegetarian, a teetotaler, and a spiritualist who had become interested in Spiritism as a student in Paris and never fully left it behind. He came from one of the wealthiest landowning families in northern Mexico, had studied in Maryland and Berkeley and a French business school, and had never held political office. He believed, with a sincerity his enemies found baffling and his allies found exasperating, in honest elections and constitutional democracy. His supporters called him the Apostle of Democracy. His detractors, which eventually included nearly everyone, called him naive — which was true, and which turned out to matter enormously.

What Madero had done was light a match. His 1910 Plan de San Luis Potosí, issued from exile in Texas after Díaz jailed him to prevent his running in the presidential election, called on all Mexicans to rise at the same hour on the same day against the dictatorship. The rising was a failure. But the match was lit. In the north, Pascual Orozco and Pancho Villa mobilized ragged armies and began raiding federal garrisons. In the south, Emiliano Zapata went to war against the rural bosses who had been stealing village land for a generation. By early 1911, the federal army was overstretched, its aged generals unable to respond coherently to guerrilla campaigns on multiple fronts.

The decisive moment came at Ciudad Juárez, on the Rio Grande, in May. Madero had ordered no attack — he was in negotiations with Díaz's representatives and wanted to keep the pressure diplomatic. Villa and Orozco attacked anyway, blaming the outbreak on a spontaneous incident when a federal officer insulted a female rebel on a bridge. They went to considerable lengths to avoid Madero while the battle was underway so they would not have to formally disobey him. When Madero finally reached Orozco in person and ordered him to stop, Orozco told him the fighting was already too far along. Two days later, the federal garrison surrendered.

In the aftermath, Villa and Orozco wanted the captured federal commander, General Navarro, executed. Navarro had previously ordered rebel prisoners bayoneted, and the two generals considered his death a matter of justice. Madero, noting that Navarro was eighty years old, ordered him released. During the argument over this, Orozco threatened Madero with a pistol. Madero had Navarro quietly escorted to safety.

This small episode contained the whole shape of what was coming. Madero had the legitimacy but not the ruthlessness. The men who had won his war for him had the ruthlessness but not the patience for what legitimacy required. They had fought to destroy the old order; Madero wanted to inherit it carefully.

On May 21st, Madero and a representative of the Díaz government signed the Treaty of Ciudad Juárez. Its terms were, in retrospect, the original sin of the Maderista presidency. Díaz would resign and go into exile. A provisional president would be appointed — not a revolutionary, but a Porfirian diplomat named Francisco León de la Barra, acceptable to the old establishment. New elections would follow. The revolutionary armies would be demobilized. The federal army — Díaz's federal army, the one that had been fighting the revolution for six months — would remain intact.

Four days later, on May 25th, Porfirio Díaz boarded the SS Ypiranga at Veracruz and sailed for France, where he would live out his remaining years in a modest apartment near the Arc de Triomphe. As he left, he said: "Madero has unleashed a tiger. Let us see if he can control it."

He could not.

Madero entered Mexico City in June to enormous crowds. In October he won the presidential election by a margin that reflected genuine popular enthusiasm — it was probably the most honest election in Mexican history. He took office in November. Three weeks later, from the town of Ayala in Morelos, Emiliano Zapata issued the Plan de Ayala.

Zapata was a horse trainer and village leader from Morelos, in the sugar country south of the capital, where haciendas had been swallowing communal village land for decades. He had fought for Madero because Madero had promised land reform. The Treaty of Ciudad Juárez had said nothing about land reform. The provisional government had sent troops into Morelos and executed one of Zapata's lieutenants for the crime of taxing the local rich. Madero's first act on meeting Zapata in June had been to demand that the Zapatistas disarm before any discussion of land could begin. Zapata had refused. Madero had declared him an outlaw.

The Plan de Ayala, drafted with a village schoolteacher named Otilio Montaño, was a document of furious clarity. It denounced Madero as a traitor to the revolution, withdrew recognition of his presidency, and demanded the immediate restitution of lands stolen from villages under the Porfiriato. It dismissed what Madero called democracy in language that has not aged badly: "Freedom of the press for those who cannot read; free elections for those who do not know the candidates; proper legal recourse for those who have anything to do with an attorney." The plan was not interested in constitutions. It was interested in land.

By the end of 1911, the cast of the decade was assembled and already in motion. Díaz was gone, heading toward a quiet death in Paris in 1915. Madero was in the National Palace, surrounded by the federal army he had preserved and the Porfirian Congress he had inherited, governing a country whose most effective military commanders considered him a traitor. Zapata was in the hills of Morelos, at war. Orozco would rebel by March 1912, financed by the Chihuahua cattle barons who had briefly backed Madero and decided he was too dangerous to the existing order. Villa would be arrested by one of Madero's own generals, stand before a firing squad, and survive only because a last-minute telegram arrived from the president himself.

And behind all of them, still in office, was the federal army's most capable and most sinister officer — General Victoriano Huerta, a heavy drinker with a gift for intrigue, whom Madero would soon have to call on to suppress his own former allies, and who was already making his own calculations about what came next.

The old order had fled. The new order was not yet born. In the space between them, the tiger was loose.

Coda: Inferno

The fire started on a Saturday afternoon, which mattered because Saturday was payday, which meant the workers were still there at quitting time rather than slipping out early, and because the building's exits were monitored at the end of each shift. The supervisors at the Triangle Shirtwaist Company on the eighth, ninth, and tenth floors of the Asch Building near Washington Square kept the door on the Washington Place side locked during working hours. The reason was practical: as workers left, their bags were checked for stolen fabric. It was standard practice in the garment industry. On the 146 days that ended on March 25th, 1911, it had been an indignity the workers absorbed because they had no alternative. On March 25th itself, it was a death sentence for the women nearest that door when the fire reached them and found it would not open.

The fire began in a wastebasket on the eighth floor, jumped immediately to the paper patterns hanging from the ceiling, and was fully out of control within minutes. Frances Perkins, then a thirty-year-old social reformer, had been having tea with friends on the other side of Washington Square when she heard the alarms. She ran toward them. "People who had their clothes afire would jump," she recalled fifty years later, still precise about the detail. "It was a most horrid spectacle. There was no place to go." Women hung from the windowsills of the eighth and ninth floors, crowded from behind by the smoke and fire, and eventually let go. Some clasped each other's hands. The nets the fire department brought were not designed for bodies falling from that height at that speed. Everyone who jumped died.

Within fifteen minutes, 146 workers were dead. Most of them were young immigrant women. Some were as young as fifteen. They had been working seven-day weeks in thirteen-hour shifts for six dollars a week, doing overtime that Saturday on blouses for Easter.

The owners, Max Blanck and Isaac Harris — both recent immigrants who had built the Triangle Waist Company into one of the largest shirtwaist manufacturers on the East Coast — were indicted on manslaughter charges in April. The trial ran through December. The prosecution's case was straightforward: the door was locked, the locked door caused deaths, therefore the owners were criminally responsible. The defense lawyer, Max Steuer, attacked the credibility of the survivors who testified by getting the key witness to repeat her account of the fire word for word, then doing it again, then a third time — the identical phrasing each time was enough to convince the jury she had been coached. After deliberating for one hour and forty-five minutes, the jury acquitted both men. One juror explained afterward: "I believed that the door was locked at the time of the fire. But we couldn't find them guilty unless we believed they knew the door was locked."

The judge had Blanck and Harris smuggled out through a back entrance while the families of the dead hissed in the corridor. The owners collected $65,000 in insurance on the damaged property. Three years later, civil suits brought by twenty-three families resulted in a settlement of seventy-five dollars per dead worker. In 1913, Blanck and Harris were caught sewing fraudulent labels into their shirtwaists certifying the garments had been made under sound conditions.

What the law could not do, the street did. On April 5th, one hundred thousand people marched in a procession up Fifth Avenue. Four hundred thousand watched. There was no music. The marchers had decided that silence was the appropriate register for what they were saying.

A week after the fire, a meeting was held at the Metropolitan Opera House. Three thousand five hundred people packed the hall. The working-class mourners filled the galleries; the middle- and upper-class reformers sat in the boxes. Tension broke out almost immediately between the two groups over what kind of response the fire demanded — a bureau of fire prevention, one side suggested; class solidarity, answered the other. The meeting was heading toward disorder when Rose Schneiderman stood up to speak.

Schneiderman was a Polish-born former garment worker turned union organizer, small enough that Perkins later said she couldn't have come up to her shoulder. She spoke barely above a whisper. She did not thank anyone for coming. She said: "I would be a traitor to these poor burned bodies if I came here to talk good fellowship. We have tried you good people of the public and we have found you wanting." She described what the fire actually was: not an accident, not an anomaly, but the ordinary and predictable result of a system in which property was protected by law and labor was not. "The life of men and women is so cheap and property is so sacred," she said. "There are so many of us for one job it matters little if 146 of us are burned to death."

Al Smith, then the majority leader of the New York State Assembly, had himself appointed to the Factory Investigating Commission that was formed in response to the fire rather than delegate it to someone else. "This is too raw," he said. "I'm not going to turn this over to somebody else." The commission, chaired by Robert Wagner in the state Senate and vice-chaired by Smith, surveyed 323 establishments in New York City alone, examining the working conditions of nearly eleven thousand men, women, and children. What they found was not an anomaly. Unsafe machinery, overcrowding, poor ventilation, and blocked exits were present in factory after factory across the state.

Thirty-eight new laws followed over the next three years — mandatory fire drills, automatic sprinklers, requirements that factory doors open outward and remain unlocked during working hours, restrictions on night work for women, regulations governing the employment of children. Perkins, recommended by Theodore Roosevelt as executive secretary of the Committee on Public Safety, became the commission's indispensable operator, pushing its mandate outward from fire safety to the full range of industrial hazard. She would describe what she learned in those years as her education in what government could actually do. Two decades later, as Franklin Roosevelt's Secretary of Labor — the first woman to hold a cabinet position in American history — she brought that education to Washington. "The New Deal began on March 25th, 1911," she said. "The day that the Triangle factory burned."

It is worth pausing on the chain of causation implied by that sentence. A fire on a Saturday afternoon in a Greenwich Village garment factory produced a commission that produced thirty-eight laws that produced a generation of reformers who produced, eventually, the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938, the forty-hour work week, the minimum wage, and the institutional architecture of the modern American welfare state. The fire also produced, less directly, OSHA — the federal agency that now enforces workplace safety standards and was established in 1970, sixty years after the fact, carrying forward a mandate that had its origin in a locked door on the ninth floor of a building that its owners had assured the authorities was perfectly fireproof.

None of this was visible in March 1911. What was visible was that the legal system had weighed the deaths of 146 workers and found them insufficient to hold anyone accountable, and that the political system, prodded by a hundred thousand silent marchers and a red-haired woman speaking barely above a whisper at the Metropolitan Opera House, had decided to respond anyway.

Elsewhere in 1911, the Supreme Court had that May dissolved the Standard Oil Trust — the largest corporation in the world, controlling ninety percent of American petroleum — into thirty-nine separate companies. The suit had been launched by Theodore Roosevelt and inherited by William Howard Taft, and its resolution was the capstone of the progressive antitrust agenda that had been building for a decade. It was the kind of reform that worked through process: legal argument, judicial decision, institutional reorganization, conducted at a remove from anyone who pumped oil or bought it. The companies that descended from Standard Oil's breakup would become ExxonMobil, Chevron, and BP. The machine had been taken apart and reassembled in a different configuration, and the people who worked the rigs and the refineries were largely unaffected either way.

By the end of 1911, the year had delivered its full inventory: an emperor dethroned, a dictator exiled, an empire of oil dissolved by judicial order, a fire that killed a hundred and forty-six people and eventually produced a labor secretary and a New Deal. A painting had been stolen and made famous by its absence. Two men had raced to the bottom of the world, one of them rationally and one of them beautifully, and the rational one had come home. Bombs had been dropped from airplanes for the first time in history, and the international community had investigated and concluded the damage was probably minimal and filed the complaint accordingly. Every one of these events seemed, in its moment, like something that could be managed — a crisis resolved, a reform achieved, a threshold crossed. Most of them were. The ones that weren't would take another three years to detonate.
