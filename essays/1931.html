Intro: The Sound of Cinema

On the night of January 30, 1931, a new movie palace opened on Broadway in downtown Los Angeles. It had cost more than a million and a half dollars. It had a ballroom, a restaurant, a nursery and a crying room for mothers with infants. It was the last of the great picture palaces the street would ever build, and it opened fifteen months after the world’s money ran out. Its first film was Charlie Chaplin's City Lights. Albert Einstein sat beside Chaplin for the premiere.

About half an hour in, the projector stopped. The house lights came up. The theater's manager walked out and asked the audience to take a few minutes to appreciate the building they were sitting in. Chaplin, by his own account, wanted to kill him. The crowd booed until the film started again.

Chaplin had spent nearly three years on City Lights. When he began, The Jazz Singer was a novelty. When he finished, every studio in Hollywood had converted to sound, and theaters across the country had laid off their orchestras. Chaplin owned his own studio and his own distribution, so he could refuse, and he did. The film has music, which Chaplin composed himself, and it has sound effects. It has no talking. "I'll give the talkies three years," he told a reporter. "That's all."

The joke is right in the first scene. A crowd gathers in a city square for the unveiling of a monument to Peace and Prosperity. Dignitaries step up to speak. What comes out of their mouths is the squawk of a kazoo, a sound with the rhythm of a speech and none of its sense. Then the sheet is pulled away, and the Tramp is asleep in the statue's lap. It's a gag about talking pictures. In January 1931 it was also a gag about the speeches of the decade just ended.

The rest of the film is about people who can't see each other properly. A millionaire befriends the Tramp only when drunk and forgets him when sober. A blind girl selling flowers takes him for a rich man. He works, steals and goes to prison to pay for the operation that restores her sight. In the last scene she gives a coin to a ragged stranger outside her shop, takes his hand, and knows him by touch. "You?" she asks. He nods. The film ends there, before anyone has to decide what happens next. Einstein cried.

Everyone else in the industry had gone the other way, and sound had given them new things to sell. In January, Warner Brothers released Little Caesar, with Edward G. Robinson as a small-time hood who climbs to the top of the Chicago rackets and dies in the gutter under a billboard. In April came The Public Enemy. James Cagney pushes a grapefruit into a woman's face, and the film ends with his corpse, trussed up like a delivery, falling through his mother's front door. Silent films had had gangsters. They hadn't had the tommy gun's stutter, the screech of tires, or Cagney's fast, sour voice. Hollywood released nine gangster pictures in 1930 and twenty-six in 1931.

The pressure came quickly. Civic groups and newspaper editors complained that the films made heroes of killers. The Hays Office hired a former police chief to study whether they did any harm. Jack Warner announced that his studio would stop making them, and said he had never let his teenage son see one. Warner kept making them. The real Capone was in court by autumn. He was convicted in October, not for any killing, but for failing to pay his taxes.

Universal found another use for sound. Dracula opened at the Roxy in New York on February 12. It has almost no music: a few bars of Tchaikovsky over the credits, a fragment of Wagner at the opera, and otherwise silence. What fills the silence is a voice. Bela Lugosi had played the part on Broadway, and he spoke English carefully and with pauses, as though every word had to be fetched from somewhere far off. "I am... Dracula." The studio told the papers that people in the audience had fainted. Whether they did hardly mattered. The film made a fortune, and Universal went looking for another monster.

It arrived in November. Frankenstein opens with a man in evening dress stepping out from behind a curtain to warn the audience that what follows may shock them, even horrify them, and that anyone who would rather not be tortured should leave now. Then comes the graveyard, the gallows, the laboratory and the storm. Boris Karloff plays the creature, and he never speaks a word. In the year's two most remembered American films, the two figures at the center have no voice. One is a tramp, and the other is a dead man stitched back together.

The film that understood sound best was made in Berlin. Fritz Lang had built Weimar cinema's grandest silent spectacles, and M was his first talking picture. It opens in a tenement courtyard. A circle of children plays a counting game, and the rhyme they chant is about a murderer coming with his little chopper. A woman leans out of a window and tells them to stop. Upstairs, a mother sets the table and waits for her daughter, Elsie, to come home from school. The clock moves. She goes to the stairwell and calls the girl's name. The camera shows an empty stairwell, an empty attic, an empty chair at the table. A ball rolls out of some bushes and stops. A balloon drifts up and catches in the telephone wires.

The man who bought Elsie that balloon whistled while he did it. The tune is from Grieg, "In the Hall of the Mountain King," the music of trolls chasing a man through a mountain. Peter Lorre plays the killer, but the whistling is Lang's own. The man who sold the balloon is blind. Days later he hears the same tune on a crowded street, and he remembers it.

By then the whole city is hunting. The police raid the bars and question every known criminal in Berlin. The raids are so bad for business that the criminals organize a search of their own, using the city's beggars as lookouts. A thief chalks an M on the back of the killer's coat. The underworld catches him first and drags him to an abandoned distillery to put him on trial, before a jury of pickpockets, safecrackers and prostitutes. Lorre, cornered, sweating and pop-eyed, screams that he can't help himself. He says there is something inside him that he can't escape. The crowd wants him killed. The police arrive just in time to give him to a different court.

The film's last words go to the mothers. Elsie's mother faces the camera dressed in black. A sentence won't bring the children back, she says. One has to keep a closer watch on them.

M premiered at the Ufa-Palast am Zoo on May 11, 1931. That same day in Vienna, Austria's largest bank announced that it had lost more than half its capital, and the banks of Europe began to fall.

Part I: The Second Republic

The Bourbon monarchy didn't fall in a battle. It ended with a municipal election. On Sunday, April 12, 1931, Spaniards voted for town councils. It was the first real vote of any kind since the dictatorship of Primo de Rivera, which collapsed the year before, and everyone understood the question was the king. The countryside, where local bosses still delivered votes the way they always had, came out monarchist. The cities didn't. The republican and socialist coalition carried Madrid, Barcelona, and almost every provincial capital in the country. By Monday the generals were telling Alfonso XIII that they couldn't guarantee the army. The Civil Guard's commander told him the same.

On the morning of April 14, the town council of Eibar, a small Basque arms-making town, raised the republican tricolor of red, yellow and purple. By evening the flag was flying over the Ministry of the Interior in Madrid. The Puerta del Sol was packed. Strangers embraced. Trams stopped because there was no way through. That night the king left the palace by car and drove south through the dark to Cartagena, where a cruiser was waiting. He didn't abdicate. His farewell statement said he was suspending the exercise of royal power so as not to set Spaniards against Spaniards in a civil war. By dawn he was off Spanish soil. The Second Republic had come into being without a shot being fired.

The people who now ran Spain were lawyers, professors and journalists. They had been in exile, in prison or in the cafés. The provisional president was Niceto Alcalá-Zamora, a devout Catholic landowner from Andalusia who had served the monarchy as a minister. The minister of war was Manuel Azaña, a novelist and translator, a bureaucrat by profession, who had never worn a uniform. The socialists took labor and justice. The government promised a constitution, a parliament, land for those who worked it, schools for the illiterate, and a state free at last of the Church. It had a little over two years to do it.

Four weeks later, Madrid was on fire. On Sunday, May 10, a new monarchist club opened on the Calle de Alcalá. Someone put a recording of the old royal anthem on a gramophone and played it out of an open window. The crowd walking home from the Retiro heard it. Within hours a car had been burned, the offices of the monarchist newspaper ABC were under siege, and rumors were spreading that a monarchist had killed a republican. The next morning crowds began setting fire to churches and convents. The Jesuit residence on the Calle de la Flor went first. In the following days the fires spread to Seville, Valencia, Alicante, Murcia and Cádiz, and worst of all to Málaga, where whole quarters of the old city burned. Nuns fled into the streets. Centuries of altarpieces, archives and libraries were destroyed. More than a hundred religious buildings burned before the government declared martial law.

For a day and a half, the Republic had done nothing. In cabinet, Azaña refused to send in the Civil Guard. The sentence attributed to him, repeated for the next forty years by the people who hated him, was that all the convents in Spain weren't worth the life of a single republican. The cardinal primate, Pedro Segura, had published a pastoral letter at the start of the month praising the fallen king. Within weeks he was expelled from the country. Neither side forgot those days in May. The right remembered that the Republic had let the churches burn. The left remembered that the Church had mourned the king.

In July the elected parliament met. The Constituent Cortes opened on the fourteenth, Bastille Day, which was a deliberate choice. The republican and socialist parties held an overwhelming majority. The deputies came to the chamber to write a constitution, and there were cheers for the Republic.

On that same day, three hundred kilometers away in Zaragoza, General Francisco Franco said goodbye to his cadets. He was thirty-eight, the youngest general in Europe, a veteran of the colonial war in Morocco. Since 1928 he had been the director of the General Military Academy, the school the monarchy built to train a new generation of officers in a single spirit. Azaña considered it expensive and politically suspect, and he closed it as part of his reform of the army. Franco obeyed the order. His farewell speech was short. He used the word discipline again and again. He told the cadets that discipline had no merit when you agreed with the command, and that it reached its full value only when every instinct said the opposite. He spoke of the sad days of Spain's decline, and of what he called injustice. Azaña read the speech and entered a formal reprimand in his service record. The young men in that room were the officers Franco had shaped. Five years later, most of them would follow him.

The constitution was written over the autumn. Its first article declared Spain "a democratic republic of workers of every class." The fights were over everything else. On October 1 the chamber took up the women's vote. There were three women deputies in the Cortes, all elected by men, and two of them took part in the debate. Clara Campoamor, a lawyer from the working-class Madrid, argued that a republic which denied half its citizens the vote was no republic. Victoria Kent, a lawyer and the Republic's director of prisons, argued for delay. She said it was not a question of women's ability. It was a question of timing for the Republic: Spanish women, she said, still listened to their confessors and would vote the way the priests told them to. The papers called the two of them the egg white and the yolk. Campoamor won, 161 votes to 121. Two years later women voted for the first time. Neither Campoamor nor Kent kept her seat.

Then came the Church. Article 26 ended state funding for the clergy, barred the religious orders from teaching, and provided for the dissolution of any order that took a vow of obedience to a foreign authority. Everyone knew that meant the Jesuits. In the early hours of October 14, Azaña rose to defend it. He was not yet prime minister. His argument was historical: the culture of Spain was no longer Catholic, whatever the census said, and the state had to be built for the country that actually existed. He put it in one sentence. "Spain has ceased to be Catholic." The article passed. Alcalá-Zamora resigned that day, and so did his interior minister. Azaña became prime minister in his place. Millions of Spaniards who went to Mass every Sunday read the sentence in the next morning's papers. They didn't read it as a description of their country. They read it as a threat.

The constitution was adopted on December 9. The next day the Cortes elected Alcalá-Zamora, the man who had resigned over it, president of the Republic. It was meant as a gesture of reconciliation, and in the chamber it worked as one.

It counted for less in the countryside. In Andalusia and Extremadura the land belonged to a few thousand families, and the people who worked it were day laborers, hired at dawn in the town square for whatever the landlord's manager would pay and turned away when the harvest was done. They had been promised land. In 1931 they got decrees: that landlords must hire local men first, that fields could not be left uncultivated, that working hours had limits. The agrarian reform itself was still being argued over in committee. The anarchists, the largest workers' movement in the country, had never accepted parliament in the first place, and spent the year striking. The landlords waited. The Civil Guard, the rural police who had protected their property for ninety years, was still at its posts.

On the last day of the year, the socialist land workers' federation called a strike across the province of Badajoz. In Castilblanco, a village on the far side of the Guadiana, a few hundred laborers gathered in the square. The four Civil Guards in the village ordered them to disperse. One of the guards fired, and a man fell. The crowd killed all four guards with stones, knives and hoes. They mutilated the bodies. Over the next days, Civil Guards elsewhere in Spain fired on crowds in other towns. In Arnedo, in the Rioja, they killed eleven people, women and a child among them. In Castilblanco the bodies of the four guards lay in the square into the new year.

Part II: Crash

The Creditanstalt was the largest bank in Austria. It was founded in 1855 by the Vienna Rothschilds, and the Rothschilds still sat at the head of its board. For most of a century it had financed the Habsburg empire's railways, mines and factories. The empire was gone. The bank's business was still spread across the successor states, now divided by tariff walls and new currencies. Through the 1920s it had absorbed weaker banks at the government's request. Their losses came with them. By the spring of 1931, a Dutch bank was politely asking for a higher rate on its credits.

On Friday, May 8, the bank told the government it had lost more than half its capital. Under Austrian law, that made it insolvent. The government, the National Bank and the Rothschilds spent the weekend negotiating a rescue. They announced it on Monday, May 11. The point of announcing a rescue was to calm depositors. It did the opposite. Austrians and foreigners lined up together to withdraw their money. The notes in circulation in Austria nearly quadrupled in three weeks. At the Bank of England, an official named Harry Siepmann read the telegrams from Vienna and telephoned the Treasury. He said it might well bring down the whole house of cards.

Austria needed a foreign loan, and France had money. France also had a grievance. In March, Austria and Germany had announced a customs union, and in Paris that looked like the first step toward the union of the two countries that the peace treaties forbade. The French attached political conditions to their help. The help came slowly. By June the run had crossed the border.

Germany had paid its way through the 1920s on borrowed money. American and British banks had lent German banks money for three months at a time, and the German banks had lent it on to industry for years. Every quarter the loans had to be rolled over. In 1931 the foreign creditors stopped rolling them over. On June 6, Chancellor Heinrich Brüning announced that the German people could not keep bearing the burden of reparations. He meant it as a demand. The markets heard it as a warning, and gold left the Reichsbank in a flood.

On June 20 President Hoover proposed a one-year suspension of all reparations and war-debt payments between governments. It was the boldest thing he did in office, and it took the French two weeks to accept it. In those two weeks the rest of the money left. On July 13 the Darmstädter und Nationalbank, the second-largest bank in Germany, closed its doors. The government shut every bank in the country. When they reopened, the mark sat behind exchange controls. Germans could no longer freely convert their money or send it abroad. Formally, the mark was still tied to gold. In practice the link was cut.

Brüning had governed for over a year without a parliamentary majority. The constitution allowed the president to issue emergency decrees, and the president was Field Marshal Paul von Hindenburg, eighty-three years old, the hero of Tannenberg. Brüning wrote the decrees and Hindenburg signed them. They cut wages, salaries, pensions and unemployment pay, and raised taxes on almost everything that was left. The aim was to show the world that Germany was paying everything it could. The result was the deepest deflation of any major economy. In Berlin they called Brüning the Hunger Chancellor. By winter Germany had more than five million registered unemployed, and millions more who had stopped registering.

In Moscow, the crisis was read as a forecast coming true. Capitalism was finally collapsing, as the theory said it must, and the Soviet Union had planned its way out of the system just in time. In February Stalin had addressed the managers of Soviet industry. "We are fifty or a hundred years behind the advanced countries," he told them. "We must make good this distance in ten years. Either we do it, or they crush us." The Five-Year Plan was building steel mills, tractor works and hydroelectric dams from nothing, and the machinery came from Germany, Britain and the United States. It had to be paid for in foreign currency. That meant grain.

The Soviet Union was not immune to the crash. It was tied to it through the price of wheat. As world prices collapsed, every ton of grain bought less machinery, so the state shipped more tons. In 1929 the Soviet Union had exported a few hundred thousand tons of grain. In 1930 it exported nearly five million. In 1931 it exported more than five million, from a smaller harvest. The grain was taken from collective farms the peasants had been forced into, often at gunpoint. In Kazakhstan the state was forcing nomads into collective farms, and their herds died by the millions. The famine there had already begun. By most estimates, more than a million Kazakhs would die of it by 1933, perhaps a third of the nation. That year, a Soviet trading company in New York advertised six thousand jobs for skilled American workers. In eight months it received more than a hundred thousand applications.

London was next. The City had lent heavily to Germany, and that money was now frozen behind the German controls. In late July the foreign withdrawals began from London. On July 31 a committee of businessmen chaired by Sir George May reported that Britain faced a budget deficit of £120 million. It recommended cutting the pay of teachers, police and the armed forces, and cutting unemployment benefit by a fifth. The report was meant to reassure the markets. Like the rescue in Vienna, it frightened them instead. Ramsay MacDonald, the first Labour prime minister, was called back from his holiday in Scotland. The Bank of England told him that the loans that could save the pound in New York and Paris depended on the budget being balanced.

Labour had been founded by the trade unions. Its voters were the people who drew the dole. On the night of Sunday, August 23, the cabinet voted on a ten percent cut in unemployment benefit. Eleven ministers voted for it and nine against. A government could not survive a split like that. MacDonald went to the palace to resign. He came back as prime minister of a National Government of Conservatives, Liberals, and the handful of Labour men who followed him. Within days his own party had expelled him.

The cuts passed. On September 15, at Invergordon on the Cromarty Firth, sailors of the Atlantic Fleet read in the newspapers how much their pay was to be reduced. For the lowest ranks it was a quarter. They refused to put to sea. There was no violence. The men cheered from the decks and stood on the forecastles, and the officers didn't know what to do. The Admiralty negotiated within two days. But the world had now read that the Royal Navy had mutinied, and the run on the pound became a stampede. On Sunday, September 20, the government announced that Britain was suspending the gold standard. The next morning sterling was floating. Within weeks it had lost a quarter of its value against the dollar. For a century the pound had been the money the world measured itself against.

In October the country voted. The National Government asked for a free hand to save the nation and got it. It took 554 seats. Labour came back with barely fifty. MacDonald stayed prime minister at the head of a parliament in which most members belonged to the party he had spent his life opposing.

When the pound left gold, the dollar became the next target. Foreign governments and banks began converting their dollars into gold. To stop the drain, the Federal Reserve Bank of New York did what the rules of the gold standard required. In October, in the middle of the worst depression in American history, it raised its interest rate from one and a half percent to three and a half. The gold stayed. The banks didn't. In 1931, 2,294 American banks failed. Each failure wiped out the savings of the people who had trusted it.

On October 10, Hindenburg received Adolf Hitler for the first time. It went badly. The field marshal found the Nazi leader long-winded and was reported to have called him a Bohemian corporal, someone he might make a postmaster, never a chancellor. Hitler left Berlin that night for the spa town of Bad Harzburg. There, on October 11, the German right gathered to announce a common front against Brüning and the Republic: the Nationalists of the press baron Alfred Hugenberg, the veterans of the Stahlhelm, the Pan-German League, princes of the old ruling houses, retired generals. Hjalmar Schacht, who had saved the mark in 1923 as president of the Reichsbank, rose to accuse the government of hiding the true state of the nation's finances. The foreign press reported that he left for Italy soon afterward. Hitler sat on the reviewing stand while his Brownshirts marched past. Then, before the Stahlhelm could parade, he got up and left.

Part III: The Rising Waters

The water started coming in the spring. The winter of 1930 had been hard in central China, with heavy snow in the mountains to the west. When it melted, the rivers came down full, and the spring rains followed without a break. Then, in July, the storms arrived. In an ordinary year, two cyclones might cross the Yangtze basin over a whole summer. By one count, nine came in July 1931 alone. Some weather stations along the river recorded more than two feet of rain that month. The Yangtze, the Huai and the Grand Canal all broke their banks at once. By August an area larger than England was underwater. That was a single sheet of water across the most densely populated farmland on earth.

The Yangtze valley had always flooded. For thousands of years its lakes and marshes had soaked up the summer surge and let it go again. Over the centuries people had drained the marshes to farm them and walled in the river with dikes to protect the fields, so each year the river had less room. In places the dikes had raised the water above the plain around it. The Grand Canal at Gaoyou ran several meters above the rooftops of the city beside it. When the earthworks held, the land they protected was some of the richest in China. When they gave way, the water fell on people living below it.

Wuhan was three cities at the meeting of the Yangtze and the Han: Hankou, Wuchang and Hanyang. Refugees from the drowned countryside had been arriving there since late spring. Just before six on the morning of July 27, a dike on the city's edge failed, and water poured into Hankou. Within days most of the city was underwater. People moved into upper floors, onto rooftops, onto railway embankments and the few patches of high ground. Merchants turned sampans into ferries and charged for the trip across what had been streets. The water was full of sewage, rubbish and the bodies of animals and people. On August 19 the river at Hankou reached its highest mark, more than fifty feet above normal. Close to eight hundred thousand people in and around the city lost their homes. Parts of Wuhan stayed underwater for nearly three months.

Some in Wuhan had their own explanation. The city had long had a temple to the Dragon King, the deity who governs rain and water, at the place where the two rivers meet. By local accounts it had been torn down the year before to make room for a road. Many people believed the flood was the dragon's revenge. The city's officials, educated modern men, took part in the prayers and rites to appease him anyway. They had no better idea what else to do.

On August 24 a typhoon came ashore near Shanghai and moved inland. After dark the next evening, the dikes of the Grand Canal broke above Gaoyou. The widest breach was more than fifteen hundred feet across. The water came down from above onto a sleeping city. Somewhere between ten and fifteen thousand people drowned that night. Some later accounts put the number at two hundred thousand. Nothing supports a figure that high, but it is repeated to this day.

No one knows how many people the flood killed. That isn't for lack of trying. Immediately afterward, a team from the University of Nanking surveyed the flood zone household by household. They estimated that about 150,000 people had drowned, and that drowning accounted for less than a quarter of the deaths in the first hundred days. The rest died of what came after. Cholera and dysentery spread through the refugee camps. Malaria came with the standing water. Schistosomiasis spread through the parasites that lived in the flooded fields. Hunger took others, because the harvest was gone and the seed for next year's planting was gone too. Chinese historians working from contemporary reports have counted about 420,000 dead. The commission that ran the relief spoke of two million. A figure of nearly four million appears in lists of the world's deadliest disasters, but on examination it rests on very little. The water spread across eight provinces and took months to go down, and the dead were mostly poor and mostly uncounted.

The government in Nanjing was four years old. It had won its civil wars without ever fully winning the country. Chiang Kai-shek was in Jiangxi when the floods peaked, fighting the communists in the hills. In August the government set up a National Flood Relief Commission. It was chaired by T. V. Soong, the finance minister and Chiang's brother-in-law, who had trained at Harvard. To run the relief day to day, Soong brought in Sir John Hope Simpson, a retired Indian civil servant who had just finished reporting on refugees and land in Palestine for the British government. The commission hired doctors and engineers. It set up camps. It paid flood victims in grain to rebuild the dikes that had failed them, on the principle that charity without work bred dependence.

The grain came from America. Two years of collapsing prices had left the American government holding millions of bushels of wheat that American farmers couldn't sell, bought up in a failed attempt to hold prices. In September, Nanjing agreed to buy 450,000 tons of it on credit. Wheat that had been worthless in Kansas was shipped across the Pacific to feed people in Anhui. The commission estimated that it would cover about half of what the starving needed to get through to spring.

That same September the most famous man in the world came down the coast. Charles and Anne Lindbergh had flown their seaplane across Canada, Alaska and Siberia to map a northern air route to Asia. When they reached China, the government asked them to survey the flood. For two weeks they flew over the drowned provinces, mapping where the dikes had broken and where the water was still rising. Anne kept the log. One day they landed on the floodwater near a town in northern Jiangsu, carrying a Chinese doctor and medical supplies. Starving people paddled out to them in sampans and swarmed the plane, thinking it carried food, until it nearly sank under them. The plane got away. In early October at Hankou, it was lowered from the British aircraft carrier Hermes into the fast current of the Yangtze, and it capsized. The Lindberghs and their passenger were pulled from the river. The plane was hoisted back aboard damaged and shipped home. The survey was over.

By then China had a second disaster. On the night of September 18, the Japanese army in Manchuria seized the city of Mukden. Within days the government bonds that were supposed to fund the relief collapsed on the Shanghai market. Every soldier, every dollar and every hour of the government's attention was now needed in two places at once.

In the winter, people began to go home. The water had left mud, ruined fields and villages that were mostly gone. The relief commission kept running work camps into 1932, and the cholera came back that summer. Hope Simpson told an American reporter that the relief work's greatest effect had been political. The peasant, he said, now knew there was a central government that thought of him, and the work had done a great deal to stop the spread of communism. In the hills of Jiangxi, on the anniversary of the Russian Revolution in November, the communists declared their own state, the Chinese Soviet Republic. Its chairman was Mao Zedong.

Part IV: The Generals’ War

Japan had been in Manchuria for a generation. It won the southern railway there from Russia in 1905, along with the leased territory at the tip of the Liaodong peninsula and the right to post troops to guard both. The railway company ran mines, steelworks, hotels, schools and a research bureau, and its guard grew into an army. The Kwantung Army, named for the leased territory, had about ten thousand men in 1931, and its officers had come to believe Manchuria was Japan's by right of the blood spilled taking it. In 1928 some of them assassinated the warlord who ruled the region by blowing up his train. His son, Zhang Xueliang, inherited his armies and went over to the Nationalist government in Nanjing. Manchuria was now formally part of a China that was beginning to talk about recovering its railways.

Two staff officers in the Kwantung Army, Colonel Itagaki Seishirō and Lieutenant Colonel Ishiwara Kanji, decided not to wait. Ishiwara was a military theorist with a private apocalyptic vision. He believed a final war between Japan and America was coming, and that Japan would need Manchuria's coal, iron and farmland to fight it. By the end of May 1931 he and Itagaki had a plan. Over the summer the Japanese press filled with stories of Chinese provocations: a clash between Korean and Chinese farmers over an irrigation ditch at a village called Wanpaoshan, and the killing by Chinese soldiers of a Japanese army captain traveling in disguise in the interior. In Tokyo, some officers knew roughly what was coming. The war minister sent a general to Manchuria with orders to stop it. The conspirators got word of his mission, and he was entertained at a geisha house on his first night in Mukden.

At about twenty past ten on the night of September 18, a lieutenant of the railway guard set off a small charge beside the track north of Mukden, near a place called Liutiaohu. It damaged about five feet of one rail. Within the hour, Japanese troops were attacking the Chinese barracks nearby, where seven thousand of Zhang Xueliang's soldiers were quartered, and the artillery the Kwantung Army had quietly installed in its compound in Mukden was shelling the airfield. Zhang was in Beijing, ill. His standing orders were not to resist. Whether those orders came from him or from Nanjing has been disputed ever since. Mukden was in Japanese hands by morning, and by the end of the next day so were most of the cities along the railway.

The government in Tokyo was the cabinet of Wakatsuki Reijirō, with Shidehara Kijūrō as foreign minister. Shidehara had spent a decade arguing that Japan could get what it needed from China by trade and treaty. On the morning of the nineteenth the cabinet resolved that the incident must not spread. It spread anyway. On September 21 the Japanese army in Korea crossed the Yalu River into Manchuria under its commander, General Hayashi Senjūrō. He had no authorization from the emperor, and under Japanese law that made it an act no general could take. The cabinet met and decided to pay for the troops already deployed. The emperor approved after the fact. Hayashi was celebrated in the papers as the general who crossed the border.

The papers were the key. Almost overnight the great dailies in Osaka and Tokyo, which a year earlier had criticized the army's budgets, became the army's cheerleaders. They printed extras, sent correspondents, and sold newsreels and gramophone records about the heroes of the railway zone. Their circulation soared. The public sent money and comfort packages to the troops. Whatever the civilian government wanted, it no longer had the country behind it. In October, police uncovered a plot by young officers of a secret society called the Cherry Blossom Society to massacre the cabinet in a coup and install an army government. The plotters were given a few days of mild confinement.

On October 8, Japanese planes bombed Jinzhou, the city in the southwest of Manchuria where Zhang Xueliang had moved his provincial government. Ishiwara flew with the raid. It was one of the first times since the Great War that aircraft had bombed a city. In November the army moved north across the Nen River toward Qiqihar, the capital of the northernmost province. There, for once, the Chinese fought. General Ma Zhanshan held the bridge for nearly two weeks in subzero cold and became a national hero. Newspapers across China and the world printed his picture. On November 19 he was driven out of Qiqihar. By the end of the year almost all of Manchuria, a territory larger than France and Germany combined, belonged to an army of a few tens of thousands that had never been ordered to take it.

China appealed to the League of Nations on September 21. It was the first real test of the system built at Versailles, and in theory the League had every tool it needed. The Covenant bound its members to respect each other's territory and to act together against an aggressor. Three years earlier, Japan had signed the pact named for the American secretary of state and the French foreign minister, renouncing war as an instrument of national policy. The French foreign minister was Aristide Briand, and he now presided over the Council. On September 30 the Council took note of Japan's promise to withdraw its troops to the railway zone as quickly as possible. On October 24 it voted thirteen to one that Japan must withdraw by November 16. The one vote against was Japan's, and since the Council could only act unanimously, the resolution bound no one. For the first time, an American representative sat with the Council. He was told by Washington not to say much. When the Council met again on November 16, in Paris, Briand presided while suffering a racking cough. The Japanese troops had not withdrawn. They had advanced.

The Council found a way out, and it was Japan that offered it. On November 21 the Japanese delegate proposed that the League send a commission of inquiry to see the situation on the ground. The other members welcomed it with relief. On December 10 the Council voted unanimously to send one. It would be chaired by the British Earl of Lytton. It would take three months to assemble, three months to reach Manchuria, and most of another year to report. In Tianjin, meanwhile, a Japanese intelligence officer named Doihara Kenji had paid street gangs to stage riots in the Chinese city around the Japanese concession. On the night of November 10, during the riots, he took the last emperor of China, Puyi, out of his house in the back of a car and onto a Japanese launch. Puyi was twenty-five. He had been deposed at six. He was taken to Manchuria and waited.

In China the anger had nowhere to go. Students went on strike, marched, and boycotted Japanese goods. A crowd of them in Nanjing broke into the foreign ministry and beat the foreign minister. They demanded to know why the government that had sent armies against the communists in Jiangxi would not send them against Japan. Chiang Kai-shek's answer was a slogan he would repeat for years: internal pacification first, then external resistance. China was not ready, and he would not sacrifice his army in a war he could not win. In December, under pressure from every faction in his own party, he resigned. It wasn't the last time he left office. He was back within weeks.

In Tokyo, Wakatsuki's cabinet fell on December 11, and Shidehara's diplomacy fell with it. The new prime minister was Inukai Tsuyoshi, seventy-six years old, a party politician for half a century and an old friend of Sun Yat-sen and the Chinese revolution. He was sworn in on December 13. The same day, his finance minister took Japan off the gold standard. Inukai hoped to settle the matter by talking: privately, with Nanjing, around the army. He sent a personal envoy to China. The army found out.

Five months later, on a Sunday afternoon in May, a group of young naval officers came for him at his residence. Their plan had named a second target. Charlie Chaplin was visiting Japan, and the plotters reasoned that killing the most famous man in the world would bring war with America closer. Chaplin was not there, having gone to a sumo match that afternoon with the prime minister's son. The officers found Inukai at home. He didn't run. He led them to a reception room and asked them to sit down. If they would only talk, he told them, they would understand. "Talking is useless," one of them answered, and they shot him.

Coda: Monuments to Progress

On October 12, on the summit of the Corcovado, seven hundred meters above Rio de Janeiro, Brazil dedicated a Christ thirty meters tall. It took nine years to build. Its frame was reinforced concrete, the most modern material in the world, covered in a mosaic of small soapstone tiles, a stone Brazilian sculptors had worked for centuries. The engineer was a Brazilian, and the sculptor was a Frenchman in Paris who modeled the head and hands. The arms are spread straight out to either side, a hundred feet from fingertip to fingertip, so the figure is a cross and an embrace at once. Cardinal Sebastião Leme consecrated it. The year before, during the revolution that brought Getúlio Vargas to power, the same cardinal had walked the deposed president out of the palace to make sure he left it alive. The floodlights were supposed to be switched on from Rome, by Guglielmo Marconi, with a radio signal sent across the ocean. Bad weather broke the signal. Men on the mountain threw the switch by hand.

Beneath its arms the continent was coming apart. In January a band of young nationalists seized the presidential palace in Panama, the country's first successful coup. In July the dictator of Chile, Carlos Ibáñez, resigned amid strikes and riots as copper and nitrate prices collapsed and the country's foreign loans dried up. In September the sailors of the Chilean fleet mutinied over pay cuts and took their ships, and the government sent aircraft to bomb them. In August the army forced the president of Ecuador from office. In October troops in Asunción fired on students demonstrating outside the presidential palace of Paraguay. In December the army of El Salvador overthrew a president who had been in office for nine months and handed power to his vice president, General Maximiliano Hernández Martínez. Within weeks his troops would be massacring peasants in the western coffee country. In Rio the statue stood lit on its mountain, white above the bay.

Across the Atlantic, at Portlligat, a cove on the rocky coast of Catalonia near the French border, Salvador Dalí was twenty-seven. He lived in a fisherman's hut with Gala, who was not yet his wife. Two years earlier he had made a film with Luis Buñuel that opened with a razor slicing an eye. He had a theory, which he called the paranoiac-critical method: a disciplined way of letting the mind see one thing as another. The difference between a madman and him, he liked to say, was that he was not mad.

By his own account, the painting began with a headache and a cheese. One evening Gala went to the cinema and Dalí stayed home. After dinner he sat looking at the end of a Camembert, very ripe and running in the heat. Then he went to the studio, where a small canvas stood almost finished: the cliffs of the cape at dusk, a flat stretch of shore, a dead olive tree with its branches cut off. He painted soft watches over it. When Gala came home two hours later, it was done. She told him no one who had seen it would forget it.

The canvas is about the size of a sheet of paper, and its details are painted with the patience of a Flemish master. One watch is closed, face down, crawling with ants. The others have gone soft. One droops over the edge of a stone block. One hangs from the dead branch like laundry. One lies across a pale, boneless creature asleep on the sand, which has eyelashes and something like a nose, and which looks, if you look long enough, like Dalí's own profile. Every watch still shows the time, and no two of them agree.

He showed it in Paris in June. Julien Levy, a twenty-five-year-old American about to open a gallery in New York, bought it for two hundred and fifty dollars. In November it hung in Hartford, Connecticut, in the first exhibition of Surrealism in America. People came to look at the limp watches.

Down the coast stood the newly-constructed Empire State Building. In May, Herbert Hoover had pressed a button at the White House, and in New York the lights came on in the tallest building in the world. It had gone up in just over a year, ahead of schedule and under budget, on money raised in the confidence of 1929. At the top was a mooring mast for airships, and no airship would ever dock there. When it opened, fewer than a quarter of its offices had tenants, and the upper floors stood almost entirely empty. The tourists who paid a dollar to ride up to the observation deck brought in almost as much as the rents. At night the tower stood lit above Fifth Avenue. By some accounts, the staff were told to turn on the lights in the vacant floors. From the street, it looked full.
