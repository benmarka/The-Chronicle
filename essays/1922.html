Intro: The Open Tomb

On the morning of November 4, a water boy tripped over a stone. It sat under huts that Howard Carter's crew had cleared years before and abandoned. Beneath it, cut into the bedrock, was a step. By the end of the day there were twelve more, leading down to a sealed doorway stamped with a royal cartouche. Carter had it filled back in and cabled his patron, Lord Carnarvon, in England: come at once.

Three weeks passed before Carnarvon arrived with his daughter, Lady Evelyn Herbert. On November 26, with the stairway cleared and the outer doorway breached, Carter cut a small hole in the corner of a second sealed door and held a candle to it. Hot air escaped first, then the shapes of gold began to resolve out of the dark: animals, statues, a glint everywhere he looked. Carnarvon, waiting behind him, could stand it no longer. "Can you see anything?" Carter's own diary records his answer as "Yes, it is wonderful." The version that stuck, the one he gave the world in his book two years later, was cleaner: "Yes, wonderful things."

What Carter had opened that day was not Tutankhamun's tomb, not yet. It was an antechamber, packed with over six hundred objects — chariots, beds shaped like animals, thrones — arranged with no apparent order, as if hurried into place. Beyond it stood another sealed doorway, guarded by two statues facing each other. Carter suspected, correctly, that it led to the burial chamber itself. He would not open it until the following February. For the whole of 1922, the king himself stayed sealed away, untouched for the first time in three thousand years and still, technically, undiscovered.

None of that slowed the world down. News of the find crossed the wire within days, riding telegraph and radio networks that had matured just enough to carry it everywhere at once. Harry Burton, the Metropolitan Museum's photographer, staged and lit each object before it was moved, and his images went out to newspapers on every continent. A consumer public already primed by department stores and mass advertising took to the story instantly — not as archaeology, but as spectacle. Within weeks, jewelers were cutting scarab brooches and couturiers were draping models in lotus motifs. The full flowering of "Tutmania," the papyrus-print fabrics and pyramid-shaped handbags and cinemas built to look like Egyptian temples, was still a year or two off. But its first customers were already lining up.

The timing was not incidental. Nine months earlier, in February, Britain had issued a unilateral declaration ending its formal protectorate over Egypt — a declaration that changed remarkably little on the ground, since British officials still held most of the real levers of power, but that gave Egyptian nationalists a fresh vocabulary of sovereignty to test. Tutankhamun, obscure and unimportant in life, became instantly useful in death. Poets and playwrights folded him into a movement already reaching back to pharaonic Egypt for a usable past, a king rediscovered just as the nation that housed his tomb was rediscovering itself. Egyptian newspapers, however, discovered they had no seat at the table. Carnarvon's arrangement to sell exclusive coverage to a single London paper — a deal that would be finalized that winter — was already understood as an insult in the making, one more instance of a British lord treating Egyptian soil as his to license. Pierre Lacau, running the Antiquities Service, had spent 1922 tightening the rules that let foreign excavators walk away with half of what they found. Carnarvon still assumed the old terms would hold. They would not, and the argument over who owned the "wonderful things" — the objects, the story, the imagery itself — was only beginning.

Ancient Egyptian tombs were never meant to be opened by strangers. Tutankhamun's was ringed with the era's standard defenses: spells inscribed on guardian statues, magical bricks set into the walls at the cardinal points, protective figures meant to animate against intruders in the afterlife. None of this was folklore invented for tourists; it was liturgy, taken as seriously as any other part of the burial. In 1922, though, it registered mostly as texture — a detail for journalists filling column space between descriptions of gold. The dying pharaoh's magic hadn't yet found a modern subject to attach itself to. That would take a mosquito bite the following spring, and a journalist with a flair for prophecy, and a British public newly hungry for anything that could make the story stranger than it already was. For now, the door stood a few inches open. Everyone had seen just enough to imagine the rest.

Part I: Wars on the Perimeter

By the time the Greek army reached the coast at Smyrna, it had already lost the war. The collapse took less than three weeks. On August 26, the Turkish National Movement under Mustafa Kemal launched what its own histories still call the Great Offensive: over a hundred thousand men against a Greek force twice that size but disorganized, overextended, and out of will. The line broke at Dumlupınar. Turkish cavalry reached Smyrna on September 9. What had been, three years earlier, a triumphant Greek landing meant to revive a "Greater Greece" stretching back to Byzantium ended with retreating Greek units burning towns and villages behind them as they fled to the coast.

Smyrna itself, a city of Greeks, Armenians, Jews, and Turks that had somehow avoided the worst of the previous decade's violence, burned four days later. The fire started in the Armenian quarter on September 13 and spread through the Greek district before it was done; the Turkish and Jewish quarters were largely spared. Whether the fire was set deliberately, and by whom, is still disputed and probably always will be — Greek, Armenian, and Turkish accounts blame each other, and no definitive Allied investigation ever resolved it. What isn't disputed is what happened at the waterfront. Tens of thousands of Greek and Armenian civilians were driven onto the quay, trapped between the flames behind them and the sea in front, while warships from Britain, France, Italy, and the United States sat in the harbor and largely declined to intervene. Turkish forces cordoned the area, and in the chaos, killings, and the separation of men from their families were reported by multiple foreign observers. Estimates of the dead run from the low tens of thousands into six figures; a precise count was never possible and never will be.

The Armistice of Mudanya, signed October 11, formalized what the fighting had already decided: Greece would return to its prewar borders, ceding Eastern Thrace and abandoning its claims in Anatolia entirely. Kemal used the victory to abolish the Ottoman sultanate within weeks, laying the foundation for the republic he would proclaim the following year. The negotiations at Lausanne, opening in November, would produce something with no real precedent — a compulsory exchange of populations, something over a million Greek Orthodox Christians for several hundred thousand Muslims, sorted not by language or by where they'd lived for generations but by religion, and moved, whether they wished it or not, to countries many of them had never seen. It was framed at the time as a humanitarian solution, a way to prevent further massacre by simply separating the populations that kept massacring each other. It was also the end of a Greek presence in Anatolia that had existed, in some form, since before Alexander.

Ireland's war started the same summer that Greece's was ending. Where the Anatolian catastrophe closed a chapter that had run for three years, Dublin's opened one that would run for eleven months more. The Anglo-Irish Treaty, signed the previous December, had given the twenty-six counties dominion status inside the British Empire — a settlement that fell well short of the republic that had been proclaimed in 1916 and fought for since, and that split the independence movement down the middle. Many of the men who disagreed on the Treaty had fought side by side against the British only a year before.

The anti-Treaty IRA had occupied the Four Courts, Dublin's judicial complex on the Liffey, since April, daring the new Provisional Government to move against former comrades. Michael Collins, now head of that government and the most effective guerrilla strategist the independence movement had produced, tried for months to avoid the confrontation. British pressure — sharpened by the assassination of a British field marshal in London, which the Four Courts garrison had nothing to do with but was blamed for anyway — made avoidance impossible. On the morning of June 28, Collins ordered Free State artillery, borrowed from the British Army that had occupied Ireland for centuries, to open fire on the building. The garrison surrendered after three days. An explosion during the bombardment destroyed the Public Record Office inside, taking with it centuries of Irish historical documents — an accident, most historians now think, though Free State supporters at the time called it deliberate sabotage.

What followed was not the swift, decisive campaign Collins had hoped for. The anti-Treaty IRA, pushed out of the cities by August, reverted to the guerrilla tactics that had worn down the British three years earlier, and now turned them against the state their old comrades were building. Arthur Griffith, president of the Dáil and the Treaty's chief civilian architect, died of a brain hemorrhage on August 12, worn down by the strain of the office. Ten days later, Collins was dead too, shot through the head in an ambush at a crossroads in his native County Cork, on a return trip from a meeting he seems to have arranged in hopes of finding a way to end the war he'd started. The place was called Béal na Bláth. In Irish, béal means mouth, or opening, or approach — the entrance to something. Collins had gone there, by several accounts, hoping to find a way out of the war. He found the war's most enduring image instead.

Two conflicts, then, closing and opening within months of each other, both fed by the same unresolved wreckage of the Great War's peace settlements — one closing off a set of communities that had coexisted, however precariously, for millennia, the other opening a set of wounds among people who had, until a year earlier, called each other comrades.

Part II: Form and Function

Ulysses was already famous before anyone could legally read it. Two years earlier, a New York court had fined the editors of The Little Review fifty dollars each for serializing the "Nausicaa" episode, in which Leopold Bloom watches a young woman on a beach and quietly masturbates. The ruling made the book un-publishable anywhere in the English-speaking world. So when James Joyce's novel finally appeared complete, on February 2, 1922, his fortieth birthday, it came from a bookshop, not a publishing house — Sylvia Beach's Shakespeare and Company on the rue de l'Odéon, printed in Dijon on paper stock ranging from handmade to vellum, in an edition of exactly one thousand numbered copies. Beach had never published anything before in her life. No established house would touch it.

What made Ulysses dangerous wasn't really the sex, though there was plenty for censors to seize on — a scene of masturbation, a wife's extended and unapologetic sexual reverie, blasphemy against the Catholic Church laced through the opening pages. It was the method. Joyce had built the entire novel — the ordinary, unremarkable day of an ordinary, unremarkable Dubliner, structured loosely against the Odyssey — out of consciousness itself: unpunctuated, unfiltered, jumping between memory and hunger and grief and desire with no narrator standing between the reader and the thought. Nothing in English prose had gone that far inside a mind before. Critics who admired it struggled to describe it in anything but superlatives; critics who despised it reached for words like "obscene" and "blasphemous" and, in one memorable British review, "unimaginable." Both reactions were really about the same thing: for the first time, a novel had made the inside of a head into its entire subject, with no guardrail of plot or decorum protecting the reader from what was in there.

T. S. Eliot had a breakdown that autumn. By October 1921, exhausted from his job at Lloyds Bank and by a marriage that was collapsing around him, he was granted three months' leave on doctor's orders and went first to the English seaside at Margate, then to Lausanne, Switzerland, for treatment by a nerve specialist named Roger Vittoz. He worked on a long poem in both places — fragments at Margate, a full nineteen-page draft in Switzerland — a poem built, like Joyce's novel, out of fracture: shifting voices, unmarked transitions, quotations in Latin, German, French, and Sanskrit dropped in without translation, a structure borrowed loosely from Arthurian grail legend and the myth of the wounded Fisher King. Eliot passed through Paris on his way home in January 1922 and handed the draft to Ezra Pound.

What Pound did to that draft is now one of the most studied acts of editing in the language. He cut it by nearly half — entire narrative sequences, a rowdy passage set in Boston, long passages of scene-setting Eliot had thought necessary. Eliot, by his own later account, accepted almost every cut without argument. The poem that emerged, four hundred thirty-four lines instead of nearly a thousand, appeared in October in the first issue of The Criterion, the London magazine Eliot had just founded, and the following month in the American Dial. Eliot dedicated it to Pound with a line borrowed from Dante: il miglior fabbro, the better craftsman. It was not false modesty. Eliot's original title had been He Do the Police in Different Voices, a phrase lifted from Dickens; Pound was the one who saw the mythic architecture underneath the fragments and cut everything that didn't serve it.

Nosferatu opened in Berlin a week before Eliot's breakdown poem reached print — a gala premiere at the Marble Hall of the Berlin Zoological Garden on March 4, staged by a new production company called Prana Film. Its producer, Albin Grau, had spent the war years fascinated by occultism; he later said the idea came from a Serbian farmer who told him his own father had been a vampire. Grau shaped the film's look and financed it partly through his connections to a Berlin occult society. The director, F. W. Murnau, gave the material a visual grammar — jagged shadows, natural landscapes turned menacing, a vampire killed for the first time on screen by sunlight rather than merely weakened by it — that would define horror cinema for a century after.

It was also, quite openly, a theft. The film's own opening titles credited Bram Stoker's Dracula as its source, and the plot changes — Dracula becomes Count Orlok, London becomes the German town of Wisborg — didn't disguise anything; Stoker had been dead a decade, and Prana Film simply hadn't bothered to ask his widow for the rights. Florence Stoker, who depended on Dracula's royalties for her income, would sue, and eventually win, and the film would be ordered destroyed in Germany a few years later — a ruling only partly successful, since prints had already escaped the country. None of that had happened yet in 1922. What audiences saw that March was simply the first vampire on film, rat-toothed and rigid, arriving by ship with a hold full of plague. Some critics at the time and since have read Orlok's arrival — a foreign contagion invading a placid German town, aided by a Jewish estate agent cast as his local accomplice — as something more pointed than a horror story, an anxiety about outsiders dressed up as a monster movie. Murnau himself was not a committed antisemite by most accounts, and the film's politics, if they're there at all, were more atmosphere than argument. But the atmosphere was real, and it was 1922, and Germany's appetite for stories about contamination from outside its borders would not stay confined to the movies.

Three works, then, in three countries, none of them polite about what they were doing to their own forms. A novel that dissolved plot into thought. A poem that dissolved narrative into fragment and let the fragments argue with each other. A film that dissolved the vampire's genteel Victorian menace into something closer to vermin, and discovered, in the process, that sunlight could kill it. All three found their first audiences within the same twelve months, each one certain that the old containers — the well-made novel, the well-made poem, the well-mannered ghost story — could no longer hold what needed saying.

Part III: Breakthrough

Before 1922, a diagnosis of type 1 diabetes was a death sentence, and rarely a slow one. The standard treatment, developed a few years earlier, was near-starvation: doctors restricted patients to as few calories as the body could survive on, buying weeks or months by starving the disease along with everything else. Photographs of pediatric diabetes wards from the period show children reduced to skeletons, kept alive by denying them the food that would kill them faster. There was no other option. The pancreas made something the body needed and no one could replace it.

Frederick Banting, a young, largely unproven Canadian surgeon with a small practice and a heavy debt load, had an idea in October 1920 — sketched at two in the morning, unable to sleep, in a notebook that survives today at what's now called Banting House in London, Ontario. He brought it to John Macleod, a physiology professor at the University of Toronto who controlled the lab space Banting needed and who was, by most accounts, skeptical of both the idea and the young surgeon proposing it. Macleod assigned him a research assistant, a 22-year-old named Charles Best, and gave them lab space for the summer of 1921. Working with dogs — tying off pancreatic ducts, inducing diabetes artificially, then treating it with extract drawn from other dogs' pancreases — Banting and Best produced something that looked, in Best's own description, like thick brown muck. It worked anyway. A diabetic dog treated with the extract survived seventy days, dying only when the supply ran out.

Moving from dogs to a dying child took another six months and a fourth scientist, a biochemist named James Collip, who joined the team to solve the problem the dogs hadn't posed: making the extract pure enough not to kill a human being through infection or allergic reaction. Leonard Thompson, thirteen years old, weighing sixty-five pounds and drifting in and out of a diabetic coma at Toronto General Hospital, was the one they tried it on. His father, out of options, consented. The first injection, on January 11, using an unrefined extract prepared by Banting and Best themselves, lowered Thompson's blood sugar by about a quarter and gave him an abscess at the injection site for his trouble — not nothing, but nowhere near a cure. Twelve days later, on January 23, Collip's purified version went in instead. The results were immediate and almost absurd by the standard of the previous injection: his blood sugar normalized, the sugar and ketones vanished from his urine, and a boy who had been actively dying began, within a day, to recover. He would live another thirteen years, eventually dying of pneumonia rather than the disease that should have killed him as a teenager.

What followed moved almost as fast as the discovery itself. Within the year, the University of Toronto had licensed the patent to pharmaceutical manufacturers royalty-free, on the reasoning — startling by the standards of any era, let alone this one — that a treatment this necessary shouldn't be owned by anyone. By October 1923, commercial insulin was shipping, and the same month the Nobel committee gave its Prize in Physiology or Medicine to Banting and Macleod, the fastest a Nobel has ever followed a discovery, before or since.

The generosity of the patent decision did not extend to the credit. Banting, furious that Macleod's name was on the prize and Best's wasn't, split his own half of the award money with Best in protest — a gesture read at the time as loyalty, and probably was. But it also set the terms for a decades-long argument over who had actually done what. Best spent much of the rest of his career, after Banting's death in a 1943 plane crash and Macleod's in 1935, quietly minimizing Collip's role in later speeches and accounts, insisting the real breakthrough had happened with his and Banting's own imperfect extract on January 11 rather than Collip's successful one twelve days later. It was not, on the evidence, an accurate emphasis. Collip's purification is what turned a promising animal experiment into a treatment a human body could survive.

None of that infighting touched Leonard Thompson, who got to be a teenager instead of a memory, or the diabetic children photographed skeletal in 1921 who were, within a year or two, photographed again — heavier, recognizable, alive. Insulin didn't cure anything. It gave a body back the one thing it had been quietly denied since birth, and let it go on living an ordinary life on the strength of a needle and a vial. Few discoveries that year, in a year crowded with them, changed as many individual lives as directly, or as immediately, as this one did.

Part IV: New Forces

The March on Rome was not, in any strict sense, a conquest. By the time Blackshirt columns actually reached the capital on October 28, Mussolini had already won. He'd spent the previous three years building toward this moment without ever quite touching it directly — burning Socialist union halls, beating opposition organizers, forcing castor oil down the throats of local officials who wouldn't cooperate, all through squadristi violence he could disown when convenient. By October 1922 the Fascist squads effectively controlled much of northern and central Italy already. The march itself, some twenty-five to thirty thousand poorly armed men in torrential rain, was closer to theater than to war. Rome's garrison alone had thirty thousand soldiers, machine guns, and armored cars, and its commanding general told the king plainly that the army could rout the marchers without difficulty.

What decided the outcome wasn't the army. It was one man's fear. Prime Minister Luigi Facta had a martial law decree drafted and ready, unanimously approved by his cabinet, on the morning of October 28. It needed only King Victor Emmanuel III's signature to take effect. The king, weighing rumors that his own cousin the Duke of Aosta was conspiring with the Fascists to seize the throne, and unconvinced the army would actually fire on fellow Italians, refused to sign. Facta resigned within hours. Two days later the king asked Mussolini, who had stayed safely in Milan through the entire march and arrived by train only after the outcome was settled, to form a government. Mussolini became prime minister at thirty-nine, with only thirty-two Fascist deputies in a parliament of hundreds, entirely because a frightened monarch had declined to use force he almost certainly had. The following year he would begin dismantling the parliamentary system that had just legally installed him. In October 1922, though, none of that was written yet. What existed was a coalition cabinet, mostly non-Fascist ministers, and a king reviewing Blackshirts parading past his palace with what he later insisted, unconvincingly, was simple relief at avoiding civil war.

Two months later and a continent away, a different founding took a different shape entirely. On December 30, in Moscow's Grand Kremlin Palace, delegates from the Russian, Ukrainian, Byelorussian, and Transcaucasian Soviet republics ratified a treaty and declaration establishing the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics. Stalin, as People's Commissar of Nationalities, delivered the report and moved for its adoption; Pravda greeted the new year with the headline "Creation of the Soviet Union is a New Year's Gift to the World Proletariat." On paper, the union was voluntary and federal — each republic formally equal, each retaining, in Article 26, a theoretical right to secede. The document's language leaned on family metaphors, brotherhood among peoples freely joined rather than a Russian empire simply repainted red.

The paper and the practice were already diverging before the ink dried. Stalin's original proposal, drafted earlier that year, had been blunter: fold Ukraine, Belarus, and the Transcaucasian republics into the Russian Republic as mere autonomous provinces, no pretense of equal partnership required. Lenin, recovering from the first of a series of strokes that had already begun to take his speech and his mobility, rejected the plan when he understood it fully, accusing Stalin's approach of "Great-Russian chauvinism" — old imperial habits, he warned, dressed up in new uniforms. The federal structure that emerged in December was Lenin's version, not Stalin's, forced through over the objections of the man managing the party's daily machinery. Stalin's handling of Georgian Communists who resisted the merger had already turned rough enough that Lenin, in notes dictated from his sickbed that same December, began setting down what would become known as his political testament: a warning to the party that Stalin had accumulated too much power, and a recommendation — quiet, unpublished in his lifetime — that he be removed from his post as General Secretary.

Nothing came of the warning while Lenin lived to give it teeth. He suffered another stroke within days of the union's founding, then a third the following spring that ended his ability to work at all; Stalin, informed of the testament's existence, moved to keep Lenin isolated on medical grounds and kept building his own base inside the party apparatus regardless. When Lenin died just over a year later, the testament would be read aloud to the Central Committee and quietly buried by the same men — Zinoviev and Kamenev chief among them — who would themselves be devoured by the man they chose to protect. None of that had happened by December 30, 1922. What existed that day was a document promising equal partnership among peoples, signed at the exact moment its most dangerous internal contradiction — a leadership struggle between the union's chief architect and the man already accumulating the machinery to run it — was becoming visible only to the one person too weakened to stop it.

Rome and Moscow, then, arrived at roughly the same place by nearly opposite roads. Italy's new order was installed by a monarch's timidity, dressed afterward in the language of order and national revival, its authoritarian character still ambiguous enough in 1922 that plenty of Italians, including men who would later regret it, could tell themselves nothing fundamental had changed. Russia's new order was founded on an explicit promise of federated equality, drafted by a dying revolutionary who could already see, more clearly than anyone else in the room, exactly which of his lieutenants meant to break that promise, and who no longer had the strength to stop it.

Coda: The Jail Door Closes

Gandhi had been trying to slow things down for weeks. By late January 1922, the Non-Cooperation Movement he'd built over two years — boycotting British courts, schools, and cloth, refusing to cooperate with an empire he argued survived only on Indian consent — had grown large enough to frighten the colonial government seriously. Gandhi himself was preparing something sharper: a coordinated refusal to pay taxes, launching from Bardoli in Gujarat. Then, on February 4, in a market town called Chauri Chaura in the United Provinces, a crowd of protesters, fired on by police, turned on the police station and burned it down with twenty-two officers still inside.

Gandhi's response stunned nearly everyone around him. Within eight days, without consulting most of his own leadership, he suspended the entire national movement. He undertook a five-day fast in penance, reasoning that the masses were not yet disciplined enough in nonviolence to be trusted with a campaign of this scale, and that a movement resting on ahimsa couldn't survive a mob burning men alive, whatever the provocation. The backlash from his own side was immediate and furious. Motilal Nehru and Chittaranjan Das called it a catastrophic surrender at the movement's peak; younger nationalists would go on calling it that for decades. Within a year, disagreement over the decision split the Congress and produced a breakaway party willing to work inside the councils Gandhi wanted boycotted entirely.

The government did not reward the restraint. Gandhi had spent the preceding months writing increasingly blunt essays in his weekly paper, Young India, arguing that disaffection toward an unjust government was no crime at all but something closer to an obligation — writing, by his own apparent design, in language that all but invited prosecution. He was arrested on March 10 and charged with sedition. When the case came to trial eight days later, he and his co-defendant told the magistrate they needed no time to prepare a defense, since they intended to plead guilty. The hearing was moved from the regular law courts to a private circuit house, out of official worry that a public trial might itself become a flashpoint; the presiding judge reportedly played a relaxed round of golf before breakfast that same morning, unbothered by what was, in retrospect, one of the more consequential mornings of the British Raj. Gandhi was sentenced to six years.

He would serve less than two of them, released early in 1924 on grounds of failing health. But in March 1922, none of that mitigation was visible yet. What existed was a leader who had just talked an entire nation out of its own momentum, imprisoned by the government he'd spent two years trying to render unnecessary, at the exact moment his own movement was tearing itself apart over whether he'd been right to stop it.

Nearly everything else this year had been a door cracking open somewhere — a tomb, a language, a treatment, a regime, a union, an unfinished war giving way to a new one. Gandhi's year ended in the opposite motion: a movement voluntarily shut, at its own momentum's peak, by the one man capable of shutting it, followed by the state's own answer, a cell door, in case his wasn't final enough. Both closures were his in some sense — the first chosen, the second not — and both left the same unresolved question sitting in Indian politics for years afterward, one that the Salt March would answer very differently eight years later: whether restraint, in the end, is a form of strength or its opposite.
